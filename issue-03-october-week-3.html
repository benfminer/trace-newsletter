NTACT:C publishes an Indicator 13 Checklist — the compliance rubric, walked one element at a time. It is free, and it is essentially what a reviewer holds while reading your plan. There is no advantage to anyone in you not having seen it.

So this week is a self-check, and then the thing the self-check cannot do for you. Passing the rubric means your plan is complete. Whether it is any good is a separate question, and it has a much shorter test.

Answer for a single student’s plan. Everything you answer “no” or “not sure” becomes an item on the right, with what to do about it.

Start answering

Eight questions. It takes about as long as reading the plan once.

    These questions are drawn from the required elements, not copied from the official checklist — use the real one at transitionta.org when it counts. Nothing here is stored or sent anywhere.

    02 The Quick Tip, at length

    “What does Monday look like?”

    The rubric asks whether the plan is complete. This asks whether it is real. It takes about eight seconds per goal.

    Read a postsecondary goal and try to describe, concretely, how someone would spend Monday morning working on it. Not the philosophy of it — the actual hours. If you cannot get past “well, we’d work on employment skills,” the goal is not carrying enough information.

    “Employment in the community” Monday: unclear. Someone will do something employment-shaped and log it. This is how a year passes.
    “Grounds or custodial work, with DOR” Monday: call the DOR counselor, then walk the campus grounds crew supervisor through what a two-day-a-week placement would involve.

    Notice that the second version does not require the goal to be more ambitious — only more specific. Specificity is what converts a goal into a calendar.

    When the honest answer is “I don’t know yet”

    Sometimes you genuinely do not know where a student is headed, and no amount of rewriting will manufacture it. That is a real situation and it has a legitimate form: the goal describes the finding-out.

    A goal that admits what it doesn’t know

    “Within one year of exiting, Marcus will be employed in a field identified through community-based work experiences in at least three settings this year.”

    Monday is obvious: set up the first of the three. The goal is measurable, honest, and it drives services — which is more than a confident guess would do.

    What that version refuses to do is pretend. A guess written as a certainty gets inherited: the next three teams read “warehouse work” as settled fact and stop asking, and by the time anyone notices it was invented in a forty-minute meeting, the student is 21.

    Both tests, in order. Rubric first — it catches missing pieces. Monday test second — it catches present-but-useless. Neither one substitutes for the other.

    Try this week

    Grade one plan. Fix one line.

    Run the eight questions on a single IEP — ideally one you did not write. Then take the highest item on the to-do list and do it before the week ends.

    Three months of this and IEP season stops being an emergency.
