A job isn’t
automatically a job.

The thresholds are specific, and they are lower than most staff assume in one direction and higher in another.

This month’s issue introduced Indicator 14 — the three tiers the state sorts your leavers into a year after they exit. This week, the part that trips almost everyone up: what has to be true for a student to actually count.

None of it is complicated. All of it is precise. And the precision is where good programs lose students they had already helped.

01 This Week’s Focus

The three thresholds,
in plain terms

Start with 14B. It has the most conditions and it is the one people misjudge.

14B
Competitively employed — all three must be true

At least 90 cumulative days of work — they do not have to be consecutive.
Averaging 20 or more hours a week across those days.
Paid at or above minimum wage.

Subminimum wage and sheltered work do not count. Supported employment does count, as long as the setting is integrated and the pay clears minimum wage.

Cumulative, not consecutive. A student who worked two separate summers can clear 90 days without ever holding a job for three straight months.
14A
Higher education

A two- or four-year program at a community college, technical college, or university — with at least one full term completed. Enrolling and withdrawing in week three does not count.

Two things staff routinely undercount: remedial and non-credit courses still count — enrollment is what matters, not credit type — and so do inclusive college programs for students with intellectual disability.

14C
Everything else that counts

Other training: adult ed, GED or ABE programs, registered apprenticeships, short-term vocational or technical programs, Job Corps, AmeriCorps, day support and prevocational programs.

Other employment: any paid work or self-employment for 90+ days — with no minimum hours. A family business counts. A market-rate side business counts.

The case this decides

Your student bags groceries. Twelve hours a week, minimum wage, five months straight, and they love it.

That is not 14B. Twelve hours misses the twenty-hour bar, so they land in 14C as “other employment.” Eight more hours a week changes the category — and changes what that job can pay for.

Two more that catch people. Volunteering counts for nothing in any tier, no matter how many hours. And a student earning subminimum wage in a sheltered setting counts for nothing under 14B — they may still land in 14C, but the competitive bar is unmet.

None of this is a reason to chase numbers.

It is a reason to know, in August, which students are one scheduling conversation away from a real outcome

    02 Reading Your Own Numbers

    It’s a census, not a sample

    Every student who left with an IEP in effect — graduates, certificate completers, dropouts, and age-outs alike — gets surveyed a year after they separate. Nobody is excluded for exiting the “wrong” way.

    California reports this to CDE annually, using the same federal definitions above. When you ask your SELPA for it, ask for three years, not one. A single year of a small program is noise; three years is a trend you can actually act on.

    Worth knowing

    The survey is also a scoreboard on your own offerings

    It records which programs students participated in — work-based learning, Pre-ETS, supported employment. That is the closest thing you have to outcome data on the things your program actually spends its hours on.

    Try this week

    Pick your three closest to working.

    For each one, answer honestly: if they exited today and the state called in a year, would they clear twenty hours and ninety days?

    Whoever falls short is your first scheduling conversation of the year — with them, their family, and their employer.
