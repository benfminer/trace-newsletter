Usually it isn’t
the job that costs
them the job.

Researchers have been sorting out why placements end since the 1950s. The pattern holds: it is far more often the habits than the work.

↓ Start reading

Ask an employer why someone was let go and you will rarely hear about the work. You hear that they could not be relied on, or could not take a correction, or could not be left alongside the rest of the staff.

That is not a soft observation. It is the most replicated finding in the employment-retention literature for workers with intellectual disabilities, and it has a specific shape worth knowing, because the shape tells you what to teach in January.

3 of the four reasons A 1988 review sorted job losses tied to a worker’s own performance into four categories. Only one of them is the work itself. The other three are how a person moves through a workplace.
On the size of the claim. The direction of this finding is solid and repeatedly replicated. A precise percentage is not — so this issue says “far more often” and never puts a number on it.
01 This Month’s Focus

Name the habits,
then build them in

Virginia’s 21st Century Workplace Readiness Skills framework names 22 skills across three categories — and treats every one of them as teachable and assessable, not as something a student simply has.

  1. 1
    Personal Qualities & Abilities

    Creativity & Innovation · Critical Thinking & Problem-Solving · Initiative & Self-Direction · Integrity · Work Ethic

  2. 2
    Interpersonal Skills

    Conflict Resolution · Listening & Speaking · Respect for Diversity · Customer Service Orientation · Teamwork

  3. 3
    Professional Competencies — 12 skills

    The largest category, and the one most placements lean on: Professionalism, Continuous Learning & Adaptability, Efficiency & Productivity, Big Picture Thinking, Career & Life Management, Workplace Safety, Reading & Writing, Mathematics, and four information- and technology-related skills.

Stop treating soft skills as something students have or don’t. Pick one, name it, teach it like any other skill.

Model it, practice it, give feedback, repeat

Your TRACE classroom and CBI outings are the low-stakes rehearsal space. A student who learns to take correction from you without shutting down is practicing the exact skill that protects a placement later. Treat every “that’s not how we do it” moment as instruction rather than management.

No California equivalent. The state has no named counterpart to Virginia’s framework, though the skills line up with CTE standards and DOR Pre-ETS competencies. TRACE can use it as-is.

Interactive · eight terminations, four categories

Which bucket ended it?

Eight people lost a job. For each one, decide which of the four categories the reason belongs in. The individual answers matter less than the shape of the tally at the end.

Loading the eight cases. With scripting off, the four categories are: job responsibility (attendance, punctuality, workplace rules); task-production skills (speed, accuracy, the work itself); task-related social skills (taking direction, asking for help, responding to correction); and personal-social skills (getting along with coworkers, conversation, reading the room).

      The four categories are Salzberg, Lignugaris/Kraft & McCuller’s (1988), from a review of 35 years of termination studies. All eight cases are composites. Two of them are genuinely arguable and the explanations say so rather than pretending otherwise.

      02 Practice Spotlight

      One Skill, Named Out Loud

      Pick the single habit most likely to cost your students a job. Make it this month’s target — one skill at a time beats a vague push for “professionalism” every time.

      1. 1
        Name it

        “This month we’re working on responding to feedback.” Say it plainly and post it where they can see it.

      2. 2
        Model it

        Show what “okay, I’ll fix it” looks and sounds like. Role-play the awkward version too — the one where you disagree and say so anyway.

      3. 3
        Catch it

        When a student does it well, name that on the spot. Reinforced habits stick; habits you only mention when they fail do not.

      Rate it before and after

      The framework ships with a five-point rubric — from cannot perform up to can teach peers. Put a number on your target skill at the start of the month and again at the end. “Seems better” is not something you can put in a present-levels statement. Week 1 goes through the scale →

      03 Resource of the Month
      • Free
      • 30 activities
      • Ages 14–21

      Skills to Pay the Bills

      The Department of Labor’s ODEP built this soft-skills curriculum with input from more than 100 young people. Thirty activities across six areas — communication, enthusiasm & attitude, teamwork, networking, problem solving & critical thinking, and professionalism. It is designed for all youth on Universal Design for Learning principles, with accommodation guidance built into every section.

      Pull one activity that matches your target skill and run it this week. dol.gov/odep →

      Worth knowing that ODEP and Virginia do not compete. ODEP gives you activities; Virginia gives you rating criteria. Networking is a standalone area in ODEP and is not among Virginia’s 22 — which is the cleanest argument for keeping both on the shelf.

      Check the age framing. ODEP’s stated band is 14 to 21 and your students run to 22. Nothing breaks at the top end, but read an activity before you run it.

      Quick tip for January

      Run the room like a job site.

      One week. Clock-in expectations, real deadlines, feedback in the moment. Then debrief on Friday.

      Students learn faster when the practice feels like the real thing — and you find out which habits survive contact with a deadline.
