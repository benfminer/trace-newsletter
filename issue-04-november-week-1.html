From across the room those two students look exactly the same. The response each one needs is the opposite of the other.

This month’s issue named the four components of self-determination and made the case that they are teachable. This week is about the diagnosis you have to make before you teach anything — because “this student has low self-determination” describes two completely different problems.

One student has never learned how to say what they need. The other says it fine, at home, on a Saturday, and has not been asked a single open question in a school building since September.

Both look passive in a meeting. Teaching skills to the second one is wasted effort, and making room for the first one is setting them up to fail in public.

01 This Week’s Focus

Capacity is one thing.
Opportunity is another.

This split is not a metaphor someone invented for a newsletter. It is built into one of the standard instruments, and it is the reason that instrument is worth using.

The AIR Self-Determination Scale — free, reproducible, developed by the American Institutes for Research with federal funding — deliberately measures two separate dimensions and reports them separately:

CAP
Capacity — what the student brings

Knowledge (do they know how), Ability (can they do it), and Perception (do they believe it is worth trying). All three, separately.

OPP
Opportunity — what the setting allows

Whether school, and whether home, actually gives the student occasions to exercise it. Rated by the same raters, on the same scale.

There are four forms — educator, student, parent, research — all on a five-point Never-to-Always frequency scale. The educator form is the primary validated one, at 30 items. Reliability across the field test of 450-plus students ran from .74 on test-retest up to the .90s on internal measures, and factor analysis confirmed that capacity and opportunity really do come apart as two components.

The output is a Profile sheet that plots one against the other. That plot is the entire value of the instrument, and it is what neither the Arc’s Self-Determination Scale nor the SDIS gives you: not a score, a diagnosis.

One finding worth carrying. In the field test, scores did not differ significantly by gender or by race and ethnicity — but students in special education and students who were economically disadvantaged scored significantly lower. Some of that is opportunity, and opportunity is ours.

Ask the question that separates them: has this student ever actually been given the chance to do this, on a day when it counted?

The answer is embarrassing more often than not

Clinically the same distinction shows up as capacity versus competence in the age-of-majority literature: capacity is task-specific and can be assessed informally, competence is a legal finding by a judge. The practical inheritance for us is that capacity is never all-or-none. A student can have plenty of it for choosing a shift and none of it for negotiating a goal, in the same week.

What that costs if you get it backwards

    If all four components land in the same box, rate them again. Real students are lopsided — that is the normal result, and a flat profile usually means the rater was thinking about the student in general rather than about four separate skills.

    02 The half we control

    Opportunity is cheaper than instruction

    Capacity takes a semester. Opportunity takes a decision, and it is available on Monday.

    A school day is built to remove it. Permission to move, a schedule someone else set, an adult available to answer any question directed at the student. None of that is anyone’s fault and all of it suppresses exactly the habits that have to survive contact with a job site.

    The opportunities that count share one property: a real outcome follows the choice, including a bad one. Choosing between two things you were going to be given anyway is not practice.

    1. 1
      Let the natural consequence land

      Stayed up late and is tired; skipped the shopping and there is nothing for lunch. The consequence is the teacher. Removing it removes the lesson.

    2. 2
      Redirect the question

      When another adult asks you something about the student while the student is standing there, do not answer. Look at the student. It costs three seconds and it is the highest-frequency opportunity in the building.

    3. 3
      Give away a real choice each week

      Which accommodation to try. Which of two work sites. What order to run the routine in. Small, genuine, and theirs to get wrong.

    Reasonable risk is part of the design

    The community strategies in the literature include taking reasonable risks and learning from mistakes, on purpose. A student who has never been allowed to be wrong has no evidence that they can recover from it — and recovering from it is most of adult life.

    Practice it in more than one place. Skills exercised in a single setting stay there. Home, school, and community all giving room is what makes the habit portable — which is worth one sentence to a family at the next meeting.

    Try this week

    Count the open questions.

    Pick one student and one hour. Count how many questions get asked of them that they could genuinely answer differently — not “are you ready?”, but a question with two real answers. Just count. Do not change anything yet.
