Two systems,
three tiers,
one confirmed practice.

The monthly put three evidence claims about families in a row. They do not sit on one ladder — two of them are correlations and one is an intervention finding, and knowing which is which changes what you do in March.

Following up on Issue 08 · This Month’s Focus 01 02 03

Parent training to teach knowledge of transition services is a confirmed Evidence-Based Practice. Parent involvement is Promising, which is the weakest tier available. Both sentences are true, and read side by side they look like a ranking of family things from strong to weak.

They are not a ranking. They are answers to two different questions, graded by two different systems, and the sentence you can defend in a meeting depends entirely on which one you are quoting. This is the part that gets lost every time somebody writes that family engagement is research-based.

01 This Week’s Focus

Two systems.
Not one ladder.

One literature asks what correlates with life after school. The other asks what an intervention causes. They use similar-sounding tier names, which is most of why they get blurred.

  • Predictors are correlational. In-school experiences and characteristics that correlate with post-school education, employment, and independent living. Mazzotti and colleagues (2021) is a systematic literature review — not a meta-analysis — and it names 23 predictors spread across three tiers. Under ESSA that whole body of work is Tier 3.
  • Practices are experimental. Rowe and colleagues (2021) classify interventions by what they were shown to cause. Parent training to teach knowledge of transition services clears their bar on one randomized controlled trial (Young et al., 2016), one quasi-experimental group study (Boone, 1992), and one single-case study (Rowe & Test, 2010).

A predictor tier is a correlation with a grade on it. It is not a statement about what an intervention causes.

The line the two systems turn on

So the family variables land in three different places. Parent expectations is a Research-Based predictor, for employment and nothing else. Parent involvement is Promising, for education and employment. And parent training is not a predictor at all — it is the practice claim. Career and technical education is the only predictor in the top tier, and it is not a family variable.

The phrase to retire. “One of the 23 research-based predictors” is wrong on its face — the 23 span three tiers, and only one of them sits at the top. Predictors also come with no guidance on dosage, timing, or delivery, so they cannot tell you how much of anything to do.

What the tiers are actually counting

NTACT:C’s three levels are a count of studies and a judgment about how they were designed. Nothing in them is about how much a thing matters to a family.

  1. 1
    Evidence-Based

    At least two methodologically sound a priori studies, positive correlations that hold, effect sizes you can calculate — and no sound a priori study pointing the other way.

  2. 2
    Research-Based

    The same two-study floor, but some sound negative findings are tolerated as long as more studies came out positive. This is where parent expectations sits.

  3. 3
    Promising

    One sound a priori study, or two sound exploratory ones with no planned hypothesis. Parent involvement sits here, and so do six other predictors.

Promising can rest on a single study. That is a description of how much research exists, not a verdict on families — which is exactly why the practice finding is the better thing to argue from.

On effect sizes. The 2021 review reports a mean of r = .42 across its predictors, with .40 counting as large. That is a figure about the review, not a number you can attach to any one predictor.

Interactive · seven jobs, three moves

Who does it next time

Seven things that have to happen for one family this spring. For each one, decide whether you do it for them, coach them through it while they do it, or teach the topic first so they can do it without you. There is no score. The readout is how much of this family’s work still belongs to you a year from now.

The family, mid-March

Second year at TRACE · turned 19 in January

Two parents, both working, one of them at every meeting. They came to the annual with a folder and questions about what happens when the program ends. They have never contacted an adult-services agency. They are not hesitant and they are not unengaged — nobody has ever walked them through any of this.

A composite, written so the seven jobs have somewhere to land. The three moves come from Guideposts Area 5 and from the practice literature, not from a rubric.

Loading the seven jobs. With scripting off, here is the exercise on paper. For each of these, decide whether you would do it for the family, coach them through it while they do it, or teach the topic in its own scheduled conversation first: the first call to the regional center; explaining what changed when their son turned 18; checking that the postsecondary goals are measurable and grounded in assessment; building the transition binder; raising their concern out loud in the IEP meeting; answering the fear that a paycheck ends the benefits; and getting the student ready to take part in his own meeting. Then ask which of the seven that family could do without you next spring.

    Doing it for a family is faster every single time, and it is sometimes the right call. It is also the only one of the three moves that leaves the family exactly where it started, which matters in a program the student ages out of.

      “Teach” here means what Rowe and colleagues mean by parent training: instruction between staff and family in which the family studies a topic for a set period of time. Not a handout in a folder. The family is illustrative, nothing is stored, and nothing leaves the page.

      Advocate to coach

      The most useful idea in the Guideposts for Success 2.0 for a program serving 18-to-22-year-olds is buried in a bullet. NCWD/Youth, at the Institute for Educational Leadership and funded by ODEP, published version 2.0 in May 2019 for youth ages 12 to 25. Area 5 is Family Engagement, and it asks families to shift gradually from advocate to coach as youth autonomy increases.

      An advocate speaks for someone. A coach prepares someone to speak. For a 17-year-old that shift is a good idea; for a 19-year-old whose educational rights have already transferred, it has stopped being optional.

      Area 5 runs in two directions

      Families model high expectations, take part in meetings, understand what changes at the age of majority, and make that advocate-to-coach shift. Professionals practice cultural competency, coordinate across systems, provide benefits planning support, train families on high expectations and youth autonomy, and share information on supported decision-making as an alternative to guardianship. Half of Area 5 is a list of things the family is supposed to receive. The monthly sets up the whole case →

      That two-sided framing is what keeps the coaching idea from becoming a way of handing families a job and calling it empowerment. A family cannot coach toward a future nobody has described to them, or weigh options they have never heard named. Which puts you back at the one family-facing thing in this literature that is a confirmed practice: teaching them.

      Not the master area. Area 5 is one of five, and it is not the one that touches every other. The framework’s most expansive area is Area 4, Connecting Activities — health, housing, transportation, benefits counseling, and the cross-system work.

      Try this week

      Teach one family one thing.

      Pick one family and one topic — what happens when the program ends, or what changed at 18. Put twenty minutes on the calendar for that topic alone, tell them ahead what it will cover, and hold it outside an IEP meeting.

      Scheduled, named, and studied for a set period of time. That shape is the difference between the thing that carries an Evidence-Based Practice classification and the thing that carries a handout.
