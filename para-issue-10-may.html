🗺️ This month’s focus

The Fall Para’s First Week

You already know the thing this issue is about, and you have probably never written it down: the same student is a different student at every site.

Fine at the grocery store. Falls apart at the bus stop. Needs nothing at all at the library, to the point where you have wondered whether you should even be standing there. Same student, same week, same skills on paper — three completely different afternoons.

Nobody at a desk sees that. It is only visible from where you stand, because you are the one who goes to all three places. And it is the single most useful thing anyone could tell the person who takes that student out in September.

This is the last issue of the year, so it is worth being precise about what is actually being claimed. Not that the student is inconsistent. Not that they are having a bad day at the bus stop. Something narrower and more useful: a skill and the place you use it are not separable, and our paperwork is built to record only the first half.

Why the community is the hard case

There is a finding in the employment-skills research that reads like a methodology note and is actually about your Tuesday. Reviewing the intervention studies in this area, more than 60% included community-based instruction during the teaching phase or the generalization phase — and the skills taught in community settings varied more than the ones taught at school, because a real site offers tasks a classroom cannot simulate.

That variation is the job. A register at one store is not the register at another store. A bus stop with a shelter and a bench is a different problem from a pole on a sidewalk. When you rotate a small group through several sites in a week, you are not repeating a lesson in different rooms — you are running the same student through genuinely different demands, and watching which ones land.

The field takes this seriously enough to measure it separately. Generalization is its own phase, probed on purpose, because working in the taught setting does not establish working anywhere else. When it does hold up, that is a result worth having: in one small study, three teenagers taught to seek help when they got separated from an adult — rules, role-play and praise, taught in a real store rather than a classroom — went on to ask for help in stores they had never trained in. Three students. Real finding, small study. The point is that somebody had to go and check.

The student who ignored the app. In the travel-training study March was built on, one of the four participants never reached mastery: she ignored the app’s prompts and navigated by environmental cues instead. That is not a failure of a skill. It is a student telling you what she is actually using in that setting, which is exactly the kind of thing that reaches nobody unless a para writes it down.

Everybody in the chain got a document except one person

April walked through the exit paperwork, and the shape of it is worth laying out one more time, because there is a hole in it that only shows up when you list the readers:

  • A coworker or supervisor at a job site — gets a person, in April, by being enlisted before you fade.
  • A community college DSPS office — gets the Summary of Performance, which they accept as part of disability verification.
  • A DOR counselor or an adult provider — gets the same document, written for them.
  • The student — gets it too, and at 18 and up it is theirs. More on that below, because it is a legal point and it is not optional.
  • The para who has this student in September — gets nothing.

That last line is not an oversight by anyone. The Summary of Performance is federal, and it is required only when a student leaves — graduating with a regular diploma, or aging out. A student who turns 19 in March and comes back in August has not left. There is no exit document, because there was no exit. There is an IEP, and there is a file.

The returning student’s next para is the only reader in the whole chain with nothing written for them.

What the file does hold, and what it does not

Here is where it would be easy to say something satisfying and false. The file is not empty of the good stuff, and it is not supposed to be. The present levels section is exactly where functional performance lives. The Functional Performance section of an SOP is defined as behavior across environments — school, community, and work — self-care, mobility, self-determination, safety, executive functioning. On paper, all of this belongs there.

The problem is not the location. It is the language. The documented failure is category language where usable language should be: accommodations listed with no explanation of the functional need they address, so the person on the receiving end cannot evaluate whether to grant them, or how to give it. The guidance to fix it is one sentence long — think of the next environment — and April spent a section on it, so this issue will not repeat it.

What this issue adds is the axis April did not have, because April was written around a single job site and your week is not. Naming the setting is not a stylistic improvement to a sentence. It is a second piece of information. “Needs a written sequence for multi-step tasks” is already plain language and already useful. “Needs the written sequence at the store, not at the library” is a different sentence, and nobody but you can write it.

Said as the worry it is. You will hear that a new para “starts from zero every year and runs the same experiments over again.” Nothing in what we have read measures that — there is no study of para-to-para knowledge loss across a summer, and this page is not going to pretend there is. It is a practitioner worry, widely felt, unquantified. Treat it the way March treated “one bad trip sets a student back months”: a real thing people notice, not a finding you can cite.

Interactive · three sentences, one student, September

Read it cold

For once you are not the para writing. You are the para reading. It is your first week, you have this student on Thursday, you are going to a community site, and this is everything you have been handed about them. Three sentences, all about the same student, all true. For each one, answer the only question that actually matters on a first week: what would you do differently tomorrow because of this?

From the file · sentence 1

Has difficulty with transitions and changes in routine.

From the file · sentence 2

Requires visual supports and antecedent-based intervention to access community-based instruction; see BIP.

From last year’s para · sentence 3

At the bus stop, if the bus is more than about five minutes late, they start pacing toward the street. Show them the arrival time on your phone and say the number out loud. At the store and the library this has never come up.
3 still to read

All three sentences are invented for this page, and all three are the kind of thing that really gets written. None of them is wrong. Sentence one is a category, sentence two is accurate and addressed to somebody who already knows the student, and sentence three is the only one that names a place.

☀️ Practice spotlight

Three Things to Try Tomorrow

📍 1. Say the site out loud before you say the student

A one-word habit that changes what comes out of your mouth. Start the sentence with the place: “At the trolley platform…” “In the back of the store…” Then finish it. You will notice immediately that some of the things you were about to say do not survive the move — you cannot put a place in front of “is inflexible.” That is the sentence telling you it was about the student in general when what you saw happened somewhere in particular.

⚖️ 2. Write the site where nothing happens

The library where the student needs nothing is information, and it is the piece that never gets recorded because nothing went wrong. It tells the next para three things at once: this student can do it somewhere, here is what that setting is like, and do not send a person to stand next to them there. A handoff made only of problems produces a fall para who over-supports everywhere, which is the fade going backwards without anybody deciding to.

💬 3. Ask the student which site is the hard one

They are 18 to 22 and this is their information. Ask which place they would pick if they could only go to one, and which one they would drop. You will sometimes get an answer that reorders everything you thought you knew — the site you had marked as easy is the one they dread and have been managing quietly. A student who can say that out loud is carrying a support that goes with them to every setting after this one, and it is a better handoff than anything you can write.

Whose handoff it is, legally

This needs saying plainly, because the intuitive version is wrong. At 18, educational rights transfer from the parent to the student — in California and most states — and after that transfer the school communicates directly with the student, not the parent. A parent who wants to stay involved does so with the student’s consent. Every student at TRACE is on the far side of that line.

So the handoff goes to the student, and to family with the student’s consent. Not around them. In practice that is a small change with a large effect: you write the thing, and then you show it to the person it is about, and they get to say “that’s not right” or “don’t put that in.” Which is also the fastest quality check available to you.

One caveat worth knowing rather than guessing at: a student may have a conservator, and California has a limited conservatorship specific to adults with developmental disabilities that covers some domains and not others — someone can hold authority over residence but not over educational decisions. If you do not know which applies to a student, that is a question for the teacher, not something to assume in either direction.

⚠️ Watch out for

The Trait Sentence

Not jargon. Not a category label. Something that looks like good, warm, plain writing and still leaves the reader with nowhere to stand.

A trait sentence describes the student as if the setting were not there. “He gets anxious in crowds.” “She’s great with people.” “He shuts down when he’s frustrated.” These are kind, they are recognizable, they are usually true, and they are the sentences most likely to survive a rushed handoff — because they are easy to say.

They are also the ones that travel worst, and for a specific reason: a trait is portable by construction. It claims to apply everywhere. So the reader applies it everywhere, which is how a student who is fine at the library acquires a person standing next to them at the library.

You watched something happen in a place. What you wrote down was a property of a person.

The place was half the information and it did not make it into the sentence

The tell is simple and you can run it on your own writing in about a second. Try putting a place in front of the sentence. If it still makes sense — “at the register, he stops when the total comes up” — you wrote an observation. If the place makes it sound strange — “at the register, he gets anxious in crowds” — you wrote a trait, and the next para will be guessing about which of their sites it applies to.

The other direction, which is also a failure

Do not overcorrect into a list of incidents. A handoff that is nothing but “on March 4th at the store…” is a log, not a handoff, and a person with a new caseload in September will not read it. What the fall para needs is the pattern with its setting attached: where it happens, what it looks like when it starts, what you do, and where it does not happen at all. Four things, one or two sentences, per site that matters.

And the honest limit on all of this

Nothing in what we have read defines a para-to-para handoff document, says who owns it, or says where it goes. There is no form, no policy, and no required field. The exit document has a statute behind it; this has nothing behind it but the fact that the information is real and currently ends the year in one person’s head.

Which makes this a team question rather than a solo project. Where does what you write actually go — to the teacher, into the present levels for the next IEP, into a folder somebody opens in August? Ask before you write, in May, rather than discovering in September that it went nowhere. And if what you write is going to be read by someone outside the team, it goes through the student first.

Interactive · you write, the page checks

The sentence that travels

Ten issues, and this page has never once asked you to write anything. Here it is. One sentence about one real student, the kind you would want the fall para to have. The page runs three mechanical checks on it — does it name a setting, does it say what to do rather than what the student has, and could a stranger act on it Monday — and hands back a rewrite prompt. It does not grade you. It cannot. It is a text-matching trick, and you are the one who knows the student.

🔒

Nothing here is stored and nothing is sent. What you type stays in this browser tab and disappears the moment you close or reload it. There is no saving, no account, no history, and nothing leaves your device — the checks run inside this page. Even so, this site is public: use a first name only, or no name at all.

0 words Not saved. Not sent.

The three checks come from the guidance on writing for the next environment: explain the functional need rather than the category, and write for a reader who does not already know the student. The setting check is this issue’s addition, and it is the one the guidance does not make explicit — because the guidance was written about a document that leaves school, not about a student who comes back in August to a different para and the same four sites.

🎁 Resource of the month

The “My One-Pager” template

A single page, four quadrants: Strengths, Interests, Preferences, Needs. It is free, it comes from I’m Determined at the Virginia Department of Education, and it is built to be filled in by the student and carried into IEP meetings and employer conversations. It is the output artifact of a SPIN profile, and it is the closest thing in our files to a one-page handoff that a real person will actually read.

imdetermined.org →

Two honest notes. It is a student-facing self-advocacy artifact, not a para-to-para form — which is a feature, given who the handoff legally belongs to, but it does mean the quadrants are about the person rather than the places. And it is Virginia DOE material, so where it names an agency, substitute the California one.

If you want the fourth box to be a setting box, our files have a close relative worth knowing about. The Discovering ME! foundation profile uses its own four squares — Strengths and Contributions, Interests, Conditions for Success, and Challenges — and that third square is the one this whole issue has been circling. Conditions is a place to write what has to be true around the student for things to go well, which is a setting question wearing a different name.

Whichever you use, the useful move is the same and it is small: after each quadrant, add where. Not a new form. A sentence you already had, finished.

📚 Ten issues

What This Was, in Order

August to May, ten issues, one argument told in pieces. Here it is with the pieces next to each other, which is a thing you can only do at the end.

Read in a line, the year turns out to have had a shape nobody announced at the start. It began with how much help, moved through what you see and who decides, and ends at who reads what you wrote. Every issue is still up:

  • 01 · August — Use the Least Help That Works — the prompting ladder, and the case for starting at the bottom of it.
  • 02 · September — Data You’ll Actually Keep — what you can count on a real shift, and why memory is not data.
  • 03 · October — The Support That Fades Works — taking it down a notch at a time, and back one notch when it stalls.
  • 04 · November — Real Independence Happens in Real Places — the community outing, moment by moment. The direct ancestor of this issue.
  • 05 · December — Behavior Is Communication — what happens before the behavior, and what it is asking for.
  • 06 · January — Connection Without Hovering — standing close enough to matter and far enough to be useful.
  • 07 · February — Their Voice, Not Yours — waiting out the pause, and letting the answer be theirs.
  • 08 · March — On Their Own — travel training, the staged fade, and one trip broken into its hard steps.
  • 09 · April — Hand It Over — every support you provide, with a name written beside it.
  • 10 · May — The Fall Para’s First Week — this one. The setting, written down.

There is a thread through all ten and it is not subtle once you see it. Every issue was about information that only exists where you are standing — the prompt you actually gave, the count nobody else took, the moment the behavior started, the pause you let run, the site where nothing went wrong. None of it is recoverable from a file. All of it changes what happens next.

That is the argument, and it is why this newsletter existed. Not to tell you what to do. To make the case that what you already see is the scarcest thing in the building.

One thing before your next shift

One student. One site. One sentence.

Pick the student whose September you are most worried about, and the one site where you are the reason it goes well. Write the sentence: where it happens, what it looks like when it starts, what you do, and where it does not happen at all. Show it to the student. Then find out from the teacher where it goes.

That is the whole assignment, and it is the last one. Ten issues, ten things to try before a shift. If any of them stuck, it was because you already half knew it and someone finally wrote it down — which is, as it happens, exactly what this issue is asking you to do for the next person.

Thank you for the year. This work is done in parking lots and on platforms and in the back aisles of grocery stores, mostly unwatched, and the record of it is a student who walks somewhere on their own. See you at a site.
