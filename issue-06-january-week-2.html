Name it, model it,
catch it. But
what’s the lesson?

Four worked lessons, built the same way, designed to run anywhere — and one of them is structured to make a point.

↓ Start reading
Following up on Issue 06 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

The monthly gave you the move: name the skill, model it, catch it when it happens. Reasonable advice, and it stops one step short of the thing you actually have to do, which is stand in front of a room on Tuesday.

CTI publishes four worked lessons on the workplace readiness skills. They were written for court-involved youth and are built to run in any setting — a classroom, a detention facility, a diversion program, community supervision — which is another way of saying they assume very little about your room, your materials, or your students’ reading level.

01 This Week’s Focus

Six parts, and
the two everyone cuts

Every one of the four lessons runs the same sequence. When time is short, the same two pieces are always the first to go — and they are the two doing most of the work.

  1. 1
    Objectives

    What the student should be able to do at the end. One sentence.

  2. 2
    Materials

    Deliberately minimal — paper, tape, a printed page.

  3. 3
    Icebreaker

    The thing that makes an awkward activity survivable for a student who does not want to be there.

  4. 4
    Instruction

    The direct teaching. The part it is safe to shorten.

  5. 5
    Activity

    Where the skill gets used rather than described.

  6. 6
    Reflection

    Where the skill gets named out loud — which is the entire move the monthly was arguing for.

Cut the instruction phase. Keep the icebreaker and the reflection.

The opposite of what a short period tempts you into

Four skills, four activities

Listening & Speaking — SLANT. Sit up straight, Lean forward, Answer, Nod, Track the speaker. Five physical, checkable behaviors standing in for “pay attention.” A student can be coached on Nod. Nobody can be coached on Pay Attention.

Teamwork — build a tower. Paper and tape, small groups. The build is not the lesson. The debrief is, and it goes specifically at who did what: who took a role, who gave one up, whose idea got used and how that got decided.

Creativity & Innovation — the circles. A page of pre-drawn circles, two minutes on the clock, turn them into objects. Short enough that a student who hates open-ended tasks can survive it, and the time limit is doing that work deliberately.

Conflict Resolution — two activities, not one

This is the one to look at closely. The conflict lesson contains a scenario-based resolution activity and a separate activity on calming strategies — because calm is a prerequisite for resolution, not a nice-to-have alongside it.

A dysregulated student is often unable to follow directions, learn, or self-reflect in the moment. So a conflict-resolution lesson delivered to a student who is already escalated teaches nothing at all. You are not skipping the teaching by waiting. You are making it possible.

Adjustable by design. The lessons are written to be scaled up or down for skill level rather than replaced.

Interactive · pick a skill, pick a period

Build the lesson

Choose what you are teaching and how long you actually have. The plan assembles underneath, with the reflection questions written out. Watch what happens to it when the period gets short.

Teamwork · full period

    The activities and the six-part structure are CTI’s. The reflection questions are drawn from the same lessons. What is added here is only the triage — which parts survive a ten-minute window and which do not.

    When the lesson stalls

    CTI’s companion home toolkit carries the moves for the students who will not start. None of them are complicated and all of them are worth having ready before you need them.

    • First / Then. Name what comes after before you name the task.
    • Easy–Easy–Hard. Two things they can already do, then the one you are teaching. Momentum is a real mechanism rather than a trick.
    • Reinforcement over consequences. The toolkit is explicit about the order of preference, and it is not a soft position — it is what actually moves a resistant learner.
    • For non-readers: visual schedules and task breakdowns. None of these four lessons require a student to read in order to participate, which is worth checking before assuming a lesson is above someone.
    Watch the reinforcement direction. During an escalation, attention intended as redirection can function as reinforcement instead.

    Try this week

    Run the tower.

    Paper, tape, small groups, fifteen minutes. Then spend as long on the debrief as you did on the build — who took a role, who gave one up, who decided.

    If the debrief feels longer than it should, you are doing it right. That is the part that transfers.
