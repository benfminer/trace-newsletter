Hand it over. On purpose, to someone with a name.

🤝 This month’s focus

Hand It Over

Every support you are providing right now is going to stop. The only question is whether anybody has decided what happens next.

There is a line in the systematic-instruction curricula that gets read as a throwaway and is actually the whole of this issue. It says that when you begin training, you should already be anticipating how the teaching supports come off — and then it adds the part people skip: even for a duty the person may never perform independently, plan ahead to determine who provides that support once the trainer is gone — a coworker, a personal care attendant, an adaptive device, or no one because the need resolves.

Read that again, because it contains a permission slip. It does not say fade everything. It says that for every piece of support on the job site, somebody has to be named — and “the student, independently” is one of five possible answers, not the only acceptable one.

That reframes what April is for. In October this newsletter walked through the fade: two things come down, what you say and where you stand, one notch at a time, back one notch when it stalls. All of that still holds and none of it is repeated here. This month is the inventory that should have been sitting next to it the whole time — a list of every support you provide, with a name written beside each one.

April is the right month for it for an unglamorous reason: the year ends in June, some of your students age out at 22, and the document that is supposed to carry all of this forward is legally required to be finished during their final year. More on that in a bit — it is the last section, and it is the one with a citation on it.

A word that has never appeared in this newsletter. Eight issues in, and “natural support” has not turned up once. That is an oversight, because it is the name for the thing this whole issue is about: the help that comes from coworkers, supervisors, and the workplace itself rather than from a paid disability service.

Enlist first. Then teach.

Here is the move that makes April different from October, and it is genuinely counterintuitive.

The handover is not something you do at the end. It is a decision you make before you teach anything at all.

The model behind that sentence is the Seven Phase Sequence — Marc Gold’s training sequence from 1980, revised for real work environments by Michael Callahan in 1991, and in general use through Callahan and Garner’s Keys to the Work Place (1997). It is a decision model: it tells you which support to try next, and in what order, while somebody is learning a job. Its whole logic is to start with what the workplace already does and escalate only as far as necessary, instead of defaulting to specialized instruction on day one.

Phases one through three are called the natural phases, and they happen before instruction:

  • 1. The natural ways — how jobs are actually performed here. The methods for the specific tasks, the step-by-step procedures, the culture of the place, how workers interact, the managerial style, and what the employer actually cares about in a performance.
  • 2. The natural means — how this employer communicates what it wants. What teaching strategies they use to bring on a new hire, if any. How they motivate people. What the rules are.
  • 3. Identify and enlist the natural people — who typically supports new employees here, and who may be willing to.

Then you decide who initiates phase four, which is the actual teaching. Not the other way round.

The source calls phase three “the most novel phase for traditional supported employment providers,” which is a polite way of saying almost nobody does it. And it names exactly the failure it was written to correct. In the older model, the job developer promised the employer that the trainer would provide all necessary assistance and the employer would have no investment in training at all. The result was that the job coach became an interpreter positioned between the employer and the employee, and supported employees ended up isolated inside their own workplaces.

Phase three puts the natural people back in the loop before instruction begins.

Which is a very different job from introducing them to a coworker in May

What the evidence does and does not say

Before this goes any further, the honest version, because you are going to hear the loud version somewhere and it is not supportable.

A live debate, not a settled practice

Natural supports are contested in the field, and have been for thirty years. Wehman and Bricout point out that the concept has never been clearly operationalized — writers looking at the same phenomenon coined competing narrower terms (internal supports, typicalness) rather than agreeing on one.

On whether coworker support can stand in for a job coach: the comparative contribution has not been empirically tested. The one direct comparison in that literature — Lee, Storey, Anderson, Goetz and Zivolich, 1997, mentoring versus job coach instruction — suggested mentors facilitated more social interaction, but the authors could not rule out differences in training methods and in who was in the study, and a full year passed between the training and the data collection.

Test and Wood’s 1996 review of the same question is titled The Jury is Still Out. It is still out.

So: nobody has shown that a coworker is a better support than a job coach, and this page is not going to tell you that. What the critique argues against is treating natural supports as a replacement for skilled support rather than as a target of it. Marc Gold & Associates make the matching point from the practice side: a facilitator has to hold both halves at once — that there are limits to natural capacity, and that there are things a human-services facilitator can never do as well as the natural people on a job site.

There is one thing that is not contested, and it is a consequence rather than a comparison. In the early supported employment model the coach did everything: all the training, liaison to coworkers and the boss, covering absences, absorbing production slack. When the coach eventually faded, coworkers did not know how to interact with the employee, and often believed they needed a job coach’s skills merely to give performance feedback.

That is the sentence to carry around. Not “coworkers are better.” Coworkers who were never brought in end up convinced that ordinary workplace behavior requires a specialist. Everything in this issue is aimed at that one belief.

Availability is not access. A support existing in a workplace does not mean the person will use it or benefit from it. It is common not to know what supports exist, how to choose between them, or how to get one. Closing that gap is exactly what a knowledgeable person is for.

Four reasons to start less naturally

None of this is a rule that you must always start with the coworkers. The same source names four situations that justify taking more instructional control up front, and they are worth knowing so that nobody can use this page against you:

  • The job developer already told the employer that the facilitator would do all the initial instruction — so involve the natural supporters as much as you can anyway, then fade to them.
  • The specific impact of the disability means somebody should be modeling systematic instruction for the natural supporters first.
  • Expectations — the employer’s, the student’s, or the family’s — are fragile enough that a period of greater instructional control is warranted.
  • Physical accessibility or productivity modifications are needed. Best made before the employee starts, and the employer should be part of solving them rather than having it solved for them.

And whichever way you go, the sequence is explicit that you do not flip. Departures from natural strategies get made “in a measured manner by using small, incremental changes.” That cuts both directions — toward more support and toward less.

Interactive · six supports, five holders

Who carries this?

Not how much support — that was October. This is whose. Six supports a para is commonly providing at a job site in April. For each one, name who holds it after June. There is no answer key and the five holders are not ranked; what the tool has is a different thing to say about each pairing.

1 The cue that starts the next task.

The thing that happens when one job is finished and the next one has not been started — and right now, most days, it is you saying something.

2 Knowing the task is finished, and finished right.

Every “good job,” every nod, every “okay, what’s next” is doing this job at the moment. Something has to do it in June.

3 Telling them when something is off.

Ordinary performance feedback. Slower than usual, wrong bin, missed a row — who says so, and how do they say it?

4 Knowing where to go when something goes wrong.

The machine jams, the delivery is short, somebody is bothering them. Today they look for you.

5 The unwritten rules.

What you can’t microwave. How to ask a coworker a question. Which manager wants to be interrupted and which one does not.

6 The order the work happens in.

Which cart first, which shelf after that. Right now the sequence lives in your head, and you feed it out one step at a time.

The student 0 A coworker 0 The supervisor 0 An adaptation 0 Nobody 0 6 still unassigned

The six supports are illustrative — a real inventory is written for one student at one site and it is longer than this. The five holders are not. They are the list the systematic-instruction curricula actually give: a coworker, a personal care attendant, an adaptive device, or no one because the need resolves — plus the student, which is the answer everyone starts from.

☀️ Practice spotlight

Three Things to Try Tomorrow

🙋 1. Ask to be taught the job

Phase two of the sequence has one instruction that outranks the rest, and it costs you nothing: ask to be taught the job by the people who would naturally assist the student. Not by the manager in an orientation, and not by reading the binder. By the coworker who would have shown any new hire. You learn the actual methods and the actual shortcuts, you find out whether this place teaches anybody anything, and — the part that matters in April — you have just cast somebody in the role before you asked them to play it.

🔁 2. Route the question instead of answering it

The guidance for growing natural supports is blunt about this: when questions come up, have the natural supports offer the help or the solution instead of answering yourself. Same for social time — invite conversation between coworkers rather than routing it through you. This is a small daily habit and it is the difference between a coworker who has answered your student twice and one who has never once been the person asked. Answering is faster. That is exactly the problem.

🔍 3. Find the supports that already exist

Before building anything, look for what is already there. Three questions from the field guidance: Are there connections or relationships already in place? Is there anyone who instinctively steps up and helps out? Was a connection to the employer used in landing the job? That third one is easy to forget and often the one worth chasing — somebody already vouched for this placement, and they are still standing there.

If the natural person needs help

Phase five of the sequence is the one you are most likely to actually run, and it is not “take over.” It is support, assist, or substitute for the natural people, in that order, and it lists the moves:

  • Demonstrate a more powerful technique. Show the coworker the thing that works, once, in front of them.
  • Negotiate a time for feedback with the natural supporter. A standing two minutes, not an ambush at the end of a shift.
  • Offer training information — formally or informally, whichever this workplace actually does.
  • Shadow the natural supporter rather than the student.

And if somebody genuinely has to be substituted for, the order is stated: negotiate for a different coworker or trainer first. You are the most likely substitute and you should typically be considered last. That is not modesty. It is the same anti-interpreter logic as phase three.

These are moves, not scripts. Nothing in our files gives you the words for the coworker conversation — no dialogue, no protocol, no sample opener. The four moves above are documented; what you say while making them is not. Anything you see quoted anywhere, including on this page, is somebody’s house style. Use your own.
⚠️ Watch out for

Being the Interpreter

Not hovering. Not doing the job for them. Something quieter: being the channel every message goes through in both directions.

The supervisor tells you what they need and you relay it. The student has a question and asks you. A coworker wants to say something and looks at you first. Nothing about that is unkind and none of it looks like a problem from the outside — the placement is running smoothly, everybody is getting what they need. And the workplace is learning, every single shift, that talking to this employee requires going through a specialist.

That is the documented failure mode from earlier in this issue, arriving one small relay at a time. The named caution in the job-coaching literature is over-advocacy: advocating on the student’s behalf can undermine the student’s own relationship with the employer and stunt the development of self-determination. The four roles a coach cycles through — trainer, advocate, consultant, collaborator — include a consultant role for exactly this reason: consultation to the employer, rather than constant presence, is often the right call so that the support does not become a barrier to the student-employer relationship.

A test you can run this week

For one shift, count how many times a message passes through you that could have gone directly. Not instructions you are giving — relays. Supervisor to student. Student to coworker. Coworker to student.

You are not counting mistakes. Some of those relays are you doing your job properly. You are counting how many channels currently run through a person who leaves in June, which is a different question and is not one you can answer from memory.

The other half of the caution

Do not overcorrect into disappearing. The needs on a job site are genuinely heterogeneous: one person may need a coworker to teach one task, a coach to teach another, family for transportation, VR for uniforms, a supervisor to monitor performance, a benefits consultant for something else entirely. Managing that array is itself a job-coach function. It is not something natural supports absorb, and handing it to nobody is not a handover.

Also worth saying plainly, because it is the thing this issue could accidentally imply: the workplace accommodation needs may look different from the school IEP accommodations. Do not assume they transfer directly.

The scope question, said plainly

This issue is telling you to talk to coworkers and to negotiate feedback time with a supervisor, so it owes you a straight answer about your own role, and the straight answer is the same one March gave: nothing we have read defines what a paraeducator specifically may or may not do as a job coach, what supervision is required, who signs off on a fade plan, or who is allowed to approach an employer’s staff. There is no policy here to quote. The employment literature is explicit that it has no evidence on who delivers instruction; the job-coaching material in our files is expert opinion about the practice, not about who performs it.

So: this is a team decision, made with the teacher and with whoever owns the employer relationship. Somebody in your program has that relationship — the job developer, the teacher, a transition specialist — and enlisting a coworker is a move inside it, not a move you make alone. You execute an agreed plan, and you report what you observe, accurately, including the parts that went badly. If nobody has told you where your line is, that is the question to ask before the next shift rather than after it.

And a gap you should know about

One honest limit on everything above. All of the natural-supports material in our files is adult supported employment — paid jobs, real coworkers, a supervisor with a payroll interest in the outcome. Nothing in it covers a school-based work placement, where the “coworkers” may be other students and the “supervisor” may have agreed to host somebody as a favor. Whether the model transfers to an unpaid semester-long placement is unexamined, and pretending otherwise would be inventing a finding.

Which does not make it useless — it makes it a question worth asking out loud about your own site. Is there a real workforce here with real workplace norms, or is this a site that has been kind to us? The answer changes who can plausibly hold anything after June, and it is worth saying to the team in April rather than discovering in the summer.

📝 What you write down

The Document With a Deadline

For a student who ages out, the exit document is not the IEP. It is the Summary of Performance, and it is federal.

The Summary of Performance is required by IDEA 2004 — 20 U.S.C. § 1414(c)(5)(B)(ii) — whenever a student with a disability leaves secondary school either by graduating with a regular diploma or by aging out. Your students who turn 22 are the aging-out case. The statute requires the district to provide “a summary of the child’s academic achievement and functional performance, which shall include recommendations on how to assist the child in meeting the child’s postsecondary goals.”

Here is the sentence that makes it an April topic rather than a June one: the SOP must be completed during the final year of the student’s high school programming. That is the deadline, and it is the reason the inventory you just built has somewhere to go.

The SOP is not an IEP, not a 504 plan, and not an evaluation — it is a synthesis document, written for whoever supports the student next: a community college disability services office, a DOR counselor, an adult provider, or the student themselves. Five content areas are standard. Two of them are yours in a way they are nobody else’s:

  • Functional Performance — behavior across environments: school, community, and work. Self-care, mobility, self-determination, safety, executive functioning. Nobody else on the team has watched this student at a job site for a school year.
  • Recommendations for Supports — accommodations, modifications, adaptive devices, compensatory strategies, and collateral support services the student will need in the settings that come after this one. Which is the tool from the middle of this issue, written out in a sentence apiece.

The other three are postsecondary goals, academic achievement, and next steps — concrete action items rather than intentions.

The failure mode has a name too

Three ways an SOP goes wrong

Too late, too thin — completed as a compliance checkbox in the final weeks of the last year, not connected to actual transition planning or to the student’s postsecondary goals.

Staff-written, student-excluded — finished by the case manager with no student participation. The student exits without knowing what it says or being able to explain their own disability and support needs.

Jargon-heavy, context-free — accommodations listed with no explanation of the functional need they address, so the person on the receiving end cannot evaluate whether to grant them.

All three are fixable from where you sit, and the guidance says how. Think of the next environment: minimize the educational jargon and explain each accommodation in terms of the functional need rather than a category label. “Needs a written sequence for multi-step tasks” travels. An acronym does not.

And involve the student. A student who helped write the SOP can present it — which is a very different object from a document handed to them at graduation. If a student can say out loud what they need and why, they are carrying a support that travels, and it is the one thing on this page that goes with them to every job after this one.

Why it is worth your effort. Under the framework colleges actually use, the SOP is rated the highest-utility exit document from K–12 for establishing disability at a disability services office — above the IEP, because it was designed for postsecondary use in the first place. California community college DSPS offices accept it. What you write in April is what a counselor reads in September.
🎁 Resource of the month

The Center on Transition Innovations

VCU’s practitioner-facing hub for transition resources: Fast Facts briefs on specific topics, practitioner guides, toolkits, and a free self-paced online course on work-based learning for staff running placements. Free, and most of it opens without a login. There is a Fast Facts brief on the Summary of Performance, which is a reasonable first thing to read after this issue.

centerontransition.org →

Two honest caveats. CTI is funded by the Virginia Department of Education, so the policy framing is Virginia’s — where it names an agency, substitute the California one. And its materials are primarily expert-opinion level: they synthesize what the field agrees on, and most briefs do not cite primary studies. Useful, and not the same thing as an evidence base.

One more thing about the fade, since April is when people start scheduling one. Before you write a fade plan, the readiness guidance asks three questions, and the one that belongs to this issue is the middle one:

  • Can the employee independently perform the essential job functions?
  • Does the employee know where to seek on-the-job assistance from coworkers and supervisors?
  • Are there supports ensuring the employee initiates work tasks rather than waiting to be told?

That middle question is the handover written as a testable item. Not “is there a friendly coworker” — does the student know who, by name, for what. Ask your student today. If the answer is you, you have found this month’s work.

The same guidance adds a check that has nothing to do with skills: whether the employer feels production and understanding are where they need to be, and whether there is a continued pattern of success rather than a single good day. The supervisor’s read is part of the decision, which is another reason to have talked to them before April.

One thing before your next shift

Write one name next to one support.

Pick a single support you provide — one cue, one check, one question you answer every day — and write down who holds it after June. A name, a device, or “nobody, because it resolves.” Not a category. A name.

Then find out whether that person knows. That gap — between who you have decided will carry it and who has actually been asked — is the entire job for the rest of the year.
