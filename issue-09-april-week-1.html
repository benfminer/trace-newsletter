The monthly said an IEP or a 504 Plan is sufficient. This week is about what that sentence actually does — and about the three conditions it leaves standing, one of which is an age band that national materials will hand you wrong.

New here? Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition — hover, tap, or tab to one to see it.

“Potentially eligible” is a phrase most transition staff have heard once, usually in the same breath as an assurance that it makes things easier. It does. It is also narrower than it sounds, and being precise about what it removes is the difference between a referral that goes through and a student who gets turned away by somebody acting in good faith.

Here is the shape of it. Pre-ETS eligibility rests on four conditions, and “potentially eligible” removes exactly one of them — the vocational rehabilitation eligibility determination. The other three stay. Two of those three are where your students actually get lost: the plan they happen to be on, and how old they are.

01 This Week’s Focus

Four conditions.
One of them lifted.

The wiki lists what a student has to satisfy to receive Pre-Employment Transition Services. Read it as a list of four rather than as a paragraph and the whole topic reorganizes itself.

  • Have a disability. The threshold question, and the one that is almost never in dispute for a student already carrying a plan.
  • Qualify for and receive special education or related services under an IEP or a 504 Plan. The word doing the work in that sentence is or, and it is the most under-used fact in this month’s material. The next section is about nothing else.
  • Participate in a secondary, postsecondary, or other recognized education program. Enrollment, not attendance history. This is also the condition that quietly decides the age question later on, because California’s age extension is written around program participation rather than around a birthday.
  • Fall within the age band — which is set by state law, not by a single national number. This is the one that gets printed wrong, and it gets printed wrong in both directions.

Nothing on that list is a VR eligibility determination. That is the point. Under WIOA Title IV, VR agencies must set aside 15 percent of their federal VR funds for Pre-ETS for students with disabilities who are eligible or potentially eligible for VR services — and “potentially eligible” means students do not need a full VR eligibility determination to receive services. An IEP or 504 Plan is sufficient.

The money is reserved before anybody refers anybody. What is not reserved is whether your students are among the ones it gets spent on.

The set-aside, restated

So the referral you are being asked to make is not an application for a scarce thing. It is a claim on a fund that already exists, made on the strength of a document your student is already carrying. What it is not is a guarantee of a particular service on a particular date — Pre-ETS is a framework of five required service categories, not a program a student enrolls in, and delivery in California runs through the Department of Rehabilitation either directly or through contracted providers.

What this issue will not claim. Pre-ETS is a policy framework, not a research-validated intervention with its own evidence base. Its five categories map onto practices that carry independent evidence, but the package has never been studied as a distinct intervention. Evidence level: policy. Federally mandated is a statement about law, not about effect.

The door nobody uses

Condition two says IEP or 504 Plan. Most referral practice reads only the first half, because most referral practice is attached to special education machinery — the annual meeting, the case manager, the transition file. A student on a 504 Plan sits outside all of it.

The wiki is direct about why that matters: the 504 inclusion is significant because students who are not in special education but have documented disabilities can access Pre-ETS, and that is a broader eligibility than traditional VR served. In California it is broader still in practice, because significant numbers of California students with learning disabilities, ADHD and anxiety are on 504 Plans rather than IEPs.

The instruction, as the wiki writes it

Partner schools should communicate this pathway to 504 students and families who may not know VR services are available to them at all. Not “consider whether it applies.” Tell them. A 504 student has no case manager whose job description includes agency linkage and no annual meeting at which an agency invitation is a required element — so nobody refers them, and the reason nobody refers them is that nobody’s process reaches them. The monthly makes the same case in one paragraph →

Worth noticing what this does to the entitlement-to-eligibility shift. In K-12 under IDEA the school identifies and serves, and the disclosure burden on the student is none — services come to the student. Afterward the student must self-identify, produce documentation and request. The wiki puts the consequence in five words: no one will seek them out. A 504 student is already living a partial version of that, which is exactly why the Pre-ETS pathway is worth naming to them out loud rather than leaving in a policy document.

Five categories, and the two names that get shortened

The monthly listed the five. What it did not do is separate them the way CTI’s Fast Facts table does — a school-side column and a student-side column — and the split is the useful part, because it shows which half of each category is a thing you arrange and which half is a thing a student does. Two of the five also carry a longer official name in that table than the one everybody uses, and in both cases the longer name is the more accurate one.

  1. 1
    Job exploration counseling

    School side: administer interest inventories, invite employers to speak, provide information on career-related student organizations. Student side: complete interest inventories, identify career clusters, attend a career fair.

  2. 2
    Work-based learning experiences

    School side: create a school-based enterprise, teach workplace readiness and financial literacy and travel training, match a student with an in-school work experience. Student side: conduct informational interviews, job shadow, tour a workplace, complete an internship.

  3. 3
    Counseling on Educational and Training Options

    Usually shortened to “counseling on postsecondary education,” which quietly narrows it to college. The table’s own title is the broader one, and its school-side list says so: postsecondary programs including community college, training, apprenticeships and certifications, plus CTE information sessions. Student side: college fairs, campus tours, shadowing classes at a career or technical school.

  4. 4
    Workplace readiness training

    School side: resume help, job search strategies, hygiene for employment. Student side: money management and budgeting, a mock interview — and scheduling and attending an SSI or SSDI benefits counseling session, which sits inside Pre-ETS rather than alongside it.

  5. 5
    Instruction in Self-Advocacy

    The table’s name again, and the word instruction is the difference. School side: teach students to request accommodations and then give them somewhere to practice, provide opportunities to lead an IEP or 504 meeting, encourage leadership clubs. Student side: practice disclosing disability on the job, lead an IEP meeting, attend presentations by recent graduates.

Where the examples come from. The activity lists are CTI’s Fast Facts (2024), published under a Virginia Department of Education contract. The five categories are federal and identical in California; the activities are illustrative menu options rather than a required set, and CTI material carries an expert-opinion evidence level and a Virginia lens.

Category five is the one that answers the opening of the monthly. Self-advocacy instruction is the only category on the list that is explicitly about the skill the next system requires and the current one does not — and it is a Pre-ETS service, which means it is a thing DOR can be asked to help deliver rather than a thing you have to find room for on your own.

Condition four, in full

This is the part of the topic where a national handout will hand you a number that is wrong for your students, and the error is easy to make because the federal rule does not contain a number at all. It contains two delegations.

34 CFR § 361.5(c)(51), implementing Rehabilitation Act § 7(37) at 29 U.S.C. § 705(37), defines a student with a disability as somebody in a secondary, postsecondary or other recognized education program who is not younger than the earliest age for the provision of transition services under IDEA — or, if the state elects a lower minimum for Pre-ETS, not younger than that — and not older than 21, or, if state law provides for a higher maximum age for receipt of services under IDEA, “not older than that maximum age.”

Both ends are handed to state law. That is why the same fact is printed differently in different states and why every one of those printings can be correct where it was written. California has answered both delegations, and the answers are not the ones in general circulation.

California, printed correctly

Age 16 through 21 — continuing past the 22nd birthday for a student still participating in a special education program. The floor is 16 because 16 is California’s IDEA transition age under Education Code 56345.1, and California never made the lower-minimum election that some other states did. The tail is 9 CCR § 7026.5, DOR’s own regulation, whose stated purpose is “to ensure there is no gap in pre-employment transition services.” Cal. Educ. Code § 56026(c)(4) carries the identical schedule. If you need a short form, 16 and up while they are still in the program is safe; a bare “16 to 21” silently drops your own oldest students.

The floor deserves one clarification, because it is a coincidence that looks like a rule. Sixteen is also the age at which IEP transition planning has to begin in California, under the same Education Code section. Those are two different requirements that happen to land on the same number here, because of an election California made about one of them. Do not merge them, and do not carry across the “contact the agency when students turn 14” standard you will see in national and Virginia materials — that number is a different state’s law.

The tail, and what it attaches to

The extension is real, it is Californian, and it is bounded. The regulation does not say “22.” It says a student who turns 22 before the end of their special education program keeps Pre-ETS on a schedule, and the schedule is set by the month of the birthday.

  • Turns 22 January through June — may continue participating for the remainder of the current fiscal year, including any extended school year program.
  • Turns 22 in July, August or September — shall not be allowed to begin a new fiscal year in the program. The one exception is written into the regulation: a student in a year-round program who is completing an IEP in a term that extends into the new fiscal year may complete that term.
  • Turns 22 in October, November or December — terminated from the program on December 31 of the current fiscal year, unless the student would otherwise complete their IEP at the end of that fiscal year anyway.

Now the distinction that does the real work, and it is the reason this section exists rather than a footnote. The extension attaches to program participation, not to age. A student who has already exited special education — graduated with a regular diploma, or aged out — is outside it, however recently they exited and whatever their birthday says.

That student is not out of options, and the difference is worth saying in plain terms rather than leaving as a shrug. General VR services and an Individualized Plan for Employment have no upper age limit at all. What an exited student loses is the Pre-ETS set-aside, not the agency. Two doors, and only one of them closes.

Expect the mismatch. DOR’s public Student Services page publishes eligibility as a flat “Age 16 through 21” and does not mention the turn-22 extension its own regulation grants, so a family who follows the link will read a narrower rule than the law provides. Send the link and the citation together — 9 CCR § 7026.5 — and the mismatch reads as information rather than a contradiction. Whether a given district office applies the extension in practice is an operational question this page cannot answer; ask them. dor.ca.gov/Home/StudentServices →

Interactive · one student, three questions

Read the calendar

The turn-22 rules are three sentences of regulation and they are almost impossible to hold in your head, because the answer depends on a birth month, on whether the student is still in the program, and on what already exists with DOR. Set those three and the page reads the regulation back at you — what it provides, what the calendar leaves, and what is worth doing about it. There is no score, and two of the answers are about a door that stays open rather than one that shuts.

Before you set anything

This reads the regulation, not your district

Everything the readout says about entitlement comes from 9 CCR § 7026.5 and 34 CFR § 361.5(c)(51), with Cal. Educ. Code § 56026(c)(4) carrying the same schedule. What it cannot tell you is how a particular DOR district office handles the extension in practice — DOR’s own public page omits it, so expect to have to cite it. Nothing here is stored and nothing leaves the page.

Use it on a real student in your head, not on paper. The site is world-readable.

Loading the three questions. With scripting off, here is the exercise on paper. First, the month the student turns — or turned — 22. Second, where they stand: still participating in a special education program, already exited by diploma or by aging out, or on a 504 Plan and never in special education. Third, what already exists with DOR: nothing at all, a referral with no counselor assigned yet, or Pre-ETS being delivered now. Then read the regulation against those three. If they are still in the program, the birth month decides the tail: January through June continues through the end of the fiscal year including extended school year; July, August or September may not begin a new fiscal year, except to finish a term already underway in a year-round program; October, November or December ends December 31 unless the IEP would end at fiscal year end anyway. If they have exited, the extension does not reach them at all — but general VR services and an Individualized Plan for Employment have no upper age limit. If they are on a 504 Plan, the plan is the door in, and the turn-22 tail is written around participation in a special education program rather than around them.
