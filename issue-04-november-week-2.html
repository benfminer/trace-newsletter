Following up on Issue 04 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

This month’s issue asked you to hand one student three sentences of their own meeting. This week goes through the meeting itself, moment by moment, because the three sentences are not the hard part. The hard part is the other six moments, where the habit of speaking for people is strongest and nobody notices it happening.

Read the transcript of most transition IEPs and the student appears twice: once when they are introduced, and once when they are asked whether they agree. Both times, briefly.

01 This Week’s Focus

“Student-led” is a ladder,
not a switch

Nobody goes from silent to chairing the meeting. There are steps between, and naming them is what makes the next one obvious.

  1. 1
    Present

    In the room, spoken about. This is where most students sit for years, and it is worth being honest that it is not participation.

  2. 2
    Answers when asked

    Responds to direct questions. Real, but the agenda still belongs to whoever is asking.

  3. 3
    Owns named pieces

    Has prepared and delivers specific parts — strengths, the goal, the accommodation. This is the practical target for most TRACE students, and it is genuinely achievable this year.

  4. 4
    Runs it

    Opens, moves through the agenda, invites others, closes. Uncommon, and not the point — step 3 delivers most of the benefit.

There are four packaged approaches with real evidence behind them, all classified as evidence-based practices in the Rowe and colleagues review of 114 secondary transition studies: the Self-Directed IEP, a four-unit package specifically for leading meetings; the Self-Advocacy Strategy; Whose Future Is It?; and Take Charge, which adds mentoring and peer and parent support. Effects run from small to large.

The most evidence-saturated corner of transition

That same review found six separate evidence-based practices aimed at self-determination and its component skills — more than any other functional-skills domain in secondary transition research. Whatever else is contested in this field, this is not.

You do not need to buy anything. The curricula help and are worth having. But every one of them is built out of the same materials you already have: rehearsal, a script that gets shorter, and an adult who stays quiet.

      02 Two ways this goes wrong

      The recital and the rescue

      1. The student reads a card

      A script is a scaffold and scaffolds come down. If a student reads the same laminated sentences in year one and year four, nothing has been handed over — the meeting has a new narrator and the same author.

      The test is whether they can answer a follow-up. “Why that job?” is the question that separates a rehearsed line from a position. Plan for the follow-up in rehearsal, not just the line.

      2. The room rescues them

      A student pauses. Three seconds pass, which feels like a minute to everyone else at the table, and a well-meaning adult fills it — finishes the sentence, explains what they meant, moves the agenda on.

      That is the single most common way a student-led meeting stops being one, and it is done entirely out of kindness. It is also the moment the student learns that if they wait long enough, someone else will handle it.

      Silence in a meeting is not a problem to be solved. It is the student thinking, in public, which is the whole exercise.

      Say so out loud beforehand and the room will hold

      The fix is procedural rather than personal: before the meeting, tell the other adults what the student is going to do and ask them to wait. Nobody interrupts on purpose. They interrupt because nobody told them not to.

      When the student and the family want different things

      It will happen, and the meeting is the right place for it rather than the wrong one. Since 18, the student is the one who consents — but winning the point is not the goal. A family that feels overruled disengages, and they are the support that outlasts the program by decades.

      Name the disagreement, put both positions in the notes, and give it a review date. Both of those are things the student can say themselves.

      Try this week

      Send one sentence before the meeting.

      Email the team: “Jasmine is going to open the meeting and say what she wants after she leaves. If she pauses, please wait — she has it.” That one line does more than any curriculum you could buy this month.
