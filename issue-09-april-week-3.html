You send the family the link. It is the right link — the official one, the state agency’s own page, the thing you would want them reading instead of whatever a search returns. They open it, find the eligibility line, and read that services are for students age 16 through 21. Their son is 22 and still in your program. They close the tab.

16–21 what DOR’s own Student Services page publishes And 9 CCR § 7026.5, DOR’s own regulation, extends pre-employment transition services past the 22nd birthday for a student still participating in a special education program — expressly “to ensure there is no gap.” Both of those are true at the same time. The page is still the right page. It just cannot be sent on its own.
01 This Week’s Focus

One page, sent
with a citation

Pre-ETS in California is administered by the Department of Rehabilitation and branded publicly as DOR Student Services. That page is the authoritative front door and there is no better one to hand over. dor.ca.gov/Home/StudentServices →

What it does well is what a front door should do: it names the program, says what the five service categories are, and gives a family somewhere official to start. What it does not do is mention the turn-22 extension. So a reader following your link lands on a narrower rule than the law provides, and the people most likely to be turned away by it are the oldest students on your roster — the ones with the least time to recover from a wrong answer.

Send both, in the same message

The link, and then the sentence that completes it: 9 CCR § 7026.5 extends pre-employment transition services to a student who turns 22 before the end of their special education program. Sent together, the mismatch reads as information — here is the official page, and here is the piece it leaves out. Sent apart, it reads as a contradiction, and in a contradiction between a state agency’s website and a staff member’s recollection, the website wins every time. Week 1 takes the age band apart →

Be careful about the next step, though, because there is a question this page cannot answer for you. Whether a given district office applies the extension routinely, or needs the regulation quoted at it, is an operational matter rather than a legal one — and DOR’s own page omitting the extension is a reason for care rather than confidence. Frame it as what the regulation provides, and confirm with the district office how they handle it. That is the honest shape of the advice, and it is more useful than a guarantee nobody here is in a position to make.

The gap this issue will not paper over. This vault holds no address, direct phone number, or named contact for the San Diego DOR district office. The only number it holds is DOR’s statewide line, 1-800-952-5544. So the instruction stays as honest as the record allows: ask your program lead, or search dor.ca.gov for the district office. And when you do find the counselor, write the name somewhere that outlives your caseload — the search is the part nobody should have to repeat.

One more thing about links. There is a plausible-looking eligibility page one slug over from the one above that does not exist — it returns a 404. If you keep a family-facing handout, check the URL you have written on it before April ends, because a dead link in a handout is worse than no handout: it reads to a family as a door that has been closed.

Interactive · five categories, four possible deliverers

Map the five

April’s issue said the move out loud without building it: trade lists, and find out what DOR is delivering to your students against what the IEP is already committing to. This is that, on one screen. For each of the five Pre-ETS categories, name every body currently delivering it — more than one is allowed, and so is nobody. There is no score. What comes back is what the overlap and the silence each mean.

What you are looking for

Two failure modes, opposite shapes

A category with nobody on it is a service the student is owed and is not getting. A category with two bodies on it may be richness, and may be duplicated effort — the first of the four documented barriers to interagency collaboration, ahead of competing demands, attitudes and stereotypes, and staffing shortages. Overlapping tasks between school transition staff and VR counselors are named as the exact mechanism: they dilute the team and create confusion. Neither failure is visible from inside one agency, which is why this is a list you trade rather than a list you write.

Answer it for one student, or for your program as a whole. The overlaps show up faster with a single student in mind.

With scripting off, the exercise runs on paper. The five Pre-ETS categories are job exploration counseling, work-based learning experiences, counseling on educational and training options, workplace readiness training, and instruction in self-advocacy. The four bodies that could be delivering any of them in San Diego are the school or IEP team, DOR directly, a DOR-contracted provider — DOR may deliver Pre-ETS itself or through contracted providers, and San Diego Workforce Partnership, JVS San Diego and Ability Innovations are the local names this vault holds — and San Diego Regional Center, for students with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Write the five down the page, put every name you can confirm beside each one, and then look twice: once at the rows with nothing beside them, and once at the rows with two names that have never spoken to each other.

        The five categories and their activity examples are the wiki’s, from CTI’s Fast Facts table; the delivery landscape — DOR directly or by contract, San Diego Workforce Partnership on work-based learning and workplace readiness, JVS San Diego and Ability Innovations as local providers, and the instruction to coordinate with the Regional Center so services come out complementary rather than duplicative — is recorded there too. The four barriers are documented, in that order. The reads under each combination are ours. Nothing here measures any real program, and Pre-ETS itself is a policy framework rather than a research-validated intervention with its own evidence base.

        Direct, or by contract

        The reason the map is worth drawing at all is that “DOR” is not one delivery mechanism. DOR may provide Pre-ETS directly through its own transition counselors, or through contracted providers, which means a service can be running for your students under a name that is not DOR’s. That cuts both ways. It is why “we do not have that here” is worth checking before it hardens into a fact, and it is why a category can turn out to have two deliverers when everybody believed it had one.

        • San Diego Workforce Partnership — the county’s WIOA-funded workforce development board, and a partner able to deliver the work-based learning and workplace readiness components of Pre-ETS. It is also a bridge to youth employment pathways that sit outside vocational rehabilitation altogether.
        • JVS San Diego and Ability Innovations — local providers that may deliver DOR-contracted Pre-ETS. Both are commonly funded through DOR or Regional Center vendor relationships, which is precisely the arrangement that makes a service hard to see from a school’s side of the table.
        • San Diego Regional Center — for students with intellectual and developmental disabilities, the instruction is coordination rather than referral: make sure Pre-ETS and Regional Center transition services come out complementary, not duplicative. Two agencies arranging work experience for the same student without speaking is what duplicated effort actually looks like in practice.

        Unclear role boundaries produce disjointed efforts, miscommunication, and missed opportunities.

        CTI, on why role clarity is a student outcome and not administrative tidiness

        There are two more bodies in this landscape that national materials will not prepare you for, because they have no Virginia equivalent. The SELPA can facilitate interagency agreements between school districts and outside agencies, and provides transition planning support across districts — which makes it the place to take a partnership problem that is bigger than one school. And SDCOE provides transition program support and professional development, and can broker regional employer partnerships that individual schools cannot develop alone. If your map came out thin on work-based learning, that second one is a lead.

        Coordination is not delegation. WIOA and IDEA create complementary mandates: WIOA requires VR–school collaboration, IDEA requires transition services in the IEP. Schools cannot offload IEP transition obligations onto VR — the two are additive, not substitutable. So finding out that DOR is delivering a category does not remove it from the IEP’s account. It tells you what the IEP’s contribution should be built around.

        Refer the wrong student

        April is the month the exit-year student is on everyone’s mind, which is exactly why April’s referral should go to somebody else. CTI is unambiguous: initiate referrals early — “not in the final year of high school when there is insufficient time to establish eligibility and services.” A referral started now for a student leaving in June has to fit eligibility, an assigned counselor, a plan, and actual services into the weeks that are left. A referral started now for a student with two years ahead of them has room to become something.

        And if they are on a 504 Plan, that one first

        “Potentially eligible” means no full VR eligibility determination is required — an IEP or a 504 Plan is sufficient. Students on 504 Plans sit outside the special education referral habits entirely: no case manager whose job description includes agency linkage, no annual meeting where an agency invitation is a required element. Nobody refers them because nobody’s process reaches them. In California that is a large group — significant numbers of students with learning disabilities, ADHD and anxiety are on 504 Plans rather than IEPs — and the wiki’s instruction is to make sure partner schools communicate this pathway to those students and families, who in most cases have never been told any of it applies to them. Week 2 builds the invitation →

        None of which abandons the student who is exiting. It moves them to the document that was built for it. For a student graduating with a regular diploma or aging out, the Summary of Performance is the federally mandated exit document, and it must be completed during the final year — the year that is too late to start a referral is the right year to write the summary the next system will read. Side by Side’s April issue, Hand It Over, is built on it.

        Try this week

        Send the link with the citation attached.

        One family, one message: dor.ca.gov/Home/StudentServices, and the sentence that the page leaves out — 9 CCR § 7026.5 extends services past the 22nd birthday for a student still in a special education program. Then ask the district office how they apply it, rather than telling the family you already know.

        Then pick the student who is not leaving. No DOR connection, not exiting this year, and if they are on a 504 Plan rather than an IEP, that one first. The exit-year student gets a Summary of Performance. This one gets the years.
