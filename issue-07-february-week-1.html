The two steps
everyone skips
are four and six.

The monthly named the seven-step job match in one paragraph. This is the walk-through — and the two steps that get cut are the two that decide whether the placement fits.

↓ Start reading
Following up on Issue 07 · This Month’s Focus 01 02 03

Most job development starts at step five. Somebody hears about an opening, a student is available, and the placement gets built backward from a vacancy that already existed.

The seven-step process is not arranged that way, and the two steps that get cut are the two doing the most work. Step four is where sites nobody advertised come from. Step six is where you find out whether the site you found is the site you thought it was.

01 This Week’s Focus

Seven steps,
and the order matters

The VCU/CTI job-match process is a team sequence built around the student’s SPIN rather than around what happens to be open. Read it once through before deciding which parts you already do.

  1. 1
    Know the student

    Gather information and observe across varied settings — strengths and conditions for success, not deficits.

  2. 2
    Involve family and others

    A team identifies task preferences and employment themes the student may not name alone.

  3. 3
    Engage the student

    Narrow interests directly with them and find out what actually motivates.

  4. 4
    Network

    Surface opportunities through school, colleagues, and family relationships.

  5. 5
    Approach businesses

    Select employers whose environment and tasks match the SPIN — and weigh employer attitudes.

  6. 6
    Conduct a job analysis

    Observe the site, confirm environmental fit, and identify natural supports and the instructional approach.

  7. 7
    Support student and business

    Individualized, ongoing support to both, after the placement starts.

Steps one through three build the person side, which is what SPIN is — strengths, preferences, interests, needs. Steps five through seven are the employer side. Step four is the hinge, and it is the one with no paperwork attached, which is most of why it disappears.

Step four is a list of people, not a search

Networking here does not mean a mixer. It means surfacing opportunities through relationships the team already has: school, colleagues, family. Every student arrives attached to a set of adults, and almost none of those adults have ever been asked a work question.

The list is also already half-built. The SPIN Interview has the student interview three to five people in their life about strengths, interests, and what they avoid. Those names were gathered as self-knowledge. They are, unchanged, a network — and the student chose them.

“Do you have an opening?” ends the conversation either way. “Who else do you know?” lengthens the list.

The difference between step four and step five

A referred site also starts ahead on the criterion nobody scores: employer attitude. An employer who was introduced by someone they trust begins the conversation already partly persuaded, which is not a courtesy — it is a named match criterion.

Ask where it is. Before a network lead becomes a favorite, ask for the address and check it against the routes the student rides today.

Interactive · step four, nine contacts

Work the list

One student, and the nine contacts already sitting around them. For each one, decide what you would actually say: ask about an opening, ask who else they know, or leave it out of this step. There is no score. The readout tells you what shape of network you just built.

Loading the nine contacts. With scripting off, here is the exercise on paper. Take each of these and decide whether you would ask about an opening, ask who else they know, or leave it out of step four: an aunt the student sees weekly who schedules patient transport at a dialysis center; a neighbor running a two-van mobile detailing business; the school nutrition manager two doors down; a paraeducator who worked with the student two years ago; the volunteer coordinator at the family’s congregation; the three people the student interviewed for their SPIN profile; a family friend who manages a self-storage facility; a regional recruiter who staffed a table at the fall career fair; and a map list of every business within two miles of campus. Two of the nine are not step-four contacts at all.

The network you built

Nine contacts, three moves

    Step four costs no paperwork and produces the sites no posting and no interest inventory ever names. It is also the step with nothing to submit, which is why it is the first one to fall off a busy February.

      The three moves come from step four’s own description — surfacing opportunities through school, colleagues, and family relationships. The contacts are composites. Nothing is stored and nothing leaves the page.

      Step six is a visit, not a phone call

      A job analysis is an hour on site, watching the work happen. It confirms environmental fit against the profile and identifies the natural supports already in the building. None of that can be read off a job description.

      It is also the step most often skipped, for an ordinary reason: by the time somebody says yes, the placement is late. Skipping it does not remove the work. It moves the work to week three, where it arrives as a student who stopped going.

      What the visit is looking for

      Noise level and sensory environment. Social demands — how many, how complex, how sudden. Supervision style, and whether it is the same person each shift. Schedule flexibility. Transportation access. And the thing with no row on most forms: who is already there — the coworker who would answer a question, the routine that exists already, the natural supports you build the fade around rather than replace. The monthly runs this as a grid →

      For students under 18, California requires a work permit before any paid placement starts. Schools issue it; employers keep it.

      What the match buys

      The documented payoff is not survival. It is that a good match increases engagement and reduces total job-site training time — the argument that works at a table where somebody is scheduling job-coach hours. Every dimension a site fails is a support somebody has to build, teach, and then fade.

      • Transportation access — a match criterion in its own right, and the one that most often ends a placement before anybody has evaluated the work.
      • Employer attitude and belief — named as a criterion, not just physical task compatibility. It decides whether a bad week gets a phone call or a termination.
      What we are not claiming. Whether match quality predicts long-term retention is understudied. Argue engagement and training time, which are documented.

      Try this week

      Ask one adult who else they know.

      Pick one student, and one adult already attached to them — a relative, a colleague, someone from the SPIN interview. Do not ask for a job. Ask who else they know who employs people.

      Write down every name you get. That list is step four, and it is the only part of the process that gets longer the more you use it.
