📊 This month’s focus

Data You’ll Actually Keep

The best data system is the one simple enough that you’ll still be doing it in April.

If you have ever stopped a student mid-task to jot a note — or sat down at 3:00 trying to remember what happened during second period — this one is for you.

Most paras collect data every day. The problem is almost never effort. It is that the system breaks under real conditions: it is too complicated, it pulls you out of the moment, or the behavior was never defined clearly enough for two people to count it the same way.

And here is why it matters more with 18-to-22-year-olds than it did in a classroom: your notes are often the only record of whether a student is actually gaining independence. Nobody else is standing where you are standing.

Good data has three traits

1 Specific

You are counting one clearly-defined thing — “started the task within 5 seconds” — not a general sense of “good day.” The test: could someone watching a video count the same thing you did?

2 Quick

A tally mark or a checkbox, not a paragraph. If it takes more than three seconds, you will not keep it up — not because you are careless, but because those three seconds come out of the student’s shift.

3 Consistent

Same measure, every time. Patchy data cannot show growth, and growth is the entire point. Three clean weeks beat two months of when-I-remembered.

One behavior at a time. Tracking four things badly is worth less than tracking one thing well.
👀 Picture this

One index card, one row of boxes

You are helping Jasmine, 21, bus tables at the café. Instead of narrating her whole shift from memory afterwards, you keep one index card in your pocket: a row of boxes labelled “started without a prompt.” Check it or leave it blank.

That is the entire system. By Friday you can see — not guess — whether she needed you less than she did last week.

A crooked tally mark now beats a tidy guess later. Every single time.

☀️ Practice spotlight

Three things to try tomorrow

✍ 1. Define the one thing you’re counting

Before the shift, finish this sentence: “Today I’m tracking whether ___.” Make it something you could see on video — “asked for help using words,” not “had a good attitude.”

☑ 2. Make it a checkbox, not a sentence

Index card, sticky note, or the notes app on your phone. A row of boxes you tick in real time beats a beautiful log you fill in from memory at the end of the day.

💬 3. Match the teacher’s words

Ask the teacher exactly how they define the skill you are tracking. If you both count “independent” the same way, your data adds up — and it will hold up in the IEP.

Worth knowing: the IEP team is required to build goals on real assessment data. What you write on that card is assessment data — often the most current there is.
⚠️ Watch out for

The Memory Trap

Data you write from memory isn’t data. It’s a feeling with numbers on it.

By the end of a full day, nobody can honestly reconstruct how many prompts a student needed at 9:15. Our brains smooth it out. A rough morning becomes “pretty good.” A great one gets forgotten entirely. And the smoothing is not random — it bends toward whatever you already believed about that student.

That is what makes it a trap rather than a mistake. Reconstructed data confirms. It almost never surprises you, which is the one thing good data is for.

The tell

If your numbers have never once contradicted your impression of a student, they are probably being written from the impression. Real data argues with you sometimes.

Catch it in the moment, even messily, or don’t count it at all. A blank on the card is honest. A number invented at 3:00 goes into an IEP and stays there.

One thing before your next shift

Make one card. One student. One behavior.

An index card, a student’s name, one clearly-defined behavior, and a row of empty boxes. Keep it in your pocket. Tick it in real time.

That is a working data system, and it took two minutes to build.
