You have the conversation from last week. Now the question is which published instrument, if any, is worth adding to it — and the answer depends far more on what you still don’t know than on which tool is best.

Before any of that: mine what already exists. Grades, attendance, prior IEPs, teacher observations, course-of-study data, and curriculum-based results from CTE classes are all assessment data you already own. Ordering an instrument before reading the file is how programs end up with a battery and no picture.

01 This Week’s Focus

Same center,
different animals

Both come out of the Zarrow Center at the University of Oklahoma. That is where the resemblance stops.

TAGG
Transition Assessment and Goal Generator

Covers interests and abilities, and produces draft goal language from the results. Carries a cost — modest, but it is not free, which is the detail that surprises people who arrived expecting a free download.

AIR
AIR Self-Determination Assessment

Measures self-determination, not interests. Free and reproducible. Developed by AIR with Teachers College, Columbia, and distributed through Zarrow.

If a colleague says “we should run the Zarrow thing,” the useful reply is: which one, and what are we trying to learn? Those two tools answer questions that have almost nothing to do with each other.

The thing that makes AIR unusually useful

Most self-determination measures return one score. AIR separates capacity — what the student knows and can do — from opportunity, meaning how often school and home actually let them do it. Those are scored independently, on the same profile.

Low capacity Teach the skill. Goal-setting, decision-making, self-advocacy instruction.
Low opportunity Change the environment. The student already has the skill and nobody is letting them use it.

That distinction changes what you write. A student with high capacity and low opportunity does not need a self-advocacy goal — they need adults to stop answering for them. No single-score instrument can tell you which of those two situations you are in.

Three forms: educator, student, and parent. Running more than one surfaces where the adults and the student disagree — which, as Week 1 argued, is the finding rather than the problem.

Also free, also worth knowing

The Arc’s Self-Determination Scale is a 72-item student self-report normed on adolescents with cognitive and developmental disabilities. Where AIR is strongest as a multi-rater picture, the Arc scale is a long, careful look from the student’s own seat.

    02 The Quick Tip, at length

    Read it out loud

    “Could this describe any of my students?” If yes, it is not a goal yet.

    The test takes eight seconds per goal and it is unforgiving. Generic goals pass every compliance check ever written — they are measurable, they are time-bound, they have a percentage in them — and they carry no information about the person. Three real examples, with what the profile does to them:

    Fails “Student will explore career interests and develop employability skills in a community setting with 80% accuracy.”
    Passes “Dani will complete a written opening routine at an animal-care worksite, working independently in a back-of-house area, on the same weekly schedule.”
    Fails “Student will increase self-advocacy skills as measured by teacher observation.”
    Passes “Dani will ask a supervisor for the task order at the start of each shift, in four of five shifts, without a prompt from staff.”
    Fails “Student will demonstrate appropriate workplace behavior.”
    Passes “When the work area gets loud, Dani will use the agreed break signal rather than leaving the site.”

    Notice what the passing versions have in common: each one names a condition that came out of the profile. The setting, the noise, the schedule, the written order. Those aren’t decorative details — they are the difference between a goal that can be worked on and a goal that can only be reported on.

    A goal that could belong to anyone will get worked on by no one.

    Including, eventually, the student
    One caution: specific is not the same as narrow. “Animal care” is specific; “kennel assistant at one named clinic” is a placement, not a goal, and it fails the moment that clinic stops hiring.

    Try this week

    Run the test on three goals.

    Take three draft goals off your desk — ideally not your own — and read each one out loud. Ask whether it could describe any student on the caseload.

    For every one that fails, the fix is a condition from the profile. That is the whole month, in one edit.
