Fix the worst one?
Probably not.

You have time for one rescue this week. The goal that most deserves it and the goal that most needs it are rarely the same file.

The monthly issue said to rescue one goal. This week is about which one — because the honest constraint is that you are not rewriting a caseload in October, and pretending otherwise is how none of them get done.

The instinct is to start with the worst-written goal. It is the wrong instinct, and the reason is arithmetic: a bad goal on a student with four years left costs you a semester. The same bad goal on a student who ages out in the spring costs the student everything that was still possible.

01 This Week’s Focus

The three questions,
used as a diagnostic

Where exactly, by when, and does a service back it. Each failure has its own signature.

  • Fails “where exactly” — the goal names a category, not a direction. “A job,” “college,” “independent living.” Nothing can be built from it, so nothing is.
  • Fails “by when” — usually the phrase “after high school,” which sounds like a timeframe and is not one. Unmeasurable by construction.
  • Fails “does a service back it” — the most revealing failure. The goal may be excellent. If nothing this year builds toward it, nobody ever intended to work on it.

A goal can fail all three, and plenty do. But the third failure is the one to weight most heavily, because the first two are writing problems and the third is a resourcing problem. You can fix a sentence in ten minutes. Getting a student into a work experience takes a term.

Then weight it all by how much time the student has left.

    02 Doing the rewrite

    Ten minutes, out loud

    Once you have picked the file, the rewrite itself is short. Say the sentence aloud in this order: by when, who, doing what, with whose support. If you stall on any of the four, that is the part you do not actually know yet — and the fix is to write what you are doing to find out, not to invent something plausible.

    Inventing “Within one year of exiting, Devon will live independently in an apartment.” Nobody has assessed this. It will be quietly dropped.
    Admitting “Within one year of exiting, Devon will live in a supported setting to be identified through Regional Center planning this year.” Honest, measurable, and it names the next action.

    A goal that says “we are finding out, here is how” is a real goal. A goal that asserts an outcome nobody has evidence for is a guess that will be treated as settled by the next three teams who read it.

    Then check the service. A rewrite that does not change what happens this year has changed nothing. Every rescued goal needs a matching activity added at the same sitting.

    Next IEP season

    Draft goals before the meeting, not during it. Goals written live, in a room, with a parent waiting and forty minutes on the clock, are where “a job he enjoys” comes from — nobody chooses that phrase on purpose.

    Try this week

    Sort your caseload by exit date.

    Not by name, not by case number. By how many months of eligibility each student has left. Then read the postsecondary goals of the top three.

    Rescue whichever of those three is weakest. That is the one where the ten minutes buys the most.
