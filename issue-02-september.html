Most plans fail
before they’re written.
Here’s why.

They get built on assumptions. This month: the four questions that put a real person underneath the paperwork.

↓ Start reading

A plan built on assumptions looks exactly like a plan built on evidence. Same template, same headings, same signatures. You find out which one you have about ninety days into a job placement, when it comes apart and nobody can say why.

The assumptions are rarely careless. They’re what the teacher believes the student wants, what the family hopes for, what fits neatly in the box. Every one of them is plausible. That’s the problem — plausible is not the same as true, and the plan can’t tell the difference.

So this issue is about the step before the plan: actually knowing who the student is. Not who they were at fourteen. Who is sitting in front of you now.

01 This Month’s Focus

SPIN: four questions
every plan has to answer.

Strengths. Preferences. Interests. Needs. Not a form — a way of seeing the whole person before you write a single goal.

IDEA doesn’t ask for a vibe. It requires that transition planning rest on age-appropriate transition assessment of a student’s strengths, preferences, interests and needs. SPIN is the plain-language version of that requirement — and the way most of the field actually does it.

34 CFR 300.320(b). Transition goals must be “based upon age-appropriate transition assessment.” Ongoing, not a one-time event.
S
Strengths

What can this student do well? What do they bring to a job or a team?

P
Preferences

How do they like to work? Indoors or out, people or tasks, routine or variety?

I
Interests

What do they care about, spend time on, want to learn more about?

N
Needs

What supports, accommodations, or skills do they need to succeed?

Preferences and interests are not the same thing

This is the pair people collapse, and collapsing them is how you end up with a bad match. Interests are the subject: animals, cars, music, food. Preferences are the conditions: alone or with people, quiet or loud, same thing every day or something different.

A student can love animals and hate the shelter — not because the animals were wrong, but because it was loud, crowded, and never the same task twice. The interest was right. The preferences were never asked about. That job ends at week six and goes in the file as a student who “wasn’t ready for work.”

Interests get a student in the door. Preferences are what keep them there past ninety days.

And ninety days is the number the state counts

Don’t trust the file

For students nearing 22, the SPIN in the file may have been gathered when they were fourteen. Interests mature. Strengths emerge that nobody had seen yet. A student who once said “I want to work with animals” may now light up on a warehouse floor and find the shelter unbearable.

Reassessment isn’t a sign the first assessment failed. The CTI framework treats it as expected: as students do more, they learn more about themselves, and their interests change. That’s the process working. The SPIN you gather this fall should describe the adult in front of you.

One ordering rule

Write the employment goal first. Education and training goals get built on top of it, not developed alongside it — every career carries its own training requirements, and you can’t choose the training until you know what it’s for. Teams that write all three in parallel usually end up with three goals that don’t reference each other.

Four boxes is not a person. A real profile has several answers per letter and contradicts itself in places — students want people around and get overwhelmed by them, on different days. The point of reducing it here is to show how far a single answer travels into the plan, not to suggest one answer is enough.

Without a profile

Upon completion of high school, the student will explore career interests and develop employability skills in a community work setting with 80% accuracy.

Could describe any student on your caseload. That is the test it fails.

    02 Practice Spotlight

    The 15-Minute SPIN Conversation

    Talk to the student first. Not the file, not the parent — the student.

    Sit down with one student and ask. Keep it plain and keep it real; a question that sounds like a form gets answered like a form. Four questions, one per letter:

    1. S
      “What’s something you’re actually good at — that people count on you for?”

      The second half of that question is what makes it work. It asks for evidence, not modesty.

    2. P
      “Would you rather work with your hands, with people, or on your own?”

      Concrete options beat an open question here. Most students have never been asked and need something to push against.

    3. I
      “When you have free time, where does it go?”

      Not “what are your career interests.” Where the time goes is the honest answer, and it’s the one they can give.

    4. N
      “What makes a hard day hard for you — and what helps?”

      Needs asked as deficits get denied. Needs asked as conditions get described in detail.

    Write their words down verbatim. Not your summary of their words. “I don’t like when it’s loud and everybody’s talking at me” is usable in a job-site visit. “Student reports sensory sensitivities” is not.

    Then write it down properly. In the PLAAFP, record three things for every tool or conversation: the name, the date, and the information gained.

    The student is the first informant, not the only one. Families see chores and responsibilities you never will. Paras see the job site. CTE teachers hold curriculum-based data most IEP teams never open. Employers, counselors, related service providers — all of them hold a piece of the profile.

    03 Resource of the Month
    • Free
    • ou.edu/education
    • University of Oklahoma

    The Zarrow Center — assessments you can use on Monday

    The Zarrow Center for Learning Enrichment publishes transition assessments that are actually usable by a teacher rather than a psychometrician: interest inventories, preference tools, and self-determination measures you can download and run without buying anything.

    Start with an interest inventory the student fills out themselves. Then talk through it together — the conversation about the results is worth more than the results.

    One honest caveat: not everything there is free, and the two best-known tools are different animals. Week 3 sorts out which is which.

    Do this month

    Read your draft goals out loud.

    Before your next IEP, read the draft goals aloud and ask one question: “Could this describe any of my students?” If yes, it’s too generic.

    A real SPIN profile makes the goal sound like one specific person — because it is.
