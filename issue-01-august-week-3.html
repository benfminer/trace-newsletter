We pointed you at transitionta.org this month and said “check here when you’re not sure a strategy is worth your time.” Fair advice, and useless without a map.

The site holds two different tools that people constantly mix up, and one caveat that changes how you should read all of it.

01 This Week’s Focus

Two tools that answer
different questions

TOOL
ONE
The Taxonomy 2.0 — “does our program even cover this?”

A program-level map with five areas: student-focused planning, student development, interagency collaboration, family engagement, and program structure. You use it to find holes, not to pick strategies. Run your program against the five and the gaps tend to be obvious and uncomfortable.

TOOL
TWO
Predictors & practices — “does this specific thing work?”

A sorted list of in-school experiences that correlate with better outcomes after exit, each rated evidence-based, research-based, or promising depending on how much sound research backs it.

The Taxonomy tells you family engagement is thin in your program. The predictor list tells you what to put there that has evidence behind it.

Used in that order, they are genuinely powerful
02 Read the Ratings Honestly

The caveat that matters

These predictors are correlational, not causal. “Evidence-based” here means several sound studies found students who had this experience did better afterward. It does not mean the experience caused the outcome, and it tells you nothing about how much, how long, or how to deliver it.

That is not a reason to ignore it. It is a reason to stop treating the list as a guarantee and start treating it as the best available bet.

The same honesty runs the other way. The evidence base has real holes — and if you go looking for proof behind something you know your students need and come up empty, that is often a gap in the research, not a verdict on the practice.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. It usually means nobody has run the study yet — often because the outcome is hard to measure, not because the practice is weak.

03 One Practice, Pulled Out

Twenty-three predictors.
One at the top.

Of the twenty-three predictors in the current review, exactly one carries the top evidence-based rating.

Evidence-based · the only one

Career and Technical Education

CTE coursework predicts both employment and postsecondary education. Everything else on the list — paid work experience, inclusion in general education, self-determination instruction, interagency collaboration, parent expectations — sits a tier below at research-based or promising.

Worth pairing with what we covered last week: paid work experience is only research-based, one tier down — but it is the only predictor that reaches all three outcome areas at once, education and employment and independent living, and it carries the largest single effect in the employment literature.

So the defensible fall bet for TRACE is not exotic. It is CTE access plus a paid placement.

The evidence base, unglamorous and clear
04 Closing the Month

Three weeks ago the monthly issue closed with one assignment: at your first team meeting, ask out loud “where did last year’s leavers actually end up?”

If you have asked it, you now know how to read the answer. If you haven’t, this is the week — September fills up fast, and you cannot move a number you have never seen.

Try this week

Look up your biggest line item.

Name the one thing your program spends the most staff hours on. Find its rating on transitionta.org.

  • If it is evidence- or research-based, you just earned the right to defend that schedule in your next budget conversation.
  • If it isn’t on the list at all, that is worth ten minutes of honest thinking before the year gets away from you.
