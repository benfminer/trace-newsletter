Working,
and still
below the bar.

The monthly established the direction of the alarm: 14A, 14B and 14C are nested counts, so a high 14C is good and a low one is the warning. This week goes into the space between two of those counts — the students who are inside 14C and outside 14B, what the definitions do to them, and how much the number can actually see.

Subtract your 14B from your 14C. Whatever is left is a group of real people with something in common, and the reported number tells you exactly one thing about them: how many there are. Everything else about that band — who is in it, why, and whether any of it is movable — has to come from somewhere other than the percentage.

That subtraction is the whole subject of this week. It is not a data-quality footnote. It is the group closest to an outcome your program is already trying to produce, and it is the group most likely to be described in a report as a success and in a family’s kitchen as a holding pattern. Both descriptions can be accurate at once, which is what makes the band worth an hour of attention before the building empties.

01 This Week’s Focus

The rung
below the bar

The category has a name and a definition, and both are more permissive than almost anyone expects. It is called other employment, it lives inside 14C and nowhere else, and its entry requirements are short enough to read aloud.

  • Worked for pay, or was self-employed. Not volunteered. Not job shadowed. Paid.
  • For at least 90 days, at any time in the year since leaving high school. Any time in the year — not a snapshot of what they are doing the week the phone rings.
  • Family businesses and entrepreneurships count. Working in a parent’s shop counts. So does self-employment at market rates.
  • There is no minimum hours-per-week requirement at all. This is the difference between this rung and the one above it, and it is a whole rung’s worth of difference.
  • Below-minimum-wage sheltered employment does not count. Not here either. A student in a sheltered setting earning subminimum wages is not in other employment and is not in competitive employment; on the employment side of the survey they are not counted at all.

So the rung captures youth who are working but have not met the competitive employment threshold — typically part-time, seasonal, or gig workers. It keeps them counted in 14C while signaling that they have not reached the bar that drives 14B. That second half of the sentence is the part that gets lost when a program reports one rate: the category was designed to carry a signal, and reporting only 14C throws the signal away.

Two students can both be counted in 14C and be in completely different situations. A single percentage cannot tell them apart. The band between 14C and 14B can.

Which is why the subtraction is worth doing before the meeting rather than in it

Worth saying plainly what this band is not. It is not a failure category and it is not a waiting room. Paid work experience is the strongest single predictor in this literature — students who hold paid work before exit are roughly 5.1 times more likely to be employed afterward — and a student in other employment is, by definition, a student holding paid work. The honest read is that they have cleared the hardest part and are short of a threshold, which is a much more actionable sentence than a percentage.

Three parts, and they fail independently

The competitive employment bar is not one condition. It is three, all of which must be true, and a student can miss on any one of them while clearing the other two comfortably. That matters because each of the three failures is a different problem with a different owner, and a program that knows only “they are in 14C, not 14B” cannot tell which of the three it is looking at.

  1. 1
    Ninety cumulative days — a calendar problem

    At least 90 days of employment, and they do not have to be consecutive. Two separate summers can add up. A student who misses here usually did not miss by capability; they started late, or the job ended early, or nobody was tracking it as a countable thing. This is the failure most sensitive to when a placement began, which makes it the one a school still has some grip on before exit.

  2. 2
    An average of twenty hours a week — a schedule problem

    Twenty-plus hours, defined as an average of twenty across those 90 cumulative days. This is the condition the other-employment band is mostly made of, and it is the one that turns on a conversation with an employer rather than on anything about the student. Averaging matters in both directions: a run of thirty-hour weeks can carry a run of twelve-hour ones.

  3. 3
    At or above minimum wage — not a schedule problem at all

    Paid at or above minimum wage. Sheltered employment and subminimum wage work do not count; supported employment in an integrated setting does. This is the failure that is categorically unlike the other two, because it is not a dial anybody turns on an existing placement. It is a statement about what kind of placement it is.

The one that is a placement decision

Hours and days can move inside a job. Wage-and-setting usually cannot — a subminimum-wage sheltered placement does not become competitive employment by adding hours, and adding hours to it moves no rate on the board at all. If the miss is on condition three, the conversation is about where the student works, not about how much. California has already taken a position on this: Assembly Bill 1041 (2013) prohibited new subminimum wage certificates under Section 14(c) of the Fair Labor Standards Act for Regional Center-funded programs, years ahead of most states.

The wage bar is a state number, not a national one. The federal definition says “at or above minimum wage” and leaves the figure to wherever the student works. The wiki records Virginia’s as $12.41 an hour as of January 1, 2025, and California’s minimum wage as $16 an hour as of 2024. Same federal condition, meaningfully different floor — which is one reason cross-state comparisons of 14B deserve more caution than they usually get.

One genuine hole, named rather than papered over. Self-employment at market rates is listed under other employment; the competitive employment definition is written around being paid at or above minimum wage for 90 days at an average of twenty hours. What the wiki does not settle is where a self-employed young person working thirty hours a week at market rates is supposed to land. If you have a student like that, the answer is a question for whoever administers the survey, not something to infer from the definitions.

The band is not one thing

Here is the second reason the gap between 14C and 14B resists a single interpretation: two different categories live in it. Other employment is one. The other is other postsecondary education or training, which is a large and loosely bounded list — adult and continuing education, high school completion programs including ABE and GED, apprenticeships, short-term WIOA training, Job Corps, vocational and technical schools under two years, AmeriCorps, Peace Corps, VISTA, day support and prevocational programs, compensatory education.

And the threshold for that list is deliberately generous. Completion of a semester-equivalent is defined broadly: any formal program with an application or approval process that is at least in part skill-building, even if short-term — a ten-week welding class qualifies. Which means the same gap between two percentages can contain a young person who spent the year in a paid seasonal job and a young person who spent ten weeks in a class, and the report will not distinguish them.

26% the complement, given a number Virginia’s Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission reported in December 2020 that in 2018, 26 percent of individuals with disabilities did not transition into employment, higher education, or vocational training within one year of exiting secondary school. That is the group outside 14C entirely, in one state, in one year — the thing a low 14C is measuring the complement of. It is a Virginia figure and not a California one; the wiki holds no comparable local number, which is itself worth knowing before anybody quotes a percentage in a meeting.

Nor is the band evenly distributed. Virginia’s Indicator 14 data has historically been used to identify disparities by disability category and by exit type, and the pattern is consistent: students with intellectual disabilities, and students who dropped out, have significantly lower 14A and 14B outcomes than graduates with learning disabilities or other health impairments. Both those groups are still counted in 14C at higher rates than they are in 14B, which is precisely what it looks like when a population is concentrated on the rung below the bar. A single program-wide percentage will hide that. A disaggregated one will not.

Why the Virginia numbers keep appearing. Most of the outcome data this vault holds is Virginia’s, because Virginia’s technical assistance center publishes it. The definitions are federal and identical in California; the survey methodology and the 14A/14B/14C thresholds do not change at the state line. What changes is who administers it — in California, the Department of Education’s Special Education Division rather than a university center — and what the local numbers actually are, which this vault does not hold for San Diego. TRACE students, if they are tracked after exit, appear in their district’s Indicator 14 data.

Interactive · one leaver, three facts, one phone call

Who answered the phone

Every rung on the ladder is decided by three facts about a job: how many days, how many hours a week, and how it is paid. None of those three are observed. They are reported, a year after the student left, by whoever picked up. Set what is actually true, then set who answered — and the panel says which of the three facts that person is positioned to report firsthand, which they would be estimating, and which direction the record can drift. There is no score and nothing is being assessed; the point is which facts are load-bearing and how firm the ground under them is.

Before you set anything

What is grounded here and what is judgment

The rung arithmetic is federal and exact: 90 cumulative days, an average of twenty hours a week, at or above minimum wage, sheltered and subminimum work excluded, no hours minimum for other employment. The survey procedure — phone contact, a minimum of four contact attempts, and a respondent who may be the former student, a parent or guardian, a relative, teacher or school staff, or an adult service provider — is the documented Virginia process; the outcome definitions are the same everywhere, the administration is not.

Who plausibly knows what is our reading, not a finding. Use a real student in your head, not on the page — the site is world-readable. Nothing here is stored and nothing is sent.

Loading the settings. With scripting off, here is the exercise on paper. Take one leaver and fix three facts: how many days of paid work in the year since exit (90 or more, fewer than 90, or none at all), the average hours a week (under 10, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, or 30 and up), and how the work is paid (at or above minimum wage in an integrated setting, subminimum wage in a sheltered setting, or self-employed at market rates). Those three decide the rung: 90 days plus an average of twenty hours plus minimum wage is competitive employment and lifts 14B; 90 days without the hours is other employment and sits in 14C alone; under 90 days is neither; subminimum sheltered work is counted on neither rung. Now ask who would answer the phone a year from now, and for each of the three facts ask whether that person would know it, estimate it, or be guessing. The two facts a proxy respondent is least able to give precisely are hours and wage — which are exactly the two that separate 14B from 14C.

    Three facts and a phone call. The rung is arithmetic; the inputs to the arithmetic are somebody’s recollection. Set the facts first, then set the respondent.

      The competitive employment threshold, the other-employment rules, the exclusion of sheltered and subminimum work, and the higher-education definition are the federal survey definitions as recorded in the wiki. The phone procedure, the four-contact-attempt minimum, and the list of who the survey may be conducted with are the documented Virginia administration. Non-response bias, proxy respondents with incomplete information about a student’s actual employment status, and the arbitrariness of the one-year window are the wiki’s own stated limitations. Which respondent knows which fact is our inference from those limitations and is labeled as such throughout.       02 What the number cannot do

      Four limits,
      said out loud

      None of these are reasons to discount the data. Indicator 14 remains the most consistently collected national dataset on post-school outcomes for youth with disabilities, and it is the primary source for state and national comparison. They are reasons to state the number with its conditions attached, especially when the number in question is the distance between two rates.

      1. 1
        Contact rates are often below 100 percent

        Which creates potential non-response bias. The students you could not reach are missing from the answer, and there is no particular reason to think they went missing at random. In a program small enough that the band between 14C and 14B is a dozen people, a handful of unreached leavers is not a rounding error.

      2. 2
        Respondents may not be the student

        The survey may include family members who have incomplete information about the student’s actual employment status. That is the wiki’s own language, and it lands hardest exactly on the 14B/14C boundary, because the boundary is set by average weekly hours — the single fact a proxy respondent is least likely to hold precisely.

      3. 3
        One year is an arbitrary window

        Outcomes at year one may not reflect stability at year three or five. A student in other employment in year one may be competitively employed in year two and uncounted forever, because nobody asks again. The survey is also a rearview mirror in a second sense: these students left a year ago, so schools learn about their outcomes one to two years after the students they served were in their programs.

      4. 4
        The competitive threshold is a floor, not a goal

        Ninety days, twenty hours, minimum wage — it captures very part-time minimum-wage work as a success. Which cuts both ways. It means 14B is easier to clear than its name suggests, and it means a student sitting just under it is genuinely close. It also means nobody should mistake a rising 14B for a rising standard of living.

      The questions that are already being asked

      Here is the part most programs never use. The survey collects considerably more than the three outcome categories, and none of the extra material enters the percentage. It records which agencies students were referred to during high school; which school programs they participated in — work-based learning, Pre-ETS, Project SEARCH, supported employment, self-determination programs, dual enrollment; which post-school agency services they actually received, from the same list; barriers to employment or postsecondary education, asked of those with neither after exit; which high school classes they found helpful; and life satisfaction, on a five-point scale.

      Why that list belongs in this week

      The percentage tells you how many people are in the band. The secondary data is the only part of the instrument that could tell you why — which referrals were made, which services arrived, what the students themselves named as the obstacle. It is collected on your leavers and it is not in the number you are shown. Ask for it. The worst answer is that it is not available at your level, which is worth knowing too. Week 2 puts a team around it →

      The life satisfaction item deserves a sentence on its own, because it is the only place in the whole apparatus where the young person is asked how it is going rather than what they are doing. It does not enter 14A, 14B or 14C. It has no target attached and no state is graded on it. It is also, plausibly, the item a family would most want to know the answer to. Keep both of those facts in view at once and you have a fair picture of what an accountability indicator is: useful, narrow, and not the same thing as a life.

      Try this week

      Count the band. Then name the missing fact.

      Take the leavers you can actually account for and sort them: competitively employed, working below the bar, in some other training, in higher education, or in none of it. The middle two are your band. Write down how many.

      Then, for each student in the working-below-the-bar group, name which one of the three conditions they miss — days, hours, or wage-and-setting. Days is a start-date problem. Hours is a conversation with an employer. Wage-and-setting is a placement decision, and it is the only one of the three that will not move by asking nicely.

      Do it before the last week of school, while the people who know the answers are still in the building. A year from now the person answering the phone about these students may not be any of you.
