A family asks you what happens to their son’s benefits if he takes the fifteen-hour job. You answer, because you are the person standing there. And that is roughly where the trouble starts — not because the answer was wrong, but because you were the only place it could come from.

1977 the year the parent center network started operating Parent Training and Information Centers are federally designated under IDEA and funded by OSEP. PACER, the national center this vault documents most fully, has been running since 1977, is free, and has its materials reviewed for legal accuracy. This is not a new thing you are asking a family to try.
01 This Week’s Focus

Somebody who is
not at the table

San Diego County’s PTI is Exceptional Family Center. The monthly named it on purpose. “Look up your regional parent center” is a homework assignment handed to the family with the least time; a name and a website is a referral. exceptionallives.org/san-diego →

What a PTI does is narrower and more useful than “support.” It helps a family understand the system — what an IEP has to contain, what changes at 18, how adult services eligibility works, what a district saying no actually means. Free, and delivered by somebody who is not sitting on the other side of the table from them. That independence is the whole point: the person explaining your rights should not also be the person you might need to use them against.

Where the law already names them

IDEA 2004 requires every IEP team to consider assistive technology at every IEP — genuine deliberation, not a checkbox. And when the team lacks the expertise to do that, the requirement is that external resources must be brought in, with a Parent Training and Information Center named explicitly alongside an AT specialist and a Technology Access Center. So the PTI is not a nice-to-have you mention if the meeting goes long. It is one of the answers to a question your team will be asked this spring.

Nationally, PACER Center is the fully documented example: OSEP-funded under IDEA’s Parent Training and Information program, free plain-language guides on IEP transition planning, the age of majority, and supported decision-making. Its direct services are for Minnesota families; the publications are free to anyone. Use PACER for the reading, and use Exceptional Family Center for the phone call.

Naming beats listing. A referral a family can act on this week has a name attached to it. Anything that requires them to first find the right list, then find themselves on it, is a task — and tasks are what families with the least capacity drop first.

Interactive · six questions, one call each

Route the question

Six things families actually ask, and the only question that matters for each one: who owns the answer? There is no score. Several of these have more than one defensible route, and each call gets an argument back — including the ones this analysis would not have made.

With scripting off, here are the six questions to route on paper, and the five places an answer can come from: the school or IEP team, Exceptional Family Center (San Diego’s parent center), the Department of Rehabilitation, San Diego Regional Center, and the Social Security Administration. One: “Nobody on this team knows what technology would help her at a job site.” Two: “If he works fifteen hours a week, does he lose his check?” Three: “What happens to my say in this at 18?” Four: “Who pays for a job coach after he leaves you?” Five: “The district said no. What do we do now?” Six: “We were handed the paperwork in English again.” For each one, ask yourself whether you would be answering it because you know, or because you are the person standing there.

Six questions, no answer key

        The six questions are composites. The routes are grounded in what each body is actually responsible for — the AT requirement is IDEA 2004, the parent center is federally designated under IDEA, and the benefits figures belong to SSA and to nobody else in the room. The exercise is not about knowing an org chart. It is about noticing how often the honest answer is “not me, and here is who.”

        The referral that is not a referral

        A phone number in a language a family does not use is not access. The standard is already written down: in the collaborative teaming guidance, every team member must be invited with transition information in their preferred language and format. Not translated on request at the door — invited that way.

        The vault’s sharpest illustration is a published vignette, not a case from anybody’s caseload. In Greene’s 2011 account of a 16-year-old Latina student’s IEP, the team focused on community fast-food work and independence. The mother’s stated vision — part-time work in the family dress shop, living at home, helping with child care — was dismissed and never appeared in the document. The meeting ended with a parent signing before a Spanish translation had been prepared. The professional assumptions won, and nobody in the room had to be unkind for that to happen.

        African American students with disabilities are 48% less likely to be employed up to eight years after graduation than white peers with disabilities. For Hispanic students, 53.6% less likely.

        NLTS2, on what these meetings add up to
        Where our own guidance stops. This vault does not hold San Diego-specific Spanish-language family engagement material, and language access in IEP processes is a documented equity gap. Spanish-speaking families are the county’s largest non-English-speaking group and the practices for engaging them are not well documented here. Handing over an English-language referral and calling it done is the failure mode; say the gap out loud rather than papering it.

        Ask before you present

        March’s tip is one sentence and it goes first, before the folder opens: “What do you hope for after this program?” Then let the answer decide what the next fifty minutes are spent on. The reason it has to come first is structural. Asked after the agenda has run, it invites a family to react to your framing. Asked first, it is the framing.

        It also changes what the referral is for. A family who has said out loud that they want their son working part-time and living at home has just told you which questions are coming, and most of them are not yours:

        • Two buckets — the family-facing way to explain earnings and SSI. For roughly every $2 that goes into the wages bucket, about $1 comes out of the SSI bucket — and the student always ends up with more total money than they started with. That is the shape of it; the figures are SSA’s.
        • Re-apply at 18 — families denied SSI for their child because parental income was too high should apply again once the student turns 18. At 18 SSA stops counting parental income and resources entirely, and students who were ineligible as minors often qualify under adult rules.
        • Start the adult-services conversation early — learning about adult services early reduces waitlists. That is a reason to raise regional center and DOR in March rather than in the exit IEP, and it is a reason a family gives themselves once they have said what they are aiming at.

        None of those three is a thing you should be the sole source on. All three are things a family will ask the person in front of them. The parent center is what turns “I don’t know” into a sentence with a destination in it. Week 2 is where the asking gets scheduled →

        Try this week

        Ask first. Then give them a name.

        At your next meeting, before anybody reads anything: “What do you hope for after this program?” Write down what they say in their words, and spend the rest of the hour on that.

        Then hand over Exceptional Family Center before you are asked for it. Every family this spring, not only the ones who push. A referral offered is a resource; a referral extracted is a concession.
