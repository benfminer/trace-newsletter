“I want to be
a chef” is not
a career plan.
It’s the first draft.

Career planning is a loop with a documented shape — gather, test it against a real workplace, revise. This month: what turns the loop into a placement that fits.

↓ Start reading

A student wrote “chef” on an interest inventory in ninth grade. It is still the postsecondary employment goal in the file. Nobody has asked since, and the student has never worked a kitchen shift.

That is not a career plan. It is a first draft that never got a second pass. The student who has tried three work experiences and can tell you which one felt wrong and why has done more planning than the file will ever show — and the difference between the two is not effort or insight. It is whether anybody wrote the revisions down.

4 questions, asked again every year The Lifelong Career Journey frames career assessment as a cycle rather than a destination: where do you want to go, what do you want to do, how do you get what you want, how do you move ahead. The fourth question returns you to the first. There is no last time through it.
Iterative is a requirement, not a philosophy. IDEA requires age-appropriate transition assessment to be ongoing rather than a one-time event (34 CFR 300.320(b)). A goal carried forward untouched for four years is a compliance problem before it is a planning problem.
01 This Month’s Focus

Gather, test it,
revise, repeat

The loop has a name and four quadrants. The Lifelong Career Journey, used in NTACT:C’s 2024 student supplement and adapted from Sitlington and colleagues (2007), is the version worth teaching — because a student can hold four questions in their head, and cannot hold a process diagram.

  1. 1
    Where do you want to go?

    Awareness and aspiration. The chef answer belongs here, and it is a perfectly good answer for this quadrant. It is only a problem when it is asked to do the work of all four.

  2. 2
    What do you want to do?

    Exploration of actual options — clusters, pathways, workplace tours, informational interviews, job shadows. This is where a first draft either survives contact with a real workplace or does not.

  3. 3
    How do you get what you want?

    Preparation and skill-building. Course of study, work-based learning, the supports that have to be in place before anyone is placed anywhere.

  4. 4
    How do you move ahead?

    Advancement and adjustment — and the quadrant that hands the student back to question one with better information than they had the first time.

Which is why a job shadow that flops is not a wasted Tuesday. When a student’s interests shift after an experience, the VCU Center on Transition Innovations is explicit about how to read it: this is a natural part of the transition assessment process, not a failure. The team’s job at that point is to identify the next experience, not to talk the student back into the old goal.

A natural part of the transition assessment process, not a failure.

Akom, Cage, Gokita, Holland & St. John — The Five Steps to Transition Assessment, Step 5

So the loop needs two things to keep turning. It needs a picture of the person that gets sharper each pass, and it needs a way to test that picture against a specific workplace. SPIN is the first of those — the person side, and only the person side.

  • Strengths — things the student does well, capabilities to build on.
  • Preferences — specific likes and dislikes; what they want more or less of in work and life.
  • Interests — activities, topics, and experiences that engage them, in or out of school.
  • Needs — the supports required to succeed, and the half of the profile that makes self-advocacy with an employer possible.

A SPIN profile is not a match, though. It is one side of one. The second thing the loop needs is the seven-step job-match process, which is what carries a profile out of the binder and into a building: know the student · involve family and others · engage the student · network · approach businesses · conduct a job analysis · support student and business. Steps one through three are the profile. Step five is where employer attitude gets weighed. Step six is the site visit where you find out whether any of it holds — and it is the step most often skipped when a position is open and the placement is due.

What a good match buys you

Not survival. The documented payoff is that a good match increases engagement and reduces total job-site training time — which is a budget line, not a sentiment. Two criteria earn their place here and rarely make the list: employer attitude and belief, which the guidance names explicitly as a match criterion rather than physical task compatibility alone, and transportation access, which is the row that most often ends a placement before anyone has evaluated the work. Week 1 works the seven steps →

Match also sits upstream of January. If placements end far more often over social and behavioral friction than over task performance, then a site whose social demands were never checked against the profile is not a soft-skills problem waiting to happen — it is a match problem that will present itself as one.

What we are not claiming. Whether match quality predicts long-term retention is understudied. The strong predictor in this vault is a paid job at exit, and that counts any job — it does not measure how well it fit. Argue match on engagement and training time, which is documented, rather than on retention, which is not.

Interactive · step six, run three times

Run the job analysis

One profile that already exists, three candidate placements, seven match dimensions. Row by row, decide whether the site fits as it is, fits with support somebody has to build, or does not fit at all. There is no score. The output is what each placement would cost you.

Loading the three placements. With scripting off, the seven match dimensions are: noise level, sensory environment, social demands, supervision style, schedule flexibility, transportation access, and employer attitude. The candidates are an overnight grocery restock (10pm–6am), a veterinary clinic kennel and laundry role (three mornings), and a garden center nursery job (Wednesday to Saturday mornings). The exercise is to walk each one against the profile above and decide, dimension by dimension, whether it fits as-is, needs a support built, or does not fit.

        02 Practice Spotlight

        Debrief every
        work experience

        This one is ours, not a cited framework. Three questions, asked in the van on the way back, before the memory sands down into “it was fine.”

        1. 1
          “What part of today would you do again?”

          Strengths and interests, arriving as a specific moment rather than a category. “Putting the boxes in order” is worth more to a job analysis than “I like retail.”

        2. 2
          “What part would you skip if you could?”

          Preferences and needs, and the more useful of the two questions. This is where the noise, the supervisor who leaned over their shoulder, and the twenty minutes at the front counter come out.

        3. 3
          “What does this tell us about the next thing to try?”

          The revision. It hands the loop back to the student and keeps the answer pointed forward, which matters most on the days the experience did not go well.

        Ask them in the same order every time and students start answering the second question before you get to it. That is the point — the routine is doing the work, not the wording.

        The upgrade that makes it count

        A debrief log is a nice artifact. It becomes evidence when it is written the way the assessment guidance asks: for every tool used, record the assessment name, the date given, and the information gained, and summarize it in the PLAAFP. Three fields. A job shadow at the animal hospital on February 11 that produced “will not tolerate unpredictable noise” is age-appropriate transition assessment, and Indicator 13 is audited on the IEP document rather than on how well you remember the year. Without those three fields it is a story you told at a meeting. Week 2 builds the log →

        Vary the instrument. The same guidance warns against running the same tool year over year. Three questions in a van are informal assessment and they are genuinely valuable — but they should be sitting alongside something else by spring, not standing in for the whole battery.

        My Next Move

        My Next Move appears in NTACT:C’s directory of sample transition assessment tools, under career interests and exploration — a free, plain-language site a student can work through with an adult beside them. Its sibling, O*NET, is named in the same directory and sits in Tier 1 of the transition assessment decision tree, alongside interest inventories and career cluster surveys.

        Run it next to the debrief log, not instead of it. mynextmove.org →

        Tier 1 is a starting place and a real one. It is also a ceiling: the decision tree exists because standard inventories do not surface a career interest for every student. Tier 2 is functional vocational evaluation — situational assessment, job sampling, community-based work experience. Tier 3 is person-centered planning and Discovering ME!, reached when Tiers 1 and 2 have not uncovered a matching interest. A student who cannot answer an inventory has not failed the inventory. They have told you which tier to work in.

        Two hours on a website is not a plan. The output of a Tier 1 tool is a list of things to go try, which is a step-two answer. Everything after that comes from an actual workplace and a debrief on the way home. Week 3 takes the tiers apart →

        Quick tip for February

        Offer the next experiment.

        When a student says “I don’t know what I want to do,” do not push for an answer. Name one thing they could try in the next three weeks, and put a date on it.

        Certainty comes from trying, not from thinking harder — and “I don’t know” is a perfectly good answer to question one when questions two through four are still running.
