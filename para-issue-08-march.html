🚌 This month’s focus

Getting Somewhere Without You

Travel training is short, intensive, one-to-one instruction that teaches one person one specific trip — this door to that door and back — until they can do it alone. It is taught on the actual bus, at the actual stop, on the actual corner. Not in a classroom with a laminated map.

Spring is the practical window for it. Routes are running normal schedules, the light is good at both ends of the day, and IEP season is close enough that progress made now turns into documented progress rather than a story somebody remembers in May.

One piece of vocabulary before anything else, because it matters the moment you talk to a transit agency or help write a goal. Travel training is not orientation and mobility. O&M is the credentialed discipline for travelers who are blind or have low vision, delivered by an O&M specialist. Travel training is the broader service, most often for students with intellectual and developmental disabilities, autism, or learning disabilities. The two words are not interchangeable and using them that way makes you sound like you are asking for something else.

The claim to get right

You will see it written — we have written it ourselves — that independent travel is one of the strongest predictors of post-school employment. It is not, and the evidence table says so in plain sight. It is worth two minutes to get this straight, because the version people usually repeat is the one that falls apart in front of somebody who has read the review.

What the research actually says

In the current predictor research — Mazzotti and colleagues’ 2021 review — travel skills sits at “Promising,” the weakest of three tiers, and for the employment outcome only. It entered the research base in 2016 on the strength of one exploratory study, and the 2021 update found nothing new to add to it. No effect size is reported. For scale: in the same review, career and technical education is the only predictor rated evidence-based.

And every predictor in that list is correlational. They describe what tends to travel alongside employment. None of them establishes that doing the thing causes the outcome.

So the honest sentence, which is still worth saying out loud: travel skills is a recognized predictor of post-school employment, at the promising level of evidence. Real, current, and pointed at employment specifically. Not ranked, not compared, not “one of the strongest.”

Here is the argument that needs no citation at all, and it is the stronger one anyway.

A student cannot hold a job they cannot reliably get to.

Transportation turns up in this field as an access barrier and as a matching criterion — a thing you check before you place somebody, because the ride either exists or the placement ends. That is logistics, not correlation, and it will carry more weight in an IEP meeting than any tier label. It also has the advantage of being obviously true to everyone in the room.

Nobody has shown it produces jobs. Be careful in the other direction too. The studies here measure task-analysis steps — can the student complete the trip — not employment. Travel training reliably teaches travel. That is a real claim and it is the one to make.

The goal is not a student who travels well with you. It is a student who travels without you.

Which means the whole method is built around your own removal, on purpose, in stages, on a schedule somebody agreed to in advance.

Three levels of service, not three stages

The Association of Travel Instruction names a continuum, and it is worth knowing because San Diego MTS uses exactly these three words locally. These are levels of service selected by need — not a staircase every student climbs.

  • Orientation — explaining the system itself. Options and services, reading maps and schedules to plan a trip, how the fare works, boarding and riding and exiting, what is on the vehicle. Can be a group, in a room.
  • Familiarization — riding with a travel trainer, for someone who already travels but is learning a new route or a new mode. The trainer points out access features, where the connections happen, and the landmarks along the way.
  • Travel training — strictly one-to-one, comprehensive instruction for independent travel, repeated on consecutive days. Either for a traveler who needs a new destination, or for someone who has no independent travel concepts yet at all.

A confident rider who needs one new route needs familiarization, not a six-week program. Matching the level to the student is most of the decision.

The fade itself

Easterseals Project ACTION, whose 2015 travel-instruction module is the most detailed procedure in our files, calls the method “repetitive practice with fade back techniques.” Three moves, in their words and ours:

  • Demonstrate – observe – reteach. You do the step, you watch them try it, you reteach whatever did not hold.
  • “Slowly fade back, progressively letting student take the lead.” Not a switch you flip on a Monday. A gradient, across days.
  • “Follow the student while he completes the trip independently.” You are present and you are behind them, and the trip belongs to them.

The familiar four-step version — ride together, they lead while you follow, shadow from a distance, then solo with a check-in — maps honestly onto that. But say it the way it is: this is described practice, not a named model. There is no canonical four-stage framework here, nobody has given the stages formal names, and it is not a standardized protocol. Presenting it as one would be inventing an authority that does not exist. It is a practitioner paraphrase of a real procedure, which is plenty.

🔄 Repetition is retention

Two conditions from the same source that get skipped constantly. Travel training runs on multiple consecutive days — a once-a-week ride is not travel training, it is a field trip. And attendance history should show at least 80 percent before a student starts. A student missing a day or more a week will not retain the route, and starting anyway sets up a failure that gets recorded as the student’s.

How it ends

The exit is not a good day and a gut feeling. The trainer shadows the route “using no prompts and as few interventions as possible” and scores it. The recommendation for self-travel is written on the same task analysis form that has been tracking the trip since day one. Then an IEP revision meeting is scheduled to change the student’s transportation service from school bus to fare cards — which is the moment the work becomes a line in a document rather than a thing everybody liked.

One step we are not passing on. The same source adds a second trainer who rides while the student does not know they are being watched. We have one source for it, it raises a real consent question the source never addresses, and it is not something to hand a para as routine practice. The shadowed, scored ride is the exit check we would use.

☀️ Practice spotlight

Three Things to Try Tomorrow

🗺️ 1. Teach the landmarks

“Get off after the big red church” is the kind of instruction travel instructors actually give. Pointing out landmarks along the route is one of the three things a trainer does during familiarization, and landmark identification sits in the basic instructional sequence — get oriented to the environment, learn the landmarks, then learn the route, then work on crossings and traffic. Tie the trip to things the student will see out the window, and tie the cord-pull to the landmark before the stop so there is time to stand up.

🔧 2. Plan the mistake

Do not talk through what-ifs. Engineer the error while you are still standing there. The source’s own phrase is “integrating planned mistakes to test student’s ability to problem solve” — you let them board the wrong bus, you let the stop go by, and then you watch what they do with it. The named content is delays, detours, and bad weather. A recovery that has been practiced once, with you two feet away, is worth ten conversations about what they would do.

📣 3. Teach asking for help

Seeking assistance is one of the named safety targets, and it is the one skill on this page with a study behind it. Taylor and colleagues (2004) taught three teenagers with autism to seek help when separated from an adult in a store — rules, role play, and praise, delivered in the real setting rather than a classroom, with a vibrating pager as the cue and a communication card handed to a store employee. It worked, and for all three it generalized to stores they had never trained in. Three people is a small study. It is also a real one, and the skill it tested is the skill that travels.

Be careful about the next sentence, though, because it is the one everyone adds. That study establishes nothing about whom to approach beyond “a store employee.” It did not test bus operators, trolley platforms, or transit staff, and the student handed over a card rather than speaking. The familiar “find a safe person” heuristic — someone in uniform, someone behind a counter, never the stranger who approaches you first — is common practice and probably good practice, but it is practitioner craft, not a tested taxonomy. Same for a script of what to say. Teach both. Just do not tell a family they are evidence-based.

If the student uses AAC. The help-when-lost message has to be programmed, reachable, and practiced before the first solo trip, not after the first one that goes sideways. Start that conversation with the speech pathologist now — it is a two-week lead time, not a two-minute one.
⚠️ Watch out for

Skipping a Stage

With graduation in view it is tempting to fast-forward a student to solo before the current stage is genuinely reliable.

You have probably heard the reason not to, in roughly this form: one bad experience can set a student back months and put them off transit for good. Here is the honest handling of that sentence. It is a practitioner and family concern, not a finding. Nothing in our files documents a setback duration, a refusal rate, or an avoidance effect after a bad trip. It is plausible, it matches what people describe, and it is not evidence. Say it as the worry it is.

There is a sourced version of the same caution, and it is better, because it is humane instead of ominous.

The outcome nobody talks about

If the student does not complete the task analysis, the determination is written down as “not able to self-travel at this time” — the reasons are documented, and those reasons become IEP goals to build the missing skills.

That is a real, named, expected outcome of the process. It is a re-plan, not a verdict, and it is a much easier thing to bring to a family than a warning about damage. The same source that describes fading a trainer to invisibility also says stop the program entirely if safety issues turn up during file review or observation, and do not restart until they are addressed. Both of those are the practice.

And the argument on the other side

Travel is the transition activity where protection and autonomy collide hardest, because a student learning a job site is watched and a student learning a bus route is, eventually and by design, alone. The frame for that is dignity of risk — Robert Perske’s 1972 argument that people with intellectual disabilities have the right to weigh the risks in their own choices, and that overprotection is itself a harm because it forecloses growth. Savage and Bowers (2022) work through what honoring that actually costs an agency in practice.

Be precise about what kind of claim that is, because you may have to say it to a supervisor. Dignity of risk is an ethics and rights argument, not outcome evidence. Nobody has run a trial comparing protective and permissive travel policies. What the literature offers is a principle and an honest account of the tension, which is more useful than a slogan and less useful than a study. Say which one you are making.

When a family says no

Bross, Fredrick and Kwiatek (2024) interviewed nine young adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities alongside their parents or guardians, plus ten providers, and found the barriers to independent transportation clustering into three groups: family factors, community structure, and systems challenges. Which means a family’s hesitation is usually entangled with route availability, service gaps, and agency coordination — it is structural, not merely anxious. Reassuring the parent will not touch most of it. Nine dyads is a small qualitative study, and its point is the shape of the problem rather than a rate.

The scope question, said plainly

This issue is telling you to run training rides, so it owes you a straight answer about your own role, and the straight answer is that nothing we have read defines what a paraeducator specifically may or may not do in travel instruction, or what supervision is required. There is no policy here to quote. Travel training proper is described as one-to-one instruction by a trained travel instructor; the guidance for school staff generally is to collaborate on orientation and familiarization, act as the go-between with the transit agency, and co-teach the content.

So: this is a team decision made with the teacher and the family. You execute an agreed plan, and you report what you observe — accurately, including the parts that went badly. Deciding that a student is ready for the next stage, or ready to go alone, is not your call to make on your own, and no page including this one should imply a scope of practice that has not been written down anywhere. If nobody has told you where your line is, that is the question to ask before the next ride, not after it.

🎁 Resource of the month

San Diego MTS travel training

Before you build a program from scratch, call the agency that already runs one. MTS offers two things, and they are different sizes:

Taking Transit 101 — a 90-minute small-group orientation for first-time riders and active seniors, covering the bus and Trolley system in general, led by certified volunteer trainers who ride transit themselves. A good first exposure for a student who has never been on the system.

Access-Ability — tiered travel instruction for people with disabilities, led by professionals who know transportation accessibility. Its three levels are the continuum from earlier in this issue, by name: orientation, familiarization, and one-on-one. Skills covered include trip planning, reading schedules and timetables, using the mobility-assist features on buses and Trolleys, using smartphone apps, and getting to popular destinations.

sdmts.com/travel-training →

Two things to check rather than assume. We do not know what it costs. The MTS materials name no price and do not say free, so ask — do not promise a family a free program on our say-so. (Federal transit money under Section 5310 can fund travel training, which is why many agencies offer it at no charge to the rider. That is a funding mechanism, not a price list.) And North County is NCTD, not MTS — if your student lives up the coast, MTS is the wrong agency to call.

One more thing worth knowing before you ask for anything: IDEA does not name travel training. There is no entitlement to it. Travel goals get into an IEP through the community-experiences and daily-living-skills parts of the transition-services definition, which is a real door but a narrower one than people expect. It also means the money often comes from the transit side rather than the school side — which is exactly why a transit agency can sometimes deliver instruction a district cannot fund.

If a phone is going to be part of the plan, one small study is worth carrying. Price, Marsh and Fisher (2017) taught four young adults a fifteen-step routine for using Google Maps to navigate transit, fading their own proximity until they were following by car and meeting the student at the destination. Three of the four reached mastery, holding up at 93 percent and above in places they had not trained. The fourth ignored the app entirely and navigated by environmental cues instead — which is the most useful line in the paper. She was not failing. She had a different strategy. Four people, on a university campus, is a very small base, so treat the phone as one option to try rather than the solution.

One thing before your next ride

Go quiet for one leg. Watch where they hesitate.

One direction, one leg of the trip, no narration and no prompts. Let the student make every call, including the ones you would have made half a second sooner.

Notice exactly where they hesitate. Not whether the trip worked — where the pause was. That hesitation is the next lesson, and it is the thing you cannot find while you are talking.
