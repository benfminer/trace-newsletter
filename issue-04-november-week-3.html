Everything in this month’s issue happens inside a meeting. This week is about what leaves the meeting — because the student is going to walk into rooms where nobody has read the IEP and nobody is going to.

A supervisor. A DOR counselor with a caseload of ninety. A new teacher in August. None of them will read forty pages, all of them will read one.

01 This Week’s Focus

Four boxes, one page

The I’m Determined One-Pager is a single landscape sheet with a name, a date, and four quadrants. That is the entire design, and the restraint is the feature.

S
My Strengths

What they are good at — stated so a stranger could use it on day one.

P
My Preferences

The conditions under which they do their best work.

I
My Interests

What they actually want to be doing, which is not the same as preferences and gets collapsed into them constantly.

N
My Needs

The supports that have to be there. Written as instructions rather than as deficits.

Those four are SPIN — the framework September’s issue was built on. The One-Pager is the physical artefact of it, and the version that matters is the one filled in by the student rather than assembled from the file.

It is free, it is produced by I’m Determined out of the Virginia Department of Education, and nothing in it assumes Virginia procedure. The template has been unchanged since 2018, which for a form is a compliment.

Print it blank. The temptation is to pre-fill it from the file and let the student check it over. That produces a tidier page and none of the benefit — the filling in is the intervention.

A completed One-Pager is portable. The IEP is not, and it ends at exit.

Which of those does the student still have at 23?

    02 The quick tip, properly

    The three seconds you keep taking

    A page the student wrote is worth very little if the student never gets to say any of it out loud.

    Somebody asks your student a question. There is a pause. To you it feels long; to the person waiting it feels like an eternity, and one of you fills it. Almost always the adult.

    It is not impatience. It is care — you are protecting them from an awkward moment in front of a stranger. But the awkward moment is the repetition, and filling it hands the interaction to you permanently. The student learns, accurately, that waiting works.

    1. 1
      Notice who the question was for

      “Does she need a break?” asked over her head is still a question addressed to her. Look at her instead of answering.

    2. 2
      Count before you speak

      Silently, to five. It is a long time and it is not as long as it feels. Most of the answers arrive somewhere in there.

    3. 3
      If you must help, hand it back

      “Do you want to tell them, or do you want me to?” keeps the decision with the student even when the answer does not come.

    Where this stops being about manners

    At 18 the student is the one who consents, signs, and decides. An adult who habitually answers for them is not just being warm — they are speaking on behalf of the legal decision-maker, in a room where what gets said is written down.

    Tell people you are doing it. A one-line warning to the family, the supervisor, or the team — “I’m going to wait, she has it’” — turns an uncomfortable silence into an obviously deliberate one.

    Try this week

    Print one blank page.

    Download the One-Pager, print it, and give it to one student with twenty minutes and no help beyond reading the four labels aloud. Whatever comes back is more current than anything in the file.
