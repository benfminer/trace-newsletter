Side by Side is new this year, and it’s for the paras at TRACE — at the job site, in the kitchen, in the hallway between periods. Not a policy update. Not a compliance reminder. One practical idea a month that you can use on your next shift.

🎯 This month’s focus

Use the least help that works

Your students are young adults, 18 to 22. They’re close to leaving — many age out at 22 — which makes every ounce of independence they build now matter even more.

So let’s start the year with the thing that shows up every single day, and that most of us were never taught to think about: when to step in, and when to hold back.

The whole idea, in one line

Every time a student does it with less of you, they’re building a skill they can use when you’re not there.

Nobody really tells paras this at the start: the way you help can build a student’s independence or quietly chip away at it. Not because you’re doing anything wrong — but jumping in too fast trains an adult to wait for your cue instead of reading the world around them.

A prompting hierarchy is just a ladder of support, from the lightest touch to the heaviest. Your job is to use the lightest rung that gets them moving — and to get lighter as fast as they’ll let you.

👀 Picture this

Marcus, 20, starting his shift

He walks up to his station. The apron is right there on the hook. Before you say a word — pause. Standing there is the natural cue.

If Marcus grabs it on his own, that’s a win. You never had to enter the picture.

If he doesn’t move after a few seconds, try an indirect verbal. Still nothing? Step to direct. Go only as far down as you need — and each day, try to start one rung lighter than yesterday.

A few seconds is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It is longer than it feels. The timer below is there because almost nobody waits as long as they think they do.

☀️ Practice spotlight

Three things to try tomorrow

1 Count to five before you speak

When a student reaches a task, silently count to five. It’ll feel like forever. Do it anyway. You’ll be amazed how often they start moving before you hit five. That pause — called wait time — is one of the most powerful tools you have, and it costs nothing.

2 Look for the natural cue first

Before you say or do anything, ask: what in the setting already tells this student what to do? A set table, a clock hitting 9, a coworker pulling on gloves. If the environment can do the prompting, let it. Your goal is to slowly become invisible.

3 Write down the prompt level you used

Pick one student, one task. For a week, jot the prompt level each time. You’ll spot patterns, and you’ll catch when you’ve been stuck at the same level for weeks without fading. That data matters, and you’re the only one who has it.

That third one is the hardest to keep up, so there’s a tracker further down this page that does the remembering for you.

⚠️ Watch out for

The stuck prompt

The mistake isn’t too much help. It’s the same help, every time, forever.

A student who always gets a direct verbal at the start will eventually just wait for it. They’re not being stubborn — they’ve learned the cue is you, not the environment. You’ve become part of the routine. That’s called prompt dependency, and it happens to great paras all the time, precisely because they’re consistent.

Consistency is a strength — until it becomes a ceiling.

With an 18-to-22-year-old, that ceiling is expensive: the clock to graduation is short, and every month spent waiting for your cue is a month not spent practicing independence.

The fix is simple. Every few days, start one rung lighter and see what happens. If they get stuck, you can always go heavier. But give them the chance to surprise you first.

🎁 Resource of the month

The AFIRM modules — free, and actually good

The AFIRM project out of UNC Chapel Hill has a free module on prompting that’s worth your time. It covers exactly what we did here — with videos, practice scenarios, and a certificate you can keep for professional development hours.

afirm.fpg.unc.edu  →

Free, no login to browse. Look under “Evidence-Based Practices.” The examples lean toward autism, but the prompting strategies fit any student in a transition program.
