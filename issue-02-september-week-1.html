This month’s issue made the case for SPIN — Strengths, Preferences, Interests, Needs. This week is the mechanics: where the four answers actually come from, and why the ones already in the file are the least trustworthy source you have.

A transition file for a 21-year-old often carries a SPIN profile assembled when they were fourteen or fifteen. Nobody lied. It was accurate then. The problem is that it has been quoted forward, IEP after IEP, until it reads like a fact about the student rather than a snapshot of a ninth grader.

01 This Week’s Focus

Five ways to gather it,
none of which is a test

No single method captures a full profile. The field’s standard practice is to use several and compare them.

  1. 1
    Self-reflection

    The student works through their own SPIN in a structured activity. This is the only method where the student is the author, and it is the one most often skipped for students who need support to complete it.

  2. 2
    The SPIN interview

    The student interviews three to five people in their own life — family, a teacher, a coach, someone from church or the neighborhood — with a short form: what am I good at, what do I avoid, what do you help me with? The student holds the clipboard. That reversal is most of the value.

  3. 3
    Interest inventories and learning-style tools

    Formal instruments. Useful, bounded, and never sufficient on their own — an inventory returns categories, and a plan needs conditions.

  4. 4
    Observation across settings

    Watch the student in more than one place. A student who is withdrawn in the classroom and animated in the kitchen has told you something about preferences that no form will surface.

  5. 5
    The IEP meeting itself

    Where the profile gets shared, contested, and written down — ideally with the student presenting a one-page version of it themselves.

If the student cannot self-report: pictorial interest inventories cover Interests with no reading required, and criterion-based community skills assessments cover Strengths and Needs through observation and interview.

The goal is not a completed form. It is a student who can say their own SPIN out loud.

That sentence is what they take to an employer, and to a disability services office

That is the actual test of whether this worked. Not whether the profile exists in the file — whether the student knows what is in it. A profile the student can’t articulate is a document about them, not a tool for them.

02 When sources disagree

Disagreement is not noise

Two people who know a student well, describing them differently, are usually both right about different rooms.

The instinct is to resolve the conflict — pick the credible source, write one line, move on. Resist it. When the teacher says “works well independently” and the family says “needs someone with her,” the useful reading is almost never that one of them is wrong. It is that independence is holding in a structured classroom and not holding at home, which tells you exactly what the job site has to look like.

Resolve it One line in the PLAAFP. Clean, defensible, and it has thrown away the condition that made the difference.
Record both Two lines, attributed. The disagreement itself becomes a testable question for the first work experience.

Record who said what. The documentation standard is unglamorous and it is the thing that makes a profile survive contact with next year’s team: for every tool or conversation, write down the name, the date, and the information gained.

A note on families

Families hold data nobody else has — who does which chores, what responsibilities the student actually carries, what they are trusted with. Ask specifically about that rather than asking what the family wants for the student’s future. The second question gets a hope. The first gets evidence.

And approach it as relationship-building rather than data collection. Families of transition-age students have often spent years being assessed at.

Two sources most teams never open: CTE and elective teachers, who hold curriculum-based performance data on the student in a working context, and paraeducators, who are the only adults present for the parts of the day where independence actually gets tested.
