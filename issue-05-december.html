A paid job before they exit.
5.1× more likely
to be employed after.

That is the largest single predictor in the transition literature, and most students with disabilities leave school without one. Everything else this month is about closing that distance.

↓ Start reading

New here? Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition — hover, tap, or tab to one to see it.

Students who held a paid job at the time they left high school were 5.1 times more likely to be employed after school. Work study — a structured sequence pairing instruction with paid or credited work — doubled the likelihood of full-time employment. No other single predictor in that literature comes close.

Which puts an uncomfortable question in front of every transition program, including this one: how many of our students will walk out having been paid by somebody other than us?

Not enrolled in a career class. Not signed up for a tour. Paid, by an employer, for work that employer needed doing.

What the 5.1× is and is not. It comes from a secondary synthesis rather than a single primary study, and it is not disaggregated by disability type — nobody has shown it holds equally for a student with significant support needs and a student with a specific learning disability. It is the best-supported number we have and it is still an average.
01 This Month’s Focus

Four tiers, and only
one of them is measured

Work-based learning is a continuum, not an internship. It runs from knowing jobs exist to holding one, and the tiers are not interchangeable.

01
Career awareness

Workplace tours, guest speakers, career fairs. The message is “jobs exist, and here is the range of them.”

02
Career exploration

Job shadowing, informational interviews, business mentorships. The question is “which of these fits me?”

03
Career preparation

Volunteering, service learning, internships, work crews. Real tasks in a real setting, usually unpaid, usually on a clock.

04
Training & paid employment

Apprenticeships and actual jobs. Real compensation, real work, a real employment relationship with a community business.

The evidence is not evenly spread across those four boxes. It is concentrated almost entirely in the last one. Paid employment and work study have measured effect sizes; awareness and exploration are supported by practitioner consensus and logic, and the large-scale outcome data behind them is thin.

The finding that should change a schedule

Grigal and colleagues followed 686 students with intellectual disability through college programs and out the other side. Three numbers from that study, in the order they hurt:

  1. 1
    87% did unpaid career development

    Internships, volunteering, service learning. Nearly everybody. This is the part programs are good at and it is the part that gets counted.

  2. 2
    Unpaid experience did not predict employment

    Not a significant predictor of having a paid job at exit. All that activity, and the model could not find it in the outcome.

  3. 3
    A paid job while enrolled predicted at roughly 15×

    The strongest single term in the model — and having been paid before entering the program predicted too. Only 35% of students arrived with any paid work history at all.

Eighty-seven percent of them were busy. Thirty-five percent of them had ever been paid.

Busy and effective are different measurements

Be careful about how far that travels: it is a college-program population, not a high school one, and it is correlational. A program that puts every student in a paid job may simply be a program that had the employer relationships and the students positioned to use them. But the direction is consistent with the high school literature, and the gap between 87% and 35% is not a subtle finding.

Why programs stall at tiers 1 and 2

Awareness is cheap, safe, and schedulable. A speaker comes to you. A tour takes an afternoon and needs no wage, no work permit, no supervision plan, and no employer willing to be inconvenienced. Paid work requires somebody to make forty phone calls.

So programs do the affordable thing repeatedly and it looks like a continuum, because the students keep moving to new activities. They are moving sideways.

There is not time to spend two years on job shadows. Your students are 18 to 22. A student who arrives at 20 having done awareness activities since middle school has had the top of the continuum deferred for seven years, and now has two.

Interactive · one student, one year of hours

Spend the year

Give one student a year of work-based learning. Decide how many hours go into each tier, and watch two things at once: what the federal wage rules say about the unpaid columns, and how much of the year lands in the only tier with a measured effect.

01 Career awareness Tours, speakers, career fairs. No wage question — nobody is working.
02 Career exploration Job shadowing and informational interviews. Capped at 5 hours per job before a wage is owed.
03 Career preparation, unpaid Internship, work crew, service learning. Assessment caps at 90 hours per job, training at 120.
04 Paid employment An actual job. At California minimum wage or above, on somebody’s payroll.
The hour caps are per job, not per year. Three different five-hour shadows at three different employers are compliant; fifteen hours at one is not. That is worth knowing before somebody builds a semester around a single host site.

    02 Practice Spotlight

    Tier Your Caseload

    One line per student, fifteen minutes, one sheet of paper. It will show you exactly who is stuck.

    1. 1
      Name and current tier

      Every student on the list, with the highest tier they have actually reached — not the highest one they are scheduled for.

    2. 2
      Circle anyone who has not moved in a year

      Same tier twelve months running is stalled, not settled. The distinction matters because stalled students usually look busy.

    3. 3
      Write the one move that gets each of them up a tier this semester

      One move. Named. If you cannot name it, that is the finding, and it is a more useful finding than a list of activities.

    Then sort by how close they are to exiting. A 21-year-old at tier 2 is a different emergency than an 18-year-old at tier 2, and a caseload sorted alphabetically hides that completely.

    The move that is not a move

    Adding a fourth job shadow for a student who has done three is a lateral move dressed as progress. It fills a slot, it generates a record, and it leaves the student exactly where they were. Ask what tier it ends in, not what it is called.

    03 Resource of the Month
    • Free
    • centerontransition.org
    • Practitioner-facing

    VCU Center on Transition Innovations

    CTI is where the four-tier continuum in this issue comes from, and they publish the operational half of it as well as the framing: work-based learning guidance, a free self-paced WBL course, and a nine-step business outreach sequence for getting a host site to say yes.

    Read the nine steps before your next cold call. The short version is that step one is not calling anybody — it is building the list from people you and your students already know. Week 3 takes that apart properly.

    Virginia-built, and the funding names differ here. CTI’s WBL material assumes Virginia DARS; the California equivalent is DOR Pre-ETS, where work-based learning is one of the five required service categories and a student needs only an IEP or 504 plan — no eligibility determination, from age 16. Align your WBL documentation to the Pre-ETS categories or the funding conversation gets harder than it needs to be.

    Do this month

    Write down three warm leads.

    Over break, list three local employers you already have a real connection to — the gym you go to, your grocery store, a family friend’s shop, a former student’s manager. Not a list of businesses. A list of people whose name you know.

    That list is your January pipeline, and it will outperform any amount of cold calling.
