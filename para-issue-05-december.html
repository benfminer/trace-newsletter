💭 This month’s focus

Behavior Is Communication

Not theory and not a twenty-step protocol. The practical stuff for when you are standing next to a young adult who is escalating and you have about four seconds to decide what to do.

The most useful reframe in this work is also the simplest: every behavior communicates something. When a student pushes materials off a desk, goes silent, or gets sharp with you, they are saying something they cannot say another way at that moment.

That is not an excuse for the behavior and it does not mean you let it run. It changes what you respond to. Responding to the shouting gets you a negotiation about shouting. Responding to what is underneath it usually ends the episode faster.

Your program’s behavior plan comes first, always. Everything here is general practice for ordinary escalation. If a student has a written plan, a BIP, or a crisis protocol, that document governs and this page does not.

Three things it is usually saying

Naming which one you are looking at is most of the work, because the three need genuinely different responses — and the wrong one makes it worse rather than neutral.

🚫 “This is too hard right now.”

Escape. The task or the setting is more than they can carry at this moment. Pushing harder confirms the problem. Making the task smaller — fewer steps, a break with a defined end, one part instead of all of it — usually deflates it.

👤 “I need a person.”

Connection. Even hard, ugly reactions can be a bid for somebody to be there. Withdrawing your attention as a consequence lands as abandonment on a student who is asking for contact.

🔊 “I am overloaded.”

Sensory. Noise, lights, crowding, three people talking. Nothing you say will land while the input is still coming. Change the environment first and talk second.

You are not deciding whether the behavior is acceptable. You are deciding what it is asking for.

Those are different questions and only one of them helps at 10:40am

Honest caveat: you are guessing. A real answer to “why does this keep happening” comes from the team watching patterns over time, not from you reading a face in the moment. A good guess made quickly is still better than responding to the surface, and your notes are what turn the guess into the pattern.

There is a second question, and it is the easier one. Instead of asking what the behavior is for, ask how far along it is — settled, agitated, accelerating, or past the point of talking. The Center on PBIS organizes its whole guide that way, because the phase is something you can see, and it picks your next move for you. Function takes weeks of watching. Phase takes a glance.

In the moment

Two things carry most of the weight, and both of them are about you rather than the student.

Lower your voice and slow down

Escalation feeds on urgency. Fewer words, longer gaps, quieter voice, more physical space. Calm is contagious in exactly the same way panic is — and a student who is flooded is reading your body long before they are processing your sentences.

Do not problem-solve mid-storm

“Why did you do that?” and “what should you have done instead?” are good questions asked at an impossible time. Safety and calm first; the conversation comes after they have come down, which may be twenty minutes and may be the next day.

And keep the exit available. “Do you want to take a lap and come back?” gives an escalating adult a way down that does not cost them their dignity in front of the room. “You need to calm down” gives them a public instruction to obey, which is the one thing guaranteed to raise the temperature. That difference matters more at 20 than it did at 10.

    ☀️ Practice spotlight

    Three things to try tomorrow

    👀 1. Learn one student’s early sign

    Everybody has a tell before the storm — a bouncing leg, going quiet, a particular phrase, going very polite. Pick one student and learn theirs. Catching it ninety seconds early beats any response you can make ninety seconds late.

    🤝 2. Offer an out, not an order

    “Want to take a lap and come back?” instead of “you need to calm down.” A face-saving exit lets an adult step down without losing in front of everyone, and that is most of what the argument was ever about.

    📝 3. Write down what came right before

    Afterward, jot the minute before it started. Not the incident — the minute before. Two weeks of those and the pattern shows up, and a pattern is the only thing that lets the team prevent the next one instead of riding it out again.

    Number three is the one that changes anything. The first two help you tomorrow. The third one is what eventually means tomorrow does not need them — and it is the one nobody has time for, which is why it does not happen.
    ⚠️ Watch out for

    Getting Pulled In

    You cannot pour calm from an empty cup. Your own regulation is the tool, and it is the one nobody trains you on.

    When a student escalates, your heart rate climbs with theirs. That is not a character flaw, it is a nervous system doing what nervous systems do. But a rattled adult makes a rattled student worse, and the two of you can climb together for a surprisingly long time before anybody notices.

    So take the breath first. Drop your shoulders, unclench your jaw, buy yourself two seconds before you say anything. Two seconds is enough to choose a response instead of producing one.

    And know where your edge is

    If it is past a quick de-escalation, or there is a safety concern, get the teacher or run the crisis plan. Asking for backup is not the plan failing — it is the plan working. The paras who get hurt or burned out are rarely the ones who called for help too early.

    One more, quietly: it is going to feel personal sometimes, because sometimes it is aimed at you. It still is not about you. That is easier to say in December than to feel in the moment, which is why it is worth deciding before the moment.

    🎁 Resource of the month

    Strategies for De-escalating Student Behavior in the Classroom

    A free 14-page practice brief from the Center on PBIS at the University of Oregon (Strickland-Cohen et al., September 2022). It lays out the phases an escalation moves through — prevention, escalation, crisis, then recovery and restoration — and gives you a short table of what actually helps at each one. Written for the person in the room, not for administrators.

    It is also where the two rules in this issue come from. Dysregulated students are often unable to follow directions, learn new information, or reflect on their own behavior in the moment — that is why you do not problem-solve mid-storm. And on staying calm yourself: “you cannot use your skills to regulate the intensity of the situation if you are experiencing behavior escalation.”

    Read the brief

    Free PDF. The authors are explicit that it is not a stand-alone crisis resource — it is meant to sit alongside district-approved crisis training, and it does not replace anything in a student’s own behavior plan. Same rule as the top of this page: your program’s plan comes first.

    One thing before your next shift

    Pick one student. Learn their tell.

    Do not do anything with it this week. Just watch for the thing that happens right before — the leg, the silence, the phrase, the sudden politeness. Name it to yourself when you see it.

    Naming the tell is the whole first step. Everything else gets easier once you can see it coming.
