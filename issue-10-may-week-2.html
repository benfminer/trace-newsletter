Forty-five minutes
in June.

The monthly sketched the year-end debrief in five steps. This week is the meeting itself — what has to be on the table before it starts, why one year of one indicator cannot support the conclusion everybody wants to draw from it, and the precondition on step three that almost every team skips.

Following up on Issue 10 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

The year-end data meeting is one of the few things in a school calendar that nobody defends and nobody quite cancels. It gets scheduled late, it gets moved twice, and by the time it happens somebody is projecting a percentage onto a wall while everyone else is thinking about the boxes in their room. Forty-five minutes is not the problem with it. What is on the table when it starts is the problem with it.

The documented version of this meeting is a five-step cycle — collect, analyze, plan, implement, review — and the monthly walked all five. The interesting part is not the list. It is the two specifications buried inside step one and step three, both of which are easy to read past and both of which decide whether the meeting produces a conclusion that survives being questioned. Step one says three years, and it says each transition indicator, plural. Step three says the people doing the planning have to already know the predictor evidence base. Miss the first and you are reading noise. Miss the second and you will plan toward whatever was discussed most recently.

01 This Week’s Focus

What three years
is for

A single year of Indicator 14 is one measurement of a small population with a known response problem. It is not nothing. It is also not a trend, and almost every sentence people want to say in a debrief is a sentence about a trend.

The instruction is specific: gather data from each transition indicator for the past three years, because multi-year review is what enables trend recognition and more thorough analysis. Read that as a constraint on language rather than on effort. With one year you can say what the number is. With three you can say which direction it is going, and only the second kind of sentence can justify changing anything.

The population makes this sharper than it sounds. Indicator 14 is a census, not a sample — every leaver with an IEP from the reporting year, surveyed once they have been separated from secondary education for a year. For a program the size of most, that census is small enough that four or five students moving between categories swings the percentage several points. A three-point change in a cohort of forty is not a finding. It is one student and a rounding rule.

And why it says indicators, plural

Four federal indicators touch transition, and they are meant to be read together because each one constrains how you are allowed to interpret the others. They describe four different points in the same pipeline.

  • Indicator 1 — graduation rate. A performance indicator with a state-set target. It tells you how your students left.
  • Indicator 2 — dropout rate. Also performance, also state-set. Read next to Indicator 1 it tells you whether the exit door changed shape.
  • Indicator 13 — IEP transition components. The odd one out: a compliance indicator held to 100 percent, and audited rather than surveyed. It rests on IEP document review, not on outcome data. It tells you whether the plans that produced these leavers contained the required elements — measurable postsecondary goals based on age-appropriate assessment, transition services, course of study, and agency linkage.
  • Indicator 14 — post-school outcomes. Where they landed a year out. The only one of the four that is about life after the program.

Put them in one row and the interpretive work gets easier, not harder, because a change in one is frequently explained by a change in another. If graduation slid and dropout rose in the same years the outcome rates fell, part of what you are looking at is a change in who is leaving and how — not only a change in what happened to them afterward. If Indicator 13 came off 100 percent in the years those students were being planned for, you have a candidate mechanism sitting right there in the same table.

None of these are this year’s students. Indicator 14 is a rearview mirror: the survey reaches youth who left a year ago, so schools learn their outcomes one to two years after those students were actually in the program. The meeting in June is feedback on the program as it was, run by a team that has since changed. That is not a reason to discount it. It is a reason to stop asking “what did we do wrong this year” and start asking what was true two years ago that is still true now.

The survey already asked more than you are reading

Here is the part of Indicator 14 that gets thrown away every year. The three rates are what gets reported. They are not what gets collected. The same survey that produces 14A, 14B and 14C also captures a second layer of data that is not part of the percentage calculation at all — and that second layer is much closer to what a debrief actually needs.

  1. 1
    Agency referrals during high school

    Which agencies each student was referred to before they left — vocational rehabilitation, the community services board, Social Security, the state employment agency, Job Corps, and others. Set against the same student’s outcome, this is the closest thing the instrument has to a test of last month’s argument about handoffs.

  2. 2
    School programs participated in

    Work-based learning, Pre-ETS, Project SEARCH, supported employment, self-determination programming, dual enrollment, career assessment. These are program features you control, recorded against outcomes you are trying to move — which is a different and more useful object than a headline rate.

  3. 3
    Barriers, for the students with nothing after exit

    Asked specifically of the youth reporting no employment and no postsecondary education. This is the group a low 14C is measuring the complement of, and the survey asked them directly what was in the way. Very few programs ever read those responses.

  4. 4
    Life satisfaction, and which classes helped

    A five-point satisfaction scale, plus a retrospective question about which high school classes turned out to be useful. Neither belongs in an accountability report. Both belong in a room where people are deciding what to change about a course of study.

What to ask for before the meeting

The percentage arrives on its own. The secondary data usually has to be requested, and it is the half that answers why. Ask for the referral and program-participation fields alongside the rates, and the forty-five minutes stops being a reading of a scoreboard and becomes an argument about mechanism. Set the table

You are about to run the debrief. Start with what most teams start with — this year’s Indicator 14 — and then add years and indicators one at a time. Nothing about the underlying program changes. What changes is which sentences the data will actually hold up, and which conclusion you would have walked out with if you had stopped earlier.

Before you start

Every number in this table is invented

These are illustrative figures for a fictional program, chosen to make the interpretive problem visible. They are not any state’s data, any district’s data, or TRACE’s. What is real is the structure: four transition indicators, three years, one of them a compliance measure held to 100 percent, and the rule that a single year of a census this small cannot distinguish a trend from a wobble.

Nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere. Reload the page and the table resets.

Loading the table. With scripting off, the exercise is this. Write down your own program’s Indicator 14 rate for the most recent year, and notice what you want to say about it. Then add the two years before it, and notice how many of those sentences you can still defend. Then add three more rows — graduation, dropout, and IEP transition compliance — for the same three years, and look for the year where two rows moved together. That row pair is where the meeting should spend its forty-five minutes. Finally, ask two questions about the room itself: is more than one person reading this, and has anybody in here read the predictor list?

      Who is in the room

      Step two of the cycle does not say review the data. It says review it as a team of relevant stakeholders, looking for trends, strengths, and areas needing improvement. That is a design decision, not a courtesy, and it is the cheapest of the five steps to get right.

      The reason is that a percentage cannot explain itself. Somebody in the building knows why three students from one cohort ended up in the same training program, or why the year the job coach position went unfilled looks the way it does in the numbers. That knowledge does not live in the portal. It lives with the people who were in the room when it happened, and it evaporates when the read is done alone by whoever was assigned the reporting.

      • Administrators are told to lead a cross-stakeholder team through the cycle — using three years of all four transition indicators, not a single report handed around for comment.
      • Data collectors should have relationships with students and families, and all surveyors should be trained. Who makes the calls changes the data, which means it is a program decision rather than a clerical one.
      • Response rate is a standing agenda item. Contact rates are frequently below 100 percent, so the students nobody reached are missing from the number — and there is no reason to think they are missing at random. State survey protocols set a floor on effort — in Virginia’s, a minimum of four contact attempts per leaver — and there is published guidance devoted entirely to raising Indicator 14 response rates.
      Who answered also matters. The survey may be completed by the former student, a parent or guardian, a relative, a teacher, or an adult service provider. A family member may have incomplete information about a young adult’s actual employment status, so two identical responses can carry very different confidence. When the team is arguing about a number, ask who supplied it before arguing about what it means.

      The precondition on step three

      Step three is where the meeting produces something, and it carries a requirement that is stated plainly and almost never met: the stakeholders participating in planning must be familiar with the predictors of post-school success — the evidence base for what works. Not the administrator. Not the person who ran the data. The people doing the planning.

      It is worth being blunt about what happens when that precondition is skipped, because it is not that the plan is bad. It is that the plan is recency-shaped. A team with no shared reference list reaches for whatever was discussed most recently — the conference somebody attended in March, the incident from April, the program a neighboring district is excited about. All of those may be fine. None of them is a reason, and the difference shows up next June when nobody can say why that was the thing you chose.

      Two instruments, two jobs

      Your Indicator 14 outcomes tell you whether it worked. The predictors tell you what to change. Running them together is where most year-end data conversations go wrong — the outcome data gets asked to explain itself, which it cannot do, and the predictor list gets treated as a scorecard, which it is not. Predictors are correlational, not causal. They point; they do not prove, and they carry no guidance on dosage or timing. Week 3 takes the self-assessment apart →

      Which leaves the output. Steps four and five are about execution — check in periodically, confirm the responsible parties have what they need, review progress, adjust, and keep collecting so the next round has three years to look at. All of that presumes the meeting ended with something specific enough to check in on. One change, written down, with a person and a date attached. If forty-five minutes produces a shared feeling that the program should do more work-based learning, it produced nothing; that sentence has no owner and no evidence of completion.

      If it helps to have candidates, the published guidance for educators names three concrete actions to pick from, and all three are the right size for a single school year: raise student survey participation, raise the program’s career- and college-readiness commitment, or reduce the number of unengaged respondents. Note that two of the three are about the data rather than the program. That is not evasion. A program that cannot reach its leavers is a program that will be guessing again next June.

      Try this week

      Send one email before the building empties.

      Ask for three years of all four transition indicators — graduation, dropout, IEP transition compliance, and post-school outcomes — and ask for the Indicator 14 secondary fields alongside the rates: agency referrals, program participation, and the barriers reported by students with nothing after exit. That request is the entire difference between a debrief that reads a scoreboard and one that can name a mechanism.

      Then invite three people who saw the students. Not the people who own the reporting — the people who can explain an anomaly, because they were there when it happened. Forty-five minutes, one table, and a rule that the meeting does not end until one sentence is written down: what changes, who owns it, and how you will know in June.

      And if nobody in that room has read the predictor list, that is your one change. Everything else can wait a year; that one compounds.
