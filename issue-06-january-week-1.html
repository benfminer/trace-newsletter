Unprofessional. Defensive. Doesn’t take feedback. Every one of those is a real observation about a real student, and not one of them tells you what to do on Monday.

They also do not tell you, in June, whether anything changed. Adjectives have no before and after. Virginia’s framework ships with a rating scale for exactly this reason, and it takes about ninety seconds per student once you know the levels.

01 This Week’s Focus

Why three of the
four buckets are social

The review the monthly leaned on sorted job losses tied to a worker’s own performance into four categories. The shape of the list is the argument.

  1. 1
    Job responsibility

    Attendance, punctuality, following workplace rules.

  2. 2
    Task-production skills

    Speed, accuracy, actually doing the work.

  3. 3
    Task-related social skills

    Taking direction, asking for help, responding to correction.

  4. 4
    Personal-social skills

    Getting along with coworkers, conversation, reading the room.

One of those four is the job. The other three are how a person moves through a workplace.

Which is the whole reason to spend January on habits

The five-point scale

CTI’s Workplace Readiness Skills Rubric rates each of the 22 skills on five levels. Read them from the bottom up, because the top one is the part most rating scales leave out entirely.

  1. 5
    Can teach peers

    Not just competent — able to show someone else how.

  2. 4
    Can perform independently

    With or without accommodations. Both count as independent here.

  3. 3
    Can perform with limited supervision

    Occasional check-ins, not continuous direction.

  4. 2
    Can perform with supervision

    Needs someone present and directing.

  5. 1
    Cannot perform

    Not yet, in this setting.

Most instruments we point at students only look downward, cataloguing what is missing. This one runs up to can teach peers — which means a student can top out on a skill nobody has ever credited them with, and that is a finding worth writing into a present-levels statement.

Built for IEP use. The rubric’s own instructions say to use the results to plan instruction, create practice opportunities, and reassess for growth.

Rate the whole profile

Pick a student and go down the list. Do not deliberate — first instinct, 1 to 5, and skip anything you genuinely have not seen. The readout builds as you go, and it is looking for strengths as hard as it is looking for gaps.

With scripting off, here are the 22 skills to rate on paper.     Before you use it

    Level 4 hides something. “Independently, with or without accommodations” puts a student who needs a written checklist and a student who needs nothing on the same line. For tracking growth that is fine. For a conversation with an employer it is not — so write the accommodation in the margin, because the rubric will not capture it for you.

    Rate the same skill in two settings. The rubric is built for the classroom and for work-based learning, and the gap between the two scores is the actual finding. A 4 in your room and a 2 at the job site is not an inconsistent student. It is a generalization problem, and it calls for a different response than more instruction.

    If five levels is too much apparatus

    CTI’s court-involved-youth version rates each skill as a simple + or – — job asset or job need. Same 22 skills, a fraction of the time. It is designed to be completed as a team with the student in the room, the facilitator reading each descriptor aloud and the team reaching consensus. When the team cannot convene, the student, a teacher and a parent each complete it independently and someone compiles the results. Work supervisors and community providers are explicitly welcome at that table.

    Repeat it annually. The court-involved version is designed to be re-run each year to catch growth — or regression.

    Try this week

    Three students, one skill, one number each.

    The same skill for all three. Do not research it and do not observe for a week first — go with what you already know.

    If you cannot land on a number, that is the finding. It means you have been describing the student rather than the skill.
