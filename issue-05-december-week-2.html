Every one of these
students is busy.
None of them are moving.

Stalled students do not look stalled. They look scheduled. The tell is that the tier has not changed in a year, and the next thing on the calendar would not change it either.

Following up on Issue 05 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

The monthly asked you to write one line per student — name, current tier — and circle anyone who has not moved in a year. This week is the next column: the one move that gets each of them up a tier, and whether the move you were about to make is one.

Most are not. Not through carelessness — through availability. The move that exists is the move that gets made, and the moves that exist are almost always at the tier the program is already comfortable in.

01 This Week’s Focus

Lateral moves have
all the paperwork

A fourth job shadow generates a permission slip, a log entry, a supervisor contact, and a line in the progress note. It is indistinguishable from progress in every record the program keeps.

Which is the whole problem. A student can accumulate two years of documented work-based learning without ever crossing from one tier into the next, and every individual decision along the way was reasonable. Nobody chose to stall them.

UP
A move that changes the tier

The student ends it doing something they could not do before, at a higher band of the continuum. There are not many of these and they all require somebody outside the program to agree to something.

FLAT
A move that repeats the tier

New employer, new setting, same band. Sometimes genuinely necessary. Usually it is the option that was already on the calendar.

SET
A move that unblocks the next one

Does not raise the tier itself — an assessment, a work permit, a DOR referral — but the tier cannot rise until it happens. Not a stall, provided something is scheduled behind it.

The question is never “is this a good activity.” It is “what tier does the student stand in when it ends.”

Those two questions have different answers surprisingly often

Interactive · five students, one move each

Pick the next move

Five composites, all stalled, all with something plausible already penciled in for spring. Choose what you would actually do next for each one, and the readout says where it leaves them.

1 Rosa · 19 · tier 1 Two years of career fairs and speaker panels — six events. Keeps saying she likes animals. Has never been inside a workplace.

2 Kwame · 21 · tier 3 Finishing a second unpaid internship — 90 hours at a warehouse. The supervisor has said twice that he is faster than the new hires.

3 Ivy · 18 · tier 2 Six job shadows in one semester across six industries. Enjoyed all of them. Cannot name a direction and the shadows are starting to blur.

4 Tomás · 20 · tier 4 Eight paid hours a week at a car wash. Good job, real wage — and it is seasonal. It ends in March, and he exits in two years.

5 Nia · 22 · tier 1 Exits in June. Significant support needs. Has never had a placement, and the phrase in every meeting for four years has been “not ready yet.”

All five are composites — and none of the three options for any of them is malpractice. That is the point. Every wrong answer here is something a competent program does on a Tuesday.

Students who move up

Where the caseload lands
Still undecided

    Up, flat, and setup are labels applied here for the sake of the exercise — the continuum tiers are CTI’s, the wage caps are federal, and the rest is judgment. Where a call is arguable the readout argues both sides rather than scoring you.

    The move nobody makes

    Kwame is the one worth sitting with. He has done 180 unpaid hours at a warehouse where the supervisor volunteers, unprompted, that he outworks the new hires. The next thing on the calendar is a third internship.

    The move is to ask them to hire him. Not to find him a job — to convert the one he is already doing. It costs one conversation with somebody who already knows his work, and it skips every hard part of job development: no cold call, no unknown fit, no first-day risk on either side.

    It goes unasked because asking feels like spending a relationship the program depends on. Which is real. It is also worth noticing that a host site receiving 180 free hours of competent labor is not the party with the exposure in that conversation.

    And the wage rules are on your side here. Work-related training caps at 120 unpaid hours per job. Kwame is past that. “We are at the point where this has to become a paid position” is a true sentence, an easier one to say than a favor, and it puts the law rather than the student in the middle of the ask.

    Nia, and the sentence that ends the argument

    Four years of “not ready yet” is a decision that was never made out loud. California state policy is explicit: preparing for and participating in competitive integrated employment is the highest priority for working-age people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, regardless of the severity of their disabilities. Six months is a bad runway. It is a better one than none, and she is still on the roster.

    Try this week

    Find your Kwame.

    One student, at one host site, whose supervisor has said something good about them out loud. That student is the shortest distance your program has to a paid placement, and the distance is one phone call.

    Make it before the break ends.
