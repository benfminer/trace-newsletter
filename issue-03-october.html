A checkbox never got
anyone a job.
A good plan might.

It’s IEP season. Every plan you write will be reviewed for compliance — and passing that review tells you almost nothing.

↓ Start reading

New here? Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition — hover, tap, or tab to one to see it.

Every plan you write this season gets checked for four things: measurable postsecondary goals, transition services, a course of study, and an invitation to the agencies that will matter. Have all four and you are compliant.

Here is the part that should bother you. That review is a document review. Someone reads the IEP and confirms the required parts are present. Nobody visits the student. Nobody checks whether the plan did anything.

So a plan can be flawless on paper and inert in practice, and the system has no way to tell the difference. That gap is this month’s subject.

01 This Month’s Focus

What IDEA requires —
and what good looks like

In California this is in place by age 16, so every TRACE student already has all four. The only live question is whether they are any good.

Ed. Code 56345.1. California’s trigger is age 16 — the federal minimum. Some states start at 14.
  1. 1
    Measurable postsecondary goals

    Where the student is headed after school — in employment, in education or training, and where needed, in independent living. They must be based on age-appropriate transition assessment.

  2. 2
    Transition services and activities

    The concrete steps — who does what, by when — that get the student toward those goals.

  3. 3
    A course of study

    The classes and experiences this year that actually point at the goal, rather than just accumulating credits.

  4. 4
    Agency linkage

    An invitation to the outside agencies whose participation may be needed — DOR, the Regional Center. This is the one that quietly gets skipped, and it is the one with a waiting list attached.

Thirteen and fourteen

The compliance review has a name: Indicator 13. It is scored by reading IEPs, and the expected target is 100% — not a stretch goal, a floor. Every transition IEP is supposed to contain every required element, always.

Indicator 14 is the other one, and it works completely differently. It is a performance indicator: where students actually landed a year after they left. That number is set against a state target, and nobody expects 100%.

13
Compliance — target 100%

Audited by document review. Asks: are the required parts present?

14
Performance — target set by the state

Measured by surveying leavers. Asks: did it work?

A program can sit at 100% on 13 and be failing on 14. Nothing in the system flags that as a contradiction.

Because they are not measuring the same thing

None of which makes compliance optional — it is federal law, it is monitored, and a missing element is a genuine problem for the student as well as the district. It just means clearing that bar is the beginning of the work. The law sets floors, not ceilings. What a high-quality transition IEP looks like is not specified in any statute, which is precisely why it falls to the person writing it.

Watch what one word does

The most common failure is a vague postsecondary goal — and vague goals pass review constantly, because a reviewer is checking that a goal exists, not that it is any use.

Compliant, but empty “After high school, Marcus will work in a job he enjoys.”
Good, and actionable “Within one year of exiting, Marcus will be competitively employed part-time in warehouse or stocking work, with support from DOR.”

The test: could a brand-new teacher pick up this IEP and know exactly what to work on Monday? If not, it is compliant and useless.

Why this bites harder at TRACE

The runway is short. A “someday” goal costs a student with four years left far more than it costs a fifteen-year-old, because it wastes the exact time they cannot get back. Near-term and specific enough to drive this year’s services — that is the standard.

    02 Practice Spotlight

    Rescue One Goal

    Not all of them. One. Pick the weakest postsecondary goal on your caseload and run it through three questions.

    1. 1
      Where, exactly?

      Name the job type, the training program, the living setting — not “a job.” If you cannot name it yet, the honest fix is a goal that says what you are doing to find out.

    2. 2
      By when?

      Anchor it to exit: “within one year of leaving the program.” “After high school” is a direction, not a deadline, and it cannot be measured.

    3. 3
      Does a service back it?

      Every goal needs an activity this year that builds toward it. No matching service means it is a wish, not a plan — and this is the question that most often exposes a goal nobody intends to work on.

    Fix one this week. Next IEP season, do it before the meeting rather than during it — goals drafted live, in a room, with a parent waiting, are how “a job he enjoys” gets written in the first place.

    A caution on specificity. A goal should name a direction, not a single employer. “Warehouse or stocking work” survives a hiring freeze. “Stocker at the Miramar store” does not.

    The Indicator 13 Checklist

    NTACT:C’s Indicator 13 Checklist walks the compliance elements of a transition IEP one at a time. It is essentially the rubric a reviewer uses — which means you can grade your own plan before anyone else does.

    Self-check one IEP against it this week. The gaps you find are your to-do list — and they are far cheaper to find in October than in a monitoring cycle.

    Use it for what it is. The checklist certifies that a plan is complete. It cannot tell you whether the plan is worth following — that judgment is still yours.

    Do this month

    Ask the Monday morning question.

    Read each postsecondary goal on your caseload and ask: “Would I know how to spend Monday morning on this?” If the answer is fuzzy, the goal is too.

    Five minutes now against a wasted semester later.
