The fifteen-minute SPIN conversation is the highest-yield thing in this month’s issue and the easiest to do badly. Done badly it produces four one-word answers and a note in the file saying the student was “unsure of post-school goals.”

The failure is almost never the student. It is the phrasing. A question built out of IEP vocabulary invites the student to perform the IEP back at you, and most of them have had years of practice.

01 Before you sit down

Four decisions,
made in advance

  • Somewhere that isn’t your desk. A desk with a file open on it sets the genre. Walk somewhere, or sit side by side.
  • No file in front of you. You are gathering, not verifying. If you already know what you expect to hear you will hear it.
  • Fifteen minutes, and say so. A bounded conversation is easier to enter than an open-ended one.
  • Write in their words, not yours. Decide this before you start, because summarizing is a reflex.
Not a substitute for assessment. This conversation is one informal source among several. It goes alongside inventories and observation — it does not replace them.

One more thing to settle in advance: you are asking for four answers, not four sentences. If a student gives you one real answer in fifteen minutes, that is a successful conversation. Trying to get all four is how you end up rushing past the one that mattered.

    02 When it stalls

    “I don’t know.”

    Which is usually true, and almost never final.

    “I don’t know” is a legitimate answer to a question nobody has ever asked you before. It is also what students say when the question feels like a test with a right answer. Three moves, in order of how often they work:

    1. 1
      Narrow it to two options

      “Okay — would you rather be inside or outside?” A forced choice is far easier than a blank field, and the answer is real data even when it is picked reluctantly.

    2. 2
      Ask about the past instead of the future

      “What’s the best job you’ve seen someone do?” or “what was the best day you had at your last placement?” Memory is answerable in a way that aspiration is not.

    3. 3
      Ask what they’d avoid

      Students who cannot name a preference can almost always name a dealbreaker. “What’s the worst job you can imagine?” gets you the same information in negative.

    The one to watch for

    A student who answers everything instantly and agreeably may be telling you what they think you want. Test it once: “What would you say if your mom weren’t here?” or, more gently, “is that what you want, or what you think you’re supposed to say?”

    Asked without accusation, that question gets an honest answer surprisingly often.

    And if nothing lands this week, stop. Fifteen minutes that end in “let’s try again Thursday” cost you nothing. Fifteen minutes that end in a fabricated profile cost you a year of goals built on it.

    03 Writing it down

    Verbatim, attributed, dated

    Three things go in the record for this conversation, the same three that go in for a formal instrument: the name of what you did, the date, and the information gained. “SPIN conversation, 9/18, student reports…” is a citable source. “Student expressed interest in animal care” floating unattributed in a PLAAFP is not.

    Summarized “Student reports sensory sensitivities in busy environments.” True, unusable, and no employer conversation starts from it.
    Verbatim “I don’t like when it’s loud and everybody’s talking at me.” Tells you which shift to visit and what to ask the supervisor.

    Then give it back. Read your notes to the student and ask whether you got it right. It takes two minutes, it corrects the errors you cannot see, and it is the moment the profile stops being a document about them.

    Try this week

    One student. Fifteen minutes. No file.

    Pick the student whose plan you are least sure about — not the easiest one to talk to. Ask the four questions in their plain form. Write the answers in their words.

    Then read it back and ask if you got it right.
