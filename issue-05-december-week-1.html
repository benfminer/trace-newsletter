Every transition program has a list of students who are “working.” Run down that list honestly and it usually contains four or five different things, only some of which are the thing the research measured.

This matters more than it sounds. If a program counts volunteering, work crews, unpaid internships, and actual jobs in the same column, then the column says the program is doing well — and the outcome data will disagree two years later, for reasons nobody will be able to reconstruct.

01 This Week’s Focus

Paid, competitive, integrated

California’s CIE definition — a joint CDE, DOR and DDS project — sets three tests. An experience has to pass all three, and most of what gets called work experience fails at least one.

  1. 1
    Compensation

    At or above minimum wage — the higher of federal, state, or local — and not less than what coworkers without disabilities earn in comparable positions at the same employer. Same benefits, too.

  2. 2
    Integration

    A setting where the employee interacts with coworkers without disabilities to the same extent as anybody else in that job. Supervisors and paid support staff do not count toward that interaction — a job coach is not a coworker.

  3. 3
    Advancement

    Comparable opportunity to move up. A position built to be permanent and terminal fails this one even when it pays properly.

“He has a work experience” is not a claim about anything. Ask who signs the check.

It is the fastest single question on the list

California adds a line to this that is worth having ready when somebody argues a particular student is not a candidate: participation in, or preparation for, competitive integrated employment gets the highest priority for working-age people with intellectual and developmental disabilities — regardless of the severity of their disabilities. That is state policy language, not aspiration.

Self-employment counts. The California definition explicitly includes self-employment and microenterprise as valid CIE, provided the income is comparable to that of self-employed people without disabilities in similar occupations. It is not limited to traditional wage work.

Interactive · eight experiences, one question each

Does this count?

Eight students, eight things they did this year. For each one, decide whether it is the experience the 5.1× predictor measures — a paid job at exit — or something else. Then read what the definition says.

1 Priya A six-week unpaid internship at a veterinary clinic, eight hours a week. Glowing supervisor report.

2 Marcus Twelve hours a week at a grocery store, hired by the store, on their payroll at minimum wage.

3 Dani Runs the coffee cart in the school front office, five hours a week, paid a stipend by the district.

4 André On a work crew that cleans three local businesses, paid subminimum wage under a 14(c) certificate.

5 Jasmine Volunteers at the food bank every Friday, all year, and has never missed one.

6 Theo Paid summer placement at a hotel through a WIOA youth program, minimum wage, wage subsidized by the workforce board.

7 Bree Job shadowed three different employers this fall, five hours at each.

8 Luis Twenty hours a week at his uncle’s landscaping business. Real work, real money, paid in cash.

All eight are composites. Every one of them is a shape that shows up on a real caseload, and the two that trip people up most are three and eight.

Matching the definition

    The tests are California’s CIE criteria and the federal wage rules. The judgment calls at the edges — the coffee cart, the cash job — are judgment calls, and the explanations say so rather than pretending the definition settles them.

    The two that are genuinely hard

    The school-based enterprise. A student-run coffee cart or print shop is real work with real customers, and it is a good on-ramp for a student not yet ready for a community placement. It also sits in the exploration-to-preparation band of the continuum, its evidence base is practitioner consensus rather than outcome data, and transfer to a real workplace is not guaranteed. The failure mode is specific and common: the enterprise stops being a stepping stone and becomes the only placement the program offers.

    The family business. Luis is doing more real work than anybody else on the list. But cash means no payroll record, no work permit, nothing DOR can build on, and nothing that survives as documentation after he exits. The experience is real; the record is not. Getting him onto the books is a smaller ask than finding him a new job, and it is worth making.

    On 14(c)

    Subminimum wage fails the compensation test outright, so a 14(c) work crew is not competitive integrated employment however good the crew is. California moved on this early — AB 1041 (2013) barred new 14(c) certificates for Regional Center-funded programs, years ahead of most states. If a student is still on one, that is a conversation, not a placement.

    Try this week

    Recount your working list.

    Take whatever list you keep of students with work experience and split it into two columns: paid by a community employer, and everything else. Do not remove anything from the second column — it is all worth doing.

    Just find out how long the first column actually is.
