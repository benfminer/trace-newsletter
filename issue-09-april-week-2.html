Fourteen days
and a signature.

The monthly said the referral is an auditable element rather than a courtesy. This week is the invitation itself — the three parts it has to have, and the one question about it that most published guidance answers wrong for your students.

Following up on Issue 09 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

Somebody on your team has invited a Department of Rehabilitation counselor to an IEP meeting. Probably several times. It felt like a professional courtesy at the time — an email, a date, a hope that somebody comes. That is the version of this move almost everybody has been running, and it is not the version the law describes.

The monthly made the argument in outline: agency linkage is one of the four required IDEA transition elements in the IEP, and it is checked by document review. This week takes the invitation apart. Three testable parts, a correction about whose signature belongs on it, and a federal regulation that turns two separate plans into one obligation. None of it is difficult. All of it is the difference between a referral you remember making and a referral that exists.

01 This Week’s Focus

Audited,
not surveyed

Two of the four federal transition indicators sound similar and behave nothing alike. The difference between them is the hinge this whole week turns on, and it is a distinction most staff have never been told.

The post-school outcomes indicator is a performance indicator. It measures what former IEP students are actually doing one year after they leave: enrolled in higher education, competitively employed, or in some other postsecondary training. States set their own targets for it, and the data comes from finding people after they are gone and asking them.

Indicator 13 is a compliance indicator, and states are expected to meet it at 100%. Not a target somebody negotiates — a standard, meaning every IEP for a transition-age student is expected to contain all federally required transition components. And the method is the part that matters here: Indicator 13 is audited, not surveyed. It rests on IEP document review rather than on post-school outcome data.

Indicator 13 is audited, not surveyed; it is based on IEP document review rather than post-school outcome data.

The wiki, on the four federal transition indicators

Read those two sentences next to each other and the consequence falls out on its own. What is written is what is measured. A reviewer opens the document and looks for four things: measurable postsecondary goals, transition services, course of study, and agency linkage — the invitation to relevant outside agencies when their participation may be needed. The reviewer does not know what you did. The reviewer knows what the file says you did.

  • An undocumented referral is not a weaker referral — for the purposes of a document review it is an absent one. There is no partial credit for a phone call nobody wrote down.
  • The element is the invitation, not the attendance. Nobody can compel an outside agency to send a person to your meeting. What is on you is inviting them, when their participation may be needed, and recording that you did.
  • It also outlives you. Compliance is the reason this is enforceable; the reason it is worth doing is that the next case manager inherits the document and not your memory of the spring.
Compliance is a floor. IDEA sets minimums, not quality ceilings. An IEP can contain every required element and still be a thin plan, and the wiki says so directly: the law sets floors, and what a high-quality transition IEP looks like in practice is an open question rather than a legal specification. Meeting the standard is not the same as doing the work. It is, however, the part somebody will check.

The invitation has three parts

CTI’s Interagency Collaboration Assessment includes a dimension for IEP meeting notification and participation, and its Well-Developed descriptor is unusually specific for an instrument of this kind. It does not say “the counselor is meaningfully engaged.” It names three things that either happened or did not.

  1. 1
    At least 14 days advance notice

    A minimum, published as a number so it can be checked. The interval is not politeness — it is roughly what a counselor carrying a regional caseload needs in order to protect a block of a morning. Under it, acceptance stops being a decision and becomes luck.

  2. 2
    Signed consent and information release, obtained first

    An outside agency cannot be brought into a meeting about a student, or receive that student’s records, without written permission. Note the ordering the descriptor implies: consent, then invitation. This is the part that cannot be improvised in the room, and it is the part most reliably skipped when the invitation went out late.

  3. 3
    Arrangements for input when they cannot attend in person

    VR counselors can participate in person, virtually, or by submitting information prior to the meeting. All three count. Which means a counselor who cannot make the date is not a failed invitation — unless nobody asked for the written version.

Where the wiki says most programs actually are

Many school divisions operate at the Developing stage on this dimension: the counselor is invited, but without sufficient advance notice and without a participation fallback. That is a precise diagnosis and a flattering one. Nobody in that description is neglecting anybody. They are doing part one of three and calling it the whole move. The monthly rates all seven dimensions →

The three parts are not independent, either, which is why they are worth running as a sequence rather than a checklist. Late notice is what produces missing consent, because the release becomes a thing to chase rather than a thing you already had. Missing consent is what makes attendance impossible even when the counselor is free. And a missing fallback is what converts a perfectly reasonable decline into a blank space in the record.

One number, correctly labeled. The same instrument sets its Well-Developed referral descriptor at greater than 75 percent of students with disabilities — IEP and 504 alike — connected to VR and Pre-ETS before exiting school, with the bottom rung below 25 percent. Those are rubric thresholds: a standard a program rates itself against. They are not a measurement of how many students anywhere actually connect to adult services. Nobody has that figure, and a number quoted as one should be asked for its source.

Interactive · one invitation, five things the record has to show

Audit the invitation

One student, 20 years old, one annual IEP, one DOR transition counselor invited to it. You make five decisions about how that invitation was handled — and the readout reconstructs the file entry a document review would open, saying what it can see and what it cannot. There is no score and no answer key. Every option is a version somebody runs, and each one is read back for what it leaves in the record.

The student in this exercise

Twenty years old, still in the program, no DOR connection

He is not exiting this year, which is deliberate: CTI is explicit that referrals should be initiated early rather than in the final year, when there is not enough time left to establish eligibility and services. He has an IEP, so he is potentially eligible for Pre-ETS with no VR eligibility determination required. And he is over 18, which changes the answer to one of the five questions below.

Composite. No student on anybody’s caseload is described here.

Loading the five decisions. With scripting off, the exercise is this: take one invitation you have actually sent and answer five questions about it on paper. One: how many days before the meeting did the invitation go out? Two: whose signature is on the consent and information release, and was it signed before the invitation or after? Three: what did you offer for the case where the counselor cannot attend in person? Four: what happened when they could not come? Five: what ended up written in the IEP — who was invited, on what date, by what method, what the response was, and what they contributed? Then read your five answers as a stranger would, with none of your memory of the year attached, because that is the reading a document review performs.

      Who signs it

      Here is the sentence that appears in nearly every piece of published guidance on inviting a VR counselor to an IEP meeting, including the guidance this vault records: invite the counselor with parent permission. It is in the school-staff column and it is in the counselor column. It is also written for minors, in a state whose transition rules are not California’s, and for your students it is wrong.

      The correction, stated plainly

      Educational rights transfer to the student at 18. After transfer the school communicates directly with the student on all IEP matters. The parent retains no independent right to receive records, to attend meetings without the student’s consent, or to make decisions. The right to sign consent belongs to the adult student — schools can no longer obtain parental consent for IEP implementation, and only the student can provide it. For an 18-to-22-year-old, the student signs the consent and the information release that let a DOR counselor into the meeting.

      This is not a technicality with a workaround. A release with a parent’s signature on it, for a 20-year-old, is a form in the file with the wrong name on it — and it is usually nobody’s deliberate decision. It is a habit that started when the student was 15 and never got interrupted, because the meeting kept happening and the signature kept being available.

      What the correction does not mean is that the family leaves. This is the part that gets misread in both directions: families assume they lose all access, or schools stop inviting them entirely. The accurate rule is narrower and better than either.

      • Parents are still invited — the age-of-majority provision does not exclude them from the special education process. The school continues to invite them to IEP meetings as interested parties.
      • The adult student holds the veto — and it is the student, not the school and not the parent, who has the right to exclude a parent from the meeting if they choose. In practice this is almost never exercised. It is still whose call it is.
      • So the family is looped in with the student’s agreement — which is a different sentence from “the family needs to be on board,” and a different one from “the family is out.” Ask the student, then bring everybody in.

      There is a reason to be careful about the framing beyond accuracy. The fifth Pre-ETS category is instruction in self-advocacy, and the whole argument of this month’s issue is that the next system requires a student who can identify themselves and ask. A consent form is one of the few places in a school year where that is not a lesson but a live decision with a real object attached. Handing it to a parent by reflex teaches the opposite of the curriculum.

      Know what is actually in place. Some students will have a conservator, and California has a limited conservatorship specific to adults with developmental disabilities that grants authority over some domains while preserving rights in all the others — a limited conservator may hold authority over residence and not over educational decisions. So the question is never “is there a conservatorship,” it is which domains it covers. Supported decision-making, where the student keeps full legal authority and designates people to help, is the least restrictive of the arrangements and requires no court at all.

      Two plans, one obligation

      One more citation, and it is the sharpest thing in the month’s material because it points the requirement in the other direction. Everything above is about what the IEP has to contain. 34 C.F.R. § 361.46(d) is about what the VR plan has to contain: the Individualized Plan for Employment must be coordinated with the IEP.

      That is a federal VR regulation rather than a recommendation from a toolkit, and it changes the character of the ask. When you invite a DOR counselor to an IEP meeting you are not requesting a favor from an agency with its own priorities. You are supplying the document their document is required to be coordinated with. The obligation runs both ways, and only one of the two parties usually knows that.

      What that means for the goals page

      Coordination is not a paragraph somebody writes at the end. It shows up in whether the postsecondary employment goal in the IEP and the employment outcome on the IPE are describing the same future, and whether the services underneath them overlap, contradict, or leave a gap. Issue 03 modeled the goal line with support from DOR back in October; this is the regulation that makes that phrase load-bearing rather than decorative.

      Worth being precise about what is not claimed here. Coordination is not delegation. Schools cannot hand IEP transition obligations to VR: WIOA and IDEA create complementary mandates that are additive rather than substitutable, and a coordinated plan does not become a shared workload. It becomes a plan two agencies can both act on without duplicating each other — and duplicated effort is the first of the four documented barriers to interagency collaboration, ahead of competing demands, attitudes and stereotypes, and staffing shortages.

      What the evidence supports, exactly. Interagency collaboration is a Research-Based predictor of post-school success, for employment outcomes only — not education, not independent living. Predictors are correlational and come with no guidance on dosage, timing or delivery, and career and technical education is the only Evidence-Based predictor among the 23. The compliance argument on this page does not rest on the evidence argument, which is convenient: the invitation is required whether or not it is proven, and the honest version of the research claim is narrower than the enthusiasm it usually travels with.

      Try this week

      Pull one invitation and read it as a stranger.

      Take an IEP where somebody invited a DOR counselor this year and find the three parts in the record: how many days of notice went out, whose signature is on the consent and information release, and what was offered when in-person attendance was not possible. If any of the three is missing, you now know which one to fix first — and it is almost always the notice, because the other two follow it.

      Then check the name on the signature line. If your student is over 18 and somebody else signed, that is not a filing error to correct quietly. It is a conversation with the student that should have happened already, and this is a good week to have it.
