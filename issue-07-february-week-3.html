01 This Week’s Focus

Three tiers,
in order

My Next Move is listed under career interests and abilities in NTACT:C’s directory of sample transition assessment tools, free column — a name on a good list, which is a smaller claim than a recommendation. O*NET appears in the same directory, and O*NET is the one with a place in the decision tree: Tier 1.

  1. 1
    Standard assessments

    Interest inventories, career cluster surveys, O*NET, workplace readiness. Cheap, fast, and the right first move for most students. A Tier 1 result is a list of things to go try.

  2. 2
    Functional vocational evaluation

    Situational assessment, job sampling, community-based work experience. Reached when Tier 1 has not surfaced an interest, and the tier teams skip most often: it costs transportation, staff time, and a site that will have you.

  3. 3
    Person-centered planning

    Discovering ME! and the person-centered team process, reached when Tiers 1 and 2 together have not uncovered a matching career interest. The profile is built out of what people already know about the student, not out of what the student can report on demand.

Direction matters as much as order. You do not start at Tier 3 because a student has a significant disability; you arrive there because the tiers above came back empty. And you do not stay in Tier 1 for four years because it is the tier you have a login for.

Rather than asking “what do you want to do?”, Discovery observes people in real settings.

The premise the bottom two tiers rest on
Why the top tier misses some students. Generic career exploration tools assume baseline work experience and self-knowledge that many students with significant disabilities have not yet had the opportunity to develop. An empty inventory often reports an absence of experience, not an absence of preference.

What Tier 3 actually costs

Know the footprint before you name it at an IEP meeting in April. VCU’s Center on Transition Innovations recommends three to six months of preparation before a district runs Discovering ME! at all, and the support team is five to eight people — student, family, and school staff all required.

Year one is three meetings. Meeting One runs about two hours and is an interview with everyone who knows the student, organized into a four-square: strengths and contributions, interests, conditions for success, challenges. The top half is what you build opportunities out of; the bottom half shapes how they get supported.

Where the career goal comes from

Meeting Three introduces all 17 career clusters and the team narrows to a top three — the same output a Tier 1 survey produces, reached a completely different way. Nobody asked the student to compare seventeen things alone on a screen. After year one the team meets twice a year, and it runs until the student reaches their employment goals. There is no fixed endpoint. Week 2 is where this gets written down →

Two honest notes: Discovering ME! is a Virginia development a California district would have to adopt deliberately, and its evidence level is expert opinion. A well-specified process, not a tested one.

Interactive · four situations, one call each

Diagnose the tier

Four students, four sets of circumstances, and the same question every time: which tier does this team need to be working in? There is no score and nobody is being ranked. Each call gets an argument back — including the calls the analysis would not have made.

With scripting off, here are the four situations to work through on paper. One: a 19-year-old with three interest inventories in four years, all agreeing on culinary, who has never worked a shift in a kitchen. Two: an 18-year-old who has worked through a free interest site twice with an adult beside him and said “I don’t know” both times, and who has never held a job, done a shadow, or toured a workplace. Three: a 20-year-old with complex communication needs whose two situational assessments produced pages of staff observation and no stated preference, and whose team has never interviewed the people who know her best. Four: a 21-year-old who wanted to work with animals for two years, did a shadow at a veterinary clinic in January, came back saying it is not what he wants, and is written up in the file as having regressed. The three tiers are standard assessments, functional vocational evaluation, and person-centered planning.

Four situations, no answer key

      The three tiers are the VCU/CTI transition assessment decision tree. All four students are composites written to make the tiers do something. The point of the exercise is the one the monthly ended on: a student who cannot answer an inventory has not failed the inventory — they have told you which tier to work in.

      The next experiment

      Every tier ends the same way: with something to go try. So when a student says “I don’t know what I want to do,” do not ask the question more slowly — name one thing to try in the next three weeks and put a date on it. Without a date it is a suggestion, and suggestions never reach the log. Four of the eight career exploration methods cost an afternoon rather than a placement:

      • A workplace tour — watch a business running, and talk to people doing the work.
      • An informational interview — one structured conversation with somebody in a field the student named.
      • A job shadow — a full or partial workday with an employee; a realistic job preview, and the closest thing to a Tier 2 answer you can get in a day.
      • A career fair — typically 20 to 30 businesses in one room; the most exposure per staff hour on this list.

      In California there is funding attached to exactly this, and the bar is lower than most teams assume. DOR’s Pre-ETS Category 1, Job Exploration Counseling is available to students aged 14 to 21 with an IEP or a 504 Plan, and no full VR eligibility determination is required. Its student activities include informational interviews, job shadowing, workplace tours, and internships — the same menu, funded.

      The unpaid hour caps, before anyone asks

      Under FLSA, unpaid experiences are capped by purpose: career exploration, 5 hours per job; career assessment, 90 hours; work-related training, 120 hours. Five hours is a morning, which is the point — a job shadow is not meant to need a negotiation.

      What none of this is. Our outcome evidence for awareness- and exploration-tier activity is thin. What is documented is what each tier is for — not that a tour or a shadow predicts a job. Run them because a plan needs testing, and say so plainly at the table.

      Try this week

      Name one thing. Put a date on it.

      Pick the student whose file has carried the same career goal the longest. Offer one experiment — a tour, an interview, a shadow — inside the next three weeks, and write the date where somebody else can see it.

      Then name the tier out loud at the next meeting. “Tier 1 has not gotten us anywhere in three years, so we are moving to Tier 2” is a decision. “He still isn’t sure” is not.
