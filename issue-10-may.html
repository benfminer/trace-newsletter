Do you know
where last year’s
leavers landed?

It is the question the whole federal accountability system is built to answer — and the one most often left unanswered. This month: how to read Indicator 14 as a ladder rather than a gate, which predictors are actually research-based and which are only promising, and what to carry into fall. Ten issues in, this is the last one.

For nine issues this newsletter has argued about what to do. Assess before you plan. Write an IEP that would still make sense to a stranger. Let the student run the meeting. Get them paid work. Teach the soft skills that actually end jobs. Invite the agency early, in writing, fourteen days out. May is the month the argument stops and the arithmetic starts.

Back in August we called Indicator 14 the scoreboard — where students land one year out. In May it is better read as feedback. The federal accountability metric for post-school outcomes is not, in practice, a grade on your program. It is the only systematic answer anybody collects to the question in the headline, and its whole original purpose, in OSEP’s framing, was improving educational results and outcomes rather than compliance reporting. Practitioners are meant to treat indicator data as a planning tool. Most programs treat it as a submission.

3 years of data, not one — and all four indicators, not just 14 The documented cycle is a five-step one: collect data from each transition indicator for the past three years, analyze it as a team of stakeholders, plan, implement, review. Multi-year review is what makes trend recognition possible; a single year is noise with a percentage sign on it. And Indicators 1, 2, 13 and 14 are meant to be read together — graduation, dropout, IEP compliance, and outcomes.
What the number is looking at. Indicator 14 counts youth who are no longer in secondary school, had an IEP in effect when they left, and within one year of leaving were in higher education, competitively employed, or in other postsecondary education or training or other employment. It is a rearview mirror: the students in this year’s data left a year ago. Contact rates are often below 100 percent, so non-response bias is real; some responses come from family members who may not know the student’s actual employment status; and one year is an arbitrary window that may not describe where somebody is at year three or five. It is still the most consistently collected national dataset on post-school outcomes there is.
01 This Month’s Focus

A ladder,
not a gate

Everything this year serves one outcome, and there are two instruments for checking on it. Your Indicator 14 outcomes tell you whether it worked. The predictors tell you what to change. Those are different jobs, and running them together is where most year-end data conversations go wrong.

Start with the shape of the measure, because it is the part most often misread. 14A, 14B and 14C are not three separate groups of students. They are three nested counts of the same leavers, each one wider than the last:

  • 14A — enrolled in higher education. The most restrictive of the three, and the smallest number you will report.
  • 14B — enrolled in higher education or competitively employed. Everybody in 14A is also in 14B.
  • 14C — enrolled in higher education or competitively employed or in some other postsecondary education or training or in other employment. Everybody in 14B is also in 14C.

So 14C is the broadest category and produces the highest percentage, and it is the one states report most prominently. Which means the direction of the alarm is the opposite of what it sounds like. A high 14C is good. It is the widest available measure of having landed somewhere at all. A low 14C is the signal that something has gone wrong, because its complement — the students not counted anywhere in it — is the group that finished the year in neither school, nor training, nor paid work of any kind.

14C is the widest measure of “landed somewhere” — not the bad outcome. Move students up into 14A or 14B, and make sure nobody finishes the year outside 14C entirely.

Issue 01, August — the first thing this newsletter said about the number

Read that way, the three rates stop being a gate you either pass or fail and become a ladder. There are five places a student can land: higher education, competitive employment, other postsecondary education or training, other employment, or nowhere counted at all. Two of those five sit inside 14C and nowhere else — and the students in them are the most interesting people in your data, because they are working or learning and are still below the bar that drives 14B.

The band a single
number hides

The definitions do a lot of quiet work here, and they are worth reading slowly once a year, because they decide which rung a real student lands on.

  1. 1
    Competitive employment — the 14B bar

    At least 90 cumulative days of employment, not necessarily consecutive; typically 20+ hours a week, defined as an average of 20 hours across those 90 days; paid at or above minimum wage. Sheltered employment and sub-minimum-wage work do not count. Supported employment in integrated settings does. Note what that threshold actually is: a floor, low enough that very part-time minimum-wage work counts as a success.

  2. 2
    Other employment — inside 14C, outside 14B

    Worked for pay or was self-employed for at least 90 days at any point in the year since leaving. Family businesses and entrepreneurships count. There is no minimum hours-per-week requirement. Below-minimum-wage sheltered work still does not count. This is the category that captures youth who are working — part-time, seasonal, gig — and have not reached the competitive bar.

  3. 3
    Higher education — the 14A bar

    A two-year or four-year degree program at a community or technical college or a university, with at least one full semester completed, online courses and credit-bearing independent study included. Remedial and noncredit enrollment counts; enrollment is what matters, not credit type. Students with intellectual and developmental disabilities attending inclusive college programs are counted here.

  4. 4
    Other postsecondary education or training — inside 14C, outside 14B

    Adult and continuing education, high school completion programs, apprenticeships, short-term WIOA training, Job Corps, vocational and technical schools under two years, AmeriCorps, day support and prevocational programs, compensatory education. A semester-equivalent is defined broadly: any formal program with an application or approval process that is at least in part skill-building, even a ten-week welding class.

What that means for the debrief

Two students can both sit inside 14C and be in completely different situations — one taking a ten-week class, one working thirty hours a week for less than minimum wage in a sheltered setting, who is not counted in the employment half at all. A single 14C percentage cannot tell those apart. The rung-by-rung count can, and the difference between 14C and 14B is exactly the size of that band.

The predictors,
tiered honestly

If Indicator 14 is whether it worked, the predictors are what to change. There are 23 of them, drawn from the 2021 Mazzotti review and maintained by NTACT:C, and they are sorted into three tiers by how much correlational evidence stands behind each. The tiers are not decoration — a program that treats them as one flat list will end up defending a claim the research does not make.

Of the ones this year touched, here is the honest sorting:

  • Research-based — paid work experience · self-determination and self-advocacy · self-care and independent living skills · inclusion in general education · interagency collaboration · social skills.
  • Promising (thinner evidence, same direction) — career awareness · parent involvement.
  • Evidence-based, the strongest tier of all — career and technical education. It is the only one of the 23 that reaches it.

Two of those need a note attached, because they are the two most often quoted slightly wrong. Career awareness is promising, not research-based — which does not mean stop doing it, it means do not cite it as though it carried the same weight as paid work. And there is no predictor called “family involvement.” The 23 contain parent involvement, which is promising, for education and employment; and parent expectations, which is research-based, for employment only. Those are different variables with different evidence behind them, and the difference is worth keeping, because it is the difference between a warm-feeling claim and one that survives being asked for a citation.

What a predictor is not. Predictors are correlational, not causal. A predictor is an in-school experience that statistically correlates with better post-school outcomes across multiple studies. It does not guarantee an outcome and carries no guidance on dosage, timing, or delivery method. Under ESSA, correlational research sits at Tier 3, promising evidence — it does not meet What Works Clearinghouse standards for effectiveness. The predictor tiers are also a separate system from the evidence-based-practice tiers, so a predictor labeled “evidence-based” and a practice labeled the same thing were not judged by the same rules. The 2021 review even documents negative findings inside the top tier: CTE participation was associated with decreased employment outcomes for females with specific learning disabilities and other health impairments. These lists tell you what to strengthen. They do not tell you what will cause a result.

The year,
honestly

It would be tidy to claim the ten issues were a predictor checklist all along. They were not, quite, and the un-tidy version is more useful in a debrief than the tidy one.

Most of what we covered does map onto a predictor — self-determination, paid work, social skills, career awareness, family, interagency collaboration. Two things we spent whole months on — assessment and IEP quality — are not predictors at all; they are the machinery that delivers the rest, which is why they got months. And two research-based predictors never got an issue: inclusion in general education and self-care and independent living skills. Both are worth a look this summer, and the second one is conspicuous in a program serving 18-to-22-year-olds.

Program improvement in one sentence, then: find your weakest outcome, look at which predictor your program does least well, and make that next year’s focus. You cannot move a number you never look at.

Interactive · five landing places, three rates

Move one up

A caseload rather than a student. Set how many of last year’s leavers landed on each rung, then promote one and watch which of the three rates actually moves. Some promotions lift two rates. Some lift one and leave the other two exactly where they were. That asymmetry is the whole reason to read Indicator 14 as a ladder instead of a gate, and it is not visible in a single reported percentage.

Before you start

These numbers are made up

The starting caseload is an illustration, not a measurement of any program. The definitions behind each rung are real — the 90-day thresholds, the 20-hour average, the minimum-wage floor, the one-full-semester rule — and so is the arithmetic. Put your own counts in if you have them; nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere.

Two tracks, education and employment. A student moves up within their own track, and a competitively employed student who enrolls in higher education moves across into 14A.

Loading the ladder. With scripting off, the exercise is this. Take twenty leavers and sort them into five places: higher education (counted in 14A, 14B and 14C), competitive employment (14B and 14C), other postsecondary education or training (14C only), other employment (14C only), and counted in none of the three. Now move one student up one rung and recompute. A student who goes from other employment to competitive employment lifts 14B and leaves 14C exactly where it was, because they were already inside it. A student who goes from nothing to other employment lifts 14C alone. A student who goes from other training to higher education lifts both 14A and 14B, and again leaves 14C flat. And a competitively employed student who enrolls in college lifts 14A only. Four kinds of progress; four different fingerprints in the data.

      02 Practice Spotlight

      The year-end
      debrief

      Forty-five minutes with your team before everyone scatters for summer. Not a data review in the sense of a slide deck; a working meeting whose output is one written sentence about the fall. The documented version of this is a five-step cycle, and it is worth running in that order even when you have only the one meeting.

      1. 1
        Collect — three years, four indicators

        Gather the data from each transition indicator for the past three years. Not this year alone. Multi-year review is what enables trend recognition; one year cannot tell a change from a wobble. Indicator 1 (graduation), Indicator 2 (dropout), Indicator 13 (IEP transition compliance, audited by document review and held to 100 percent) and Indicator 14 (outcomes) describe four different parts of the same pipeline.

      2. 2
        Analyze — as a team, not alone

        Review the data with a group of relevant stakeholders and look for trends, strengths, and areas needing improvement. The team matters more than the tool: assemble people who saw the students, because the ones who can explain an anomaly are usually the ones who were in the room when it happened. Then look at the ladder, not only the headline percentage — where did the students inside 14C but outside 14B actually land?

      3. 3
        Plan — and know the evidence base first

        Develop an action plan to sustain strengths and address needs. There is a documented precondition on this step that gets skipped: the stakeholders doing the planning must be familiar with the predictors. A plan written by a team that has not read the predictor list tends to reach for whatever was discussed most recently, rather than for what the evidence points at.

      4. 4
        Implement — with names and dates on it

        Execute, and check in periodically to confirm the responsible parties are on track and have the resources they need. Specificity is the whole game here: each step needs a timeline, a designated staff member, and data that shows it happened.

      5. 5
        Review — and feed the next cycle

        Review progress regularly, adjust, and keep collecting annual indicator data so the next round has three years to look at. This is the step that turns a debrief into a cycle rather than an event.

      The three questions, if forty-five minutes is all you have

      Where did this year’s leavers go? The honest version, name by name, sorted onto the five rungs. Which predictor does the program do least well? Not which one you like least — which one you could not produce evidence for. What is the one change? Single, concrete, written down somewhere you will see it in August. One honest debrief beats a binder of data nobody opens.

      Two known limits to say out loud in the room. Response rates are frequently below 100 percent, so the students you could not reach are missing from the number, and there is no reason to think they are missing at random. And the data describes students who left a year ago — it is feedback on the program as it was two years back, not as it is now. Neither is a reason to discount it. Both are reasons to state the number with its denominator attached rather than on its own.

      Predictor Implementation School/District Self-Assessment

      The NTACT:C Predictor Implementation School/District Self-Assessment — the PISA — turns the 23-predictor list into an audit a team can actually run. It asks which predictors are present, at what quality level, and where the gaps are, which is precisely the input step 3 of the debrief needs.

      Free, from NTACT:C. transitionta.org →

      Two things about it are worth knowing before you open it. The first is that it covers all 23 predictors, not a short list — so it will ask you about things this newsletter never got to, which is the point of using somebody else’s instrument rather than your own memory. The second is that it is collaborative by design, and the process is documented:

      • Form a stakeholder team — transition specialists, special and general educators, CTE staff, school counselors. It is not a solo administrative exercise.
      • Rate individually, then reach consensus — each member rates each predictor, then the team shares ratings and evidence and argues toward agreement. The disagreements are the useful part; a predictor two people score four rungs apart is a predictor nobody has a shared definition of.
      • Pick one or two predictors — not eight, not all 23 — and write clear, measurable, time-bound goals for them.
      • Build an action plan and monitor it — each step with a timeline, a responsible person, and data showing completion. Meet to check progress, surface barriers, and mark the small wins, because systemic change moves slowly enough that momentum has to be manufactured.

      Run it in the year-end debrief and the meeting has a spine. Your lowest score is a reasonable candidate for next year’s focus — with one caveat, which is the same caveat as everywhere else on this page. The goal of the rating is to identify strengths and areas for improvement, not to assign blame, and a low rating is a starting point rather than a verdict.

      One gap, named. The PISA rates implementation; it does not tell you how much of a predictor is enough. Nothing in the predictor literature does — there is no documented dosage, timing, or delivery guidance attached to any of the 23. So the honest output of a PISA cycle is “we do this one thinly and we are going to do more of it, and here is how we will know,” not a target number.

      Quick tip for May — and for the year

      Pull the data. Pick one predictor.

      Before the building empties, get three years of Indicator 14 in front of a team that saw the students, and sort last year’s leavers onto the five rungs rather than into one percentage. Then choose a single predictor — the one you could not produce evidence for — and write one measurable goal about it where you will find it in August.

      And write down the name of one student who ended up somewhere good this year. Not for the file. That one is for you, and it is the thing no indicator was built to hold.

      Ten issues, August to May. The argument underneath all of them was a single one: that transition outcomes are produced by ordinary decisions made early, in rooms nobody photographs — a referral sent in October, a sentence written so a stranger could act on it, a meeting handed to the student whose life it is about. Indicator 14 is where those decisions show up a year later, blurred and late and counted. It is worth looking at anyway. Thank you for reading.
