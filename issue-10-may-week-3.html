01 This Week’s Focus

Rate it,
then pick one

The Predictor Implementation School/District Self-Assessment — the PISA — is NTACT:C’s instrument for turning the 23-predictor list into something a school or a district can audit itself against. It is free, at transitionta.org →, along with everything else NTACT:C publishes.

Two facts about it are worth having before you open the file. The first is that it covers all 23 predictors. This newsletter touched six or seven of them across the year; the instrument will ask you about program of study, work study, occupational courses, travel skills, student support, youth autonomy, technology skills and the rest, and that is the entire reason to use somebody else’s list instead of your own memory. A list you write yourself contains exactly the things you were already thinking about.

The second is that it is collaborative by design, and the collaboration is not decoration. The documented process runs in six steps:

  1. 1
    Establish a stakeholder team

    Transition specialists, special and general educators, CTE staff, school counselors. The PISA is described as a collaborative tool, not a solo administrative exercise — and general education and CTE are on that list for a reason, because two of the 23 predictors live almost entirely on their side of the building.

  2. 2
    Complete the assessment

    Teams can work individually — each member rates each predictor, then converges — or as a group, discussing each predictor and the degree of implementation together. For each predictor the instrument supplies definitions and characteristics, which is how a team ends up with a common language rather than six private ones.

  3. 3
    Reach consensus on the ratings

    Where members rated individually first, they share ratings and evidence, then work toward agreement. The stated goal is to identify areas of strength and areas for improvement — not to assign blame. Hold onto that sentence; it is the difference between a rating meeting and a performance review, and staff can tell which one they are in within about ninety seconds.

  4. 4
    Set measurable goals

    Select one or two predictors. Write clear, measurable, time-bound goals for each. NTACT:C’s own PISA instructions include a goal framework and an action plan template, and CTI’s guide on using state indicator data for long-term transition planning offers action plan templates organized by goal duration.

  5. 5
    Build and execute the action plan

    Specificity is the requirement: each step gets a timeline, a designated responsible staff member, and data that shows completion. A step with no name on it is a wish, and a step with no data attached cannot be reviewed in November by anyone who was not there in June.

  6. 6
    Monitor and sustain

    Meet regularly, check progress, surface barriers, and share the incremental wins with stakeholders. That last clause reads like nicety and is not: systemic change moves slowly enough that momentum has to be manufactured on purpose, and acknowledging mini-victories is the documented way to do it.

Where this sits in the debrief

Week 2’s five-step data cycle has a planning step with a documented precondition attached — the stakeholders writing the plan have to be familiar with the predictors. The PISA is how a team gets familiar and produces a plan in the same sitting. Run the outcomes first, so you know which way the numbers point, and the predictors second, so you know what to change. Interactive · six predictors, two raters, one pick

Rate six, argue about one

The rating step, in miniature. For each of six predictors, set how well you think your program implements it — and then set what you think a colleague who works with the same students would say. The tool does not care about either number on its own. It cares about the distance between them, because that distance is the thing an individual rating is designed to expose and a group conversation is designed to hide. Then pick one predictor for fall and see the goal that comes out.

Before you start

Six of twenty-three, and a stand-in scale

The real PISA covers all 23 predictors and carries its own definitions, characteristics and rating scale. The four-point scale here is a stand-in so the exercise fits on one screen — what is documented is the process, not this scale, and none of these ratings is a PISA result. The six predictors and their evidence tiers are real, from the 2021 review. Nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere.

Two of these six never got an issue of this newsletter. They are still on the instrument, which is rather the point.

With scripting off, the exercise runs on paper and runs better with a second person. Write six predictors down the page: career and technical education (the only one of the 23 at the evidence-based tier), paid employment and work experience, inclusion in general education, self-care and independent living skills, interagency collaboration (all four research-based), and parent involvement (promising). Score each one privately, from “not in place” to “in place, and I could show you the evidence.” Have a colleague do the same without conferring. Then put the two columns side by side and start with the row where you are furthest apart, not with the row that scored lowest. Finish by choosing one predictor for the fall and writing a goal for it with a number and a date in it.

Nothing rated yet

        The six predictors and their evidence tiers — career and technical education at the evidence-based tier; paid employment and work experience, inclusion in general education, self-care and independent living skills, and interagency collaboration as research-based; parent involvement as promising — are as recorded in the wiki from the 2021 Mazzotti review, together with the outcome areas each one is associated with. The six-step PISA process is documented. The four-point scale, the goal wording, and the reads under each combination are ours, and no rating on this page measures any real program.

        The disagreement is the finding

        The reason the process says rate individually, then converge rather than discuss and agree is that the two produce different information. A group discussion produces one number and the illusion that it was always there. Six private ratings produce six numbers, and the spread between them is data you cannot get any other way. A predictor that two colleagues score at opposite ends of the scale is not a predictor anyone is wrong about. It is a predictor the team has no shared definition of — which is exactly why step two hands out definitions and characteristics before anybody scores.

        The goal is to identify areas of strength and areas for improvement, not to assign blame.

        The PISA process, on the consensus step — and the reason people will answer honestly the second year

        There is a practical reason to protect that framing beyond the pleasantness of it. A rating exercise that turns into an evaluation gets one honest run, and after that every score drifts upward. The instrument only works repeatedly if the people filling it in believe a low number is a piece of information rather than a piece of evidence against them. Say that out loud before the first round, not after somebody has been embarrassed.

        Ask for the evidence, not the score. The consensus step is specifically sharing ratings and evidence. In practice that turns a stalled argument into a short answerable question: not “why did you put it that low,” but “what would you have needed to see to put it higher?” Half the time the answer is a document that already exists and nobody circulates. The other half is the real finding.

        What it cannot tell you

        Here is the honest limit, and it is worth naming before you build a plan on top of the instrument. The PISA rates implementation. It tells you whether a predictor is present in your program and at what quality. It does not tell you how much of it is enough — and neither does anything else, because the predictor literature does not document dosage. There is no number of paid work hours, no threshold of general education minutes, no quantity of interagency contact attached to any of the 23. A predictor is an in-school experience that correlates with better post-school outcomes across multiple studies; it carries no guidance on optimal dosage, timing, or delivery method.

        That is a real gap and not a fatal one. It means the defensible output of a PISA cycle is a direction rather than a target: we do this thinly, we are going to do more of it, and here is the number we will look at in November. It is not we will reach the recommended level, because there is no recommended level to reach. Any vendor or slide deck offering you one has invented it.

        The second caveat, from the tiers themselves

        Predictors are correlational, not causal. Under ESSA, correlational research sits at Tier 3 — promising evidence — and does not meet What Works Clearinghouse standards for effectiveness. The 2021 review even records negative findings inside the top tier: CTE participation was associated with decreased employment outcomes for females with specific learning disabilities and other health impairments, and Black youth with specific learning disabilities were less likely than peers to gain competitive integrated employment. Strengthening a predictor is a good bet, not a mechanism. Which is also why you still read your Indicator 14 outcomes rather than assuming the plan worked. Week 1 reads the outcomes →

        Ten issues, and the two we missed

        This is the last issue. Ten of them, August to May, and the same ten for Side by Side alongside. It would be tidy to say the run was a predictor checklist all along. It was not, and the un-tidy version is more useful in a June meeting than the tidy one.

        Six of the things we spent months on are predictors: self-determination, paid work, social skills, career awareness — which is promising rather than research-based, and we said so in February — family, and interagency collaboration. Two more were not predictors at all: assessment and IEP quality are the machinery that delivers the rest, which is why they got whole months. August was the argument for why any of it matters, and this month is the arithmetic.

        And two research-based predictors never got an issue. Inclusion in general education — research-based across all three outcome areas, education, employment and independent living, which is as broad as the evidence gets. And self-care and independent living skills — research-based for education and employment, and conspicuous by its absence in a newsletter written for a program serving 18-to-22-year-olds. Neither was a decision. They are simply what a year of choosing topics one month at a time produces.

        Your Indicator 14 outcomes tell you whether it worked. The predictors tell you what to change.

        Issue 10, May — the sentence the whole year was working toward

        Which is the argument for using the instrument rather than the archive. The PISA will ask you about both of the ones we missed, and about fifteen more besides, in the same flat tone it uses for the ones you already care about. An outside list is the only kind that contains your blind spots, and that is true of this newsletter as much as of anything else it recommended.

        Try this week

        Rate it privately, then compare.

        Download the PISA from transitionta.org. Send it to two colleagues who see the same students you do, with one instruction: fill it in alone, and do not confer. Then put the three sets of ratings side by side and start with the predictor you are furthest apart on rather than the one that scored lowest. Ask what each of you would have needed to see to rate it higher. That question is the whole meeting.

        Leave with one predictor — two at the outside — and one sentence with a number and a date in it. Put a name beside every step and say what data will show it happened. Then put it somewhere you will actually find it in August, which is a different place from where you normally put things.

        That is the last of it. Ten issues, three weeks apiece for the last stretch of them, and one argument underneath all of it: that post-school outcomes are made out of ordinary decisions taken early, by people who mostly do not find out how they turned out. An assessment run in September. A referral sent in October rather than in May. A meeting handed to the student whose life it is about. A sentence written so that a stranger could act on it. None of that shows up for a year, and when it does it arrives blurred and late, as a percentage. Look at it anyway — and then go change one thing. Thank you for reading.
