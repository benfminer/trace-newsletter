The whole thing

Nine steps of business outreach, and eight of them are preparation for the moment you actually ask. Most programs skip the eight and then wonder why the sentence does not work.

↓ Start reading
Following up on Issue 05 · Resource & Quick Tip 01 02 03

The December quick tip was to spend the break writing down three employers you already have a real connection to. That list is not a shortcut around outreach. It is step one of outreach, and CTI puts it there on purpose.

Their nine-step sequence treats getting a work site as relationship-building rather than recruitment, which sounds soft until you notice what it implies: the call is not where this starts, and a program whose entire employer strategy is the call has skipped most of the method.

01 This Week’s Focus

Nine steps, and where
people fall out

VCU’s Center on Transition Innovations lays the sequence out in order. Read it once for the shape: contact is step four of nine.

  1. 1
    Brainstorm potential work sites

    Build the list from colleagues, families, and your own connections — centered on what students are actually interested in, not on who is hiring.

  2. 2
    Make community connections

    Rotary, the Chamber of Commerce, anywhere local employers already gather. Offer to come and speak about the program.

  3. 3
    Do your homework

    Know the business before you contact it — what it is, how it is structured, which tasks actually exist inside it.

  4. 4
    Make contact

    Owner, manager, or HR — whoever can say yes. Sell the partnership rather than apologizing for asking.

  5. 5
    Review the benefits

    Say what the student adds. Counter the misconceptions about hiring people with disabilities directly, because the employer is unlikely to raise them out loud.

  6. 6
    Offer training

    Disability-awareness or program orientation for their staff. This is a real offer and it is usually the first thing that makes the partnership feel two-way.

  7. 7
    Take a tour

    Walk the site and complete a WBL site assessment. You are checking environmental fit before you place anyone in it.

  8. 8
    Let the business reflect

    Give them time to consider the commitment. Do not push for the answer in the room.

  9. 9
    Follow up in two to three days

    Answer questions, decide next steps. This is the step that most often does not happen, and it is the cheapest one on the list.

Steps eight and nine are a pair. Giving them room to think only works if somebody comes back.

Otherwise it is just a conversation that ended
Two things employers ask that you should not be surprised by. What happens if it does not work out, and what it costs them. Have the answers ready: a trial period with an agreed end date, and the Work Opportunity Tax Credit and Disabled Access Credit, which exist and which most small employers have never heard of.

Skipped

    “Steps covered” is a rough read on how much of the nine-step sequence your five choices imply — it is a reading aid, not a score CTI publishes. The sequence itself, and the order of it, is theirs.

    The yes is the start of the work

    An employer saying yes is not a placement. It is permission to do steps six and seven — offer the staff training, walk the site, run the assessment on whether the environment actually fits the student you had in mind. Programs that skip straight from yes to a start date are the ones whose placements fail in week two for reasons that were visible on the tour.

    And keep the relationship after the student leaves it. Follow-along contact with an employer who has already hosted once is the cheapest employer engagement there is, and it is the difference between a program with a list of past placements and a program with partners.

    Employer engagement is not job development

    Job development is finding and negotiating one specific job for one specific person. Employer engagement is the upstream work that makes job development possible at any scale. A program with no employer engagement does job development from a cold start every single time — which is why it feels so expensive, and why it is usually the first thing that gets dropped when the caseload grows.

    You do not have to build the pipeline alone. In San Diego, the Workforce Partnership brokers employer relationships through WIOA-funded youth programs, and DOR Pre-ETS funds work-based learning placements for any student with an IEP or 504 plan from age 16. Both already hold employer relationships your program can use.

    Try this week

    Say Thursday out loud.

    Whatever else you change about how you ask, name the day you will call back. It converts a polite conversation into an appointment, it costs nothing, and it is the step in the sequence that most reliably does not happen.

    Then call on Thursday.
