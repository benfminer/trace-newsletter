The meeting starts
three weeks before the meeting.

The monthly listed four moves that happen before the day. They are not four courtesies. They are one sequence, and the order is the part that works.

↓ Start reading
Following up on Issue 08 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

An introductory call in the fall. A walk-through of how the meeting runs. A request for the family’s questions. The progress data, sent ahead. Written out as a list, the way the monthly wrote it, those four read like four separate acts of good manners.

They are not four courtesies. They are one sequence, and each contact is what makes the next one cheap. The family who took the fall call opens the March email. The family who answered the email reads the data you send. The family who read the data walks in with a question instead of a reaction — and a question is something an agenda can hold time for. So this week is the sequence itself: three contacts, what each one is actually buying, and what it costs to skip one.

01 This Week’s Focus

Three contacts,
in this order

One is CTI’s. Two are Goscicki’s. None of them happens on the day of the meeting, and that is the only property they have in common.

  1. 1
    Fall — the introductory call

    Before any IEP meeting is on the calendar. What transition planning is, what the family’s part in it looks like, and that you will be asking them things this year. CTI recommends it for families new to this or wary of it, because it arrives with no meeting attached — nothing to sign, nothing to react to.

  2. 2
    Three weeks out — the input call

    Goscicki’s second strategy, and the one that moves the agenda: a call, an email or an informal check-in to gather the family’s questions, concerns and input — explicitly including extended family and other caregivers, not only whoever is listed first on the form. Three weeks out is early enough that the answer can still change what the meeting is about.

  3. 3
    One week out — the data

    Strategy three. Progress on the current goals, sent ahead, so the family arrives able to evaluate growth and gaps rather than hearing the numbers read aloud for the first time. It is also the cheapest way to buy back agenda minutes, because reciting a progress report to people who have already read it is the most expensive thing an IEP hour routinely does.

Strategy one — telling students and parents how the meeting actually runs — is not a fourth contact. It rides along with whichever of these you are already making, which is why it gets no slot of its own here.

What the sequence is feeding

All three contacts collect material for one moment: the point where the paperwork is completed in the room. That is Goscicki’s fifth strategy, which the monthly panel already made the case for. Everything gathered before the day is only as durable as what gets typed while everyone is still sitting there. The monthly, on strategy five →

Why the order carries the weight

The finding underneath all of this is easy to misread as being about students. Goscicki and colleagues found that students have trouble participating in their own IEP meetings not because they are uninterested but because the meeting is an unfamiliar environment with implicit norms that the staff know and the student does not. Procedural unfamiliarity, not disengagement.

A parent sitting in a room where six professionals move fluently through a form they have never seen is in the same position for the same reason, and the fix is the same: say what happens before it happens. That is what makes the pre-meeting contact structural rather than a nice gesture — it is not building rapport, it is removing the reason the room is hard to speak in.

  • Contact one buys standing — the family knows who you are and what you are for before anything is at stake.
  • Contact two buys agenda — their priority arrives while the agenda is still being written, not after it is fixed.
  • Contact three buys minutes — data already read is data that does not have to be recited.

One more thing worth taking from the same study: there is no agreed definition of what meaningful participation even looks like — researchers variously mean leading the meeting, contributing to it, or simply attending to it. So decide, per meeting, what participation is supposed to look like for this family and this student. A goal nobody has stated is a goal nobody can tell you missed.

Read the study honestly. The survey reached families through disability organizations, students were not interviewed directly, and most respondents were mothers. Families with the least connection to advocacy networks are least likely to be in the sample — and are the ones for whom the fall call does the most.

Interactive · three contacts, three decisions each

Place the call

One family, one school year, three contacts before the annual. For each one you decide how you open, what you ask, and what you do with the answer. There is no answer key — every option is a real move, and each is read back for what it costs and what it leaves the family holding. The running record is what that family walks in with.

Loading the three contacts. With scripting off, the exercise is this: for a family who has said almost nothing at the last two annuals, write out what you would actually say at three moments — an introductory call in the fall with no meeting attached, an input call three weeks before the annual, and the progress data sent one week out. For each moment, decide three things: how you open, what you ask, and what happens to the answer. Then check each answer against one test — does it leave the family holding something they did not have before, or does it leave them waiting to be told?

Before the hour starts

—

Pick a contact to start

Every decision here is a transfer: something that would otherwise have to happen inside the hour, moved into a cheaper container. What you cannot transfer is the family’s own priority — that has to be asked for.

      The introductory call is CTI’s recommendation for hesitant or unfamiliar families; the input call and the data sent ahead are Goscicki’s strategies two and three. The family, the wording and the year are composites written to make the three contacts do something — no student and no household on anybody’s caseload is described here.

      It is not three calls. It is a year.

      Three contacts is the minimum viable version, and it is worth having. The better version is that none of them is the first time anything gets collected. CTI points at what already exists: Back-to-School Night and Special Education Advisory Council meetings are venues where transition partners can be introduced and planning information shared, to families who came anyway. You are not creating outreach; you are borrowing an evening somebody else scheduled.

      On the family’s side, CTI’s Expect – Engage – Empower material asks for two things that quietly solve the March problem. A comprehensive transition binder — assessments, resumes, applications, key agency contacts, in one place the family owns and carries. And a strengths board at home, which is how a family arrives able to name what their kid is good at without reconstructing a year from memory in the parking lot.

      Both work only with a rhythm behind them. Quarterly check-ins — or a short parent survey on the same cadence — mean transition information accumulates across the year instead of being assembled the week before the IEP. Which is the difference between a family who has input and a family given a week’s notice to produce some.

      The good-news call is still ours. Calling a family when nothing is wrong is a fine habit and it has no evidence base, which is why it does not appear anywhere in the sequence above. Keep doing it. Just do not let it stand in for the contact that is scheduled, has a subject, and produces something you can put on an agenda.

      Try this week

      Move one thing off the day.

      Take the next annual on your calendar and pick the single item you were going to read aloud — almost always the progress report. Send it instead, with two questions attached: does any of this not match what you see at home, and what is missing?

      Then protect the minutes you freed. Time bought back from the school half of the agenda does not walk over to the post-school half on its own. Somebody spends it on purpose — and the family told you what to spend it on when you called.
