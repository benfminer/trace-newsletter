The family came
for a different
meeting than
the one you ran.

Teachers change and caseloads shift, but the meeting where family input would land is built to spend its time somewhere else. This month: the structural fix, and what the evidence actually supports.

↓ Start reading

A student moves through three case managers in four years. The teacher who knew what worked leaves in June. The job coach changes in the fall. The family is in the room for every one of those handoffs, and they are still in the room a decade after the last of them.

That is true, and on its own it is close to useless as advice. Everybody already agrees families matter. What that agreement does not explain is why a family who came prepared, who has raised this person for twenty years and has plenty to say, so often leaves an IEP meeting having said almost none of it. The reason is not that anyone in the room dislikes families. It is that the meeting itself is shaped to spend its time on something else — and the shape is measurable.

For students already inside the transition age band, staff spent more time on the first kind than on the second.

01 This Month’s Focus

Not a bad meeting.
A biased one.

Goscicki, Sanerson, Urbano and Hodapp (2023), in the Journal of Vocational Rehabilitation, asked parents of students ages 14 to 21 what actually happens in their IEP meetings. The finding is unglamorous and hard to unsee: even for students in the transition band, school staff spent more time on school-related topics than on post-school ones.

Which means the family who drove across town to find out what happens when the program ends sits through course of study, service minutes, and a paragraph-by-paragraph read of present levels they were already sent. Nobody decided to do that. It is what happens when an agenda is built out of the items that have a box on the form.

Families who advocate for agenda time on post-school planning are not being difficult; they are correcting a structural bias in how meetings are typically run.

The wiki, on Goscicki et al. (2023)

The same study is worth reading on the student, too. Students struggle to participate not because they are checked out but because an IEP meeting is an unfamiliar room with implicit rules that every adult in it already knows. That is a procedural problem, and procedural problems have cheap fixes: tell them what happens, who will be there, and what they will be asked, before the day.

What the evidence
actually says

Here is where the usual version of this newsletter goes wrong. It is easy to say family involvement is a research-based predictor of post-school success. It is also not what the reviews found, and the accurate version is a more useful argument anyway.

  • Parent training is a confirmed Evidence-Based Practice — Rowe and colleagues (2021) classified parent training to teach knowledge of transition services at the top tier of the practice classification, on one randomized controlled trial, one quasi-experimental group study, and one single-case study. This is the strongest evidence claim on family engagement anywhere in the vault.
  • Parent expectations is a Research-Based predictor — Mazzotti and colleagues (2021), and for employment only. Not education, not independent living.
  • Parent involvement is Promising — the weakest of the three predictor tiers, for education and employment. It is the tier the phrase “families matter” usually gets attached to, and it is the one that carries the least.

Those are two different classification systems, which is worth saying out loud. Practices get graded on whether doing them causes change; predictors are correlations between in-school experiences and post-school outcomes. Predictor tiers are not causal claims and come with no guidance on dosage, timing, or delivery. The 2021 review is a systematic literature review rather than a meta-analysis, and its 23 predictors are spread across three tiers — only career and technical education sits in the top one.

Put the three lines together and the thesis changes shape. What carries evidence is not families being present. It is families being taught — the only family-facing item in this literature that is a confirmed EBP — and families expecting. Attendance is the weakest of the three, and attendance is the one most programs measure.

What that changes about March

The move is not to get more families into more meetings. It is to teach the thing and to raise what everyone expects. Guideposts for Success 2.0 puts family engagement in Area 5 and frames it in both directions: families model high expectations, understand what changes at the age of majority, and shift gradually from advocate to coach — while professionals practice cultural competency, provide benefits planning support, and share information on supported decision-making as an alternative to guardianship. A family cannot coach toward a future nobody has described to them. Week 1 takes the tiers apart →

There is one topic where all of this stops being a matter of meeting design and becomes a matter of rights, and for a program serving students who are 18 to 22 it is the most consequential conversation on the list.

The eighteenth
birthday

In California, educational rights transfer to the student at 18. The student signs consent. The school notifies both student and parent at least a year ahead — no later than the first IEP in effect after the seventeenth birthday — and a 17-year-old’s IEP without that notification is a compliance gap.

  • Families do not lose their seat. The school keeps inviting parents as interested parties. What changes is that the adult student may exclude them if they choose. Some school staff tell families that guardianship is required to stay involved after 18. That is false.
  • It is not a binary. A supported decision-making agreement requires no court at all. California’s limited conservatorship is domain-specific and leaves every other right intact. Full conservatorship is the most restrictive option, not the default one. Power of attorney is a documented third path that schools routinely fail to present.
  • The default has already tipped. Fifty-eight percent of people with intellectual and developmental disabilities ages 18 to 22 in publicly funded services have guardians, and a 2015 study found schools were the number one referral source. The National Council on Disability calls the pattern the school-to-guardianship pipeline.

California law requires alternatives to be considered before anyone files. The Office of Clients’ Rights Advocacy advises families for free at (800) 390-7032. How this gets framed in a fifteen-minute stretch of one meeting is, for a meaningful number of families, the whole decision.

What we are not claiming. Family engagement is not the strongest predictor of post-school success, and it is not a top predictor. Career and technical education is the only Evidence-Based one. Argue this on parent training, which is a confirmed EBP, and on expectations, which is Research-Based for employment — not on a ranking that does not exist.

Interactive · sixty minutes, twelve items

Divide the hour

One annual IEP, twelve agenda items, and sixty minutes that will not stretch. Cut an item, name it and move on, or give it the room. There is no score. The output is what the family heard, what got written down, and whether you reproduced the finding above.

Who is at the table

A 19-year-old, two years from exit

Second year at TRACE. Works two mornings a week at a garden center. Turned 18 last spring. His mother has taken the morning off and brought a folder. His father is on speakerphone from a job site.

  • “What happens when this program ends?”
  • “Is the work he’s doing now going anywhere?”
  • “What were we supposed to do when he turned 18?”

A composite, written to make the agenda do something. The three questions are the ones the family brought. Nobody has asked them yet.

Loading the twelve agenda items. With scripting off, the exercise is this: you have sixty minutes and twelve items, and giving every item the room it could use would take a hundred and twenty. The school-related items are present levels, course of study and credits, behavior and attendance data, accommodations and testing, and related service minutes. The post-school items are the measurable postsecondary goals and the assessment under them, what happens when TRACE ends, where he lives after this, the age-of-majority rights that transferred at 18, and what the family is already seeing at home. Two more items are neither: the opening question asked before anything is presented, and writing the IEP document during the meeting rather than after it. Decide what each one is worth, then check the total against sixty.

        02 Practice Spotlight

        The contact before
        the meeting

        The cheapest way to buy back agenda time is to stop using the meeting for things that do not need a meeting. Four moves, all of them before the day, and none of them longer than a phone call.

        1. 1
          An introductory call at the start of the year

          Before any IEP meeting happens: what transition planning is, what the family’s part in it looks like, and that you will be asking them things. CTI recommends it specifically for families who are new to this or wary of it, because it arrives without the pressure of a formal meeting attached.

        2. 2
          Tell them how the meeting runs

          Who attends, in what order, which decisions get made and which do not. This is Goscicki’s first strategy, and it is the same fix that helps the student — low participation is procedural unfamiliarity, not disinterest.

        3. 3
          Ask for their input by phone or email first

          Questions, concerns, and what they want on the agenda — including from grandparents, stepparents, and whoever else is doing the caregiving. Strategy two. It also means the family’s priorities arrive before the agenda is fixed rather than after.

        4. 4
          Send the progress data ahead of the day

          Strategy three. A family who has read the numbers can evaluate growth and gaps in the meeting instead of hearing them for the first time, and you get back the ten minutes it would have taken to read them aloud.

        None of these is a warm gesture, though they usually feel like one. Each of them is a transfer of something out of the hour and into a cheaper container — which is the only way the post-school half of the agenda ever gets real time.

        The upgrade that makes it count

        Goscicki’s fifth strategy is the sharpest and the least celebrated: complete the IEP paperwork during the meeting, so family and student input is captured in the written document rather than in somebody’s memory of a good conversation. A family’s vision that never reaches the document is a story you told at a meeting. The next team inherits the document. Week 2 builds the pre-meeting sequence →

        The good-news call is ours, not a finding. Calling a family when nothing is wrong is a genuinely good habit and we are not going to pretend it has an evidence base. The pre-meeting contact above does. Keep the warmth; make the citable version the one you schedule.

        Exceptional Family Center

        Every state has a federally funded network of Parent Training and Information Centers, and California’s network is the primary family engagement infrastructure in the state. San Diego County’s PTI is Exceptional Family Center. That is a name and a phone number, not a directory to search — and the difference between the two is roughly the difference between a referral and a homework assignment.

        Put the name in the hands of every family this spring, not just the ones who ask. exceptionallives.org/san-diego →

        A PTI is what a family needs when the thing they are trying to understand is the system rather than their kid: what an IEP has to contain, what changes at 18, how adult services eligibility works, and what to do when a district says no. It is exactly the parent-training function that shows up in the research as a confirmed practice, delivered by somebody who is not sitting on the other side of the IEP table.

        Nationally, PACER Center is the parent center this vault documents most fully — OSEP-funded under IDEA’s Parent Training and Information program, operating since 1977, with free plain-language guides on IEP transition planning, the age of majority, and supported decision-making. Its direct services are for Minnesota families; the publications are free to anybody.

        One gap, named. This vault does not hold San Diego-specific Spanish-language family engagement guidance, and language access in IEP processes is a known equity gap in a county with large Spanish, Tagalog, Somali and Arabic-speaking communities. An English-language referral is not language access, and this issue is not the place that solves it. Week 3 works the referral →

        Quick tip for March

        Ask before you present.

        At the next IEP, before the folder opens and before anybody reads anything: “What do you hope for after this program?” Then let the answer decide what the next fifty minutes are spent on.

        Asking second is not the same as asking first. A question asked after the agenda has run is an invitation to react to your framing. Asked first, it is the framing.
