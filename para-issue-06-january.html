🤝 This month’s focus

Connection Without Hovering

This issue is about peer connection — the social side of transition that gets overshadowed by job skills and daily-living tasks. For young adults, friendship and belonging are life outcomes, not extras.

Your role here is less about doing and more about creating the conditions for something to happen on its own. Which is harder, because there is nothing to show for it while it is working.

When an adult stands between two students, the students talk to the adult — not to each other.

It is hard to hear, because being close is how we help. But physical closeness can quietly block the connection you are hoping for. Peers take their cue from you: if you are always right there, they route everything through you.

🚫 Hovering

You stand between them, translate, prompt every turn. Peers speak to you. The student stays a task rather than a friend.

🤝 Bridging

You set up a shared task, spark it, then drift back. Peers talk to each other. You become scenery.

👀 Picture this

You pair Lena, 19, with a classmate to stock the supply shelf. You get them started — “you two figure out where the markers go” — and then you step to the far side of the room and reorganize a drawer you did not really need to. Two minutes later they are laughing about something you cannot hear. That is the win. You engineered it by leaving.

This is a documented effect. Research on peer support arrangements treats adult proximity as its own risk, and the recommendation is to fade the paraeducator faster rather than slower.

    ☀️ Practice spotlight

    Three Things to Try Tomorrow

    🧩 1. Build a reason to team up

    Connection grows around a shared task, not a “go be friends.” Set up a job two students have to do together — one holds, one tapes; one reads the list, one grabs the items. The task gives them a reason and takes the pressure off.

    🚶 2. Spark it, then drift

    Get the interaction going with one sentence, then physically move away. Find something to do a few feet off. Your absence is the invitation for them to turn toward each other instead of toward you.

    💭 3. Redirect to the peer, not to you

    When a student asks you something a classmate could answer, bounce it: “Ask Marcus — he knows.” Every time you route a question to a peer instead of answering it, you build the habit of turning to each other.

    ⚠️ Watch out for

    Being the Only Friend

    You will not be there after they exit. Their peers might be.

    It feels good to be a student’s favorite person, and that bond is real. But if the adult is the only relationship a young adult has, that is a warning sign rather than a success. In a year or two you will be gone, and the friendships that last are the ones with people their own age.

    The kindest thing you can do is help them need you a little less — and each other a little more.

    It does not show up on a review. A student every measure says is fine, who nobody chooses, is a common quiet reason a placement ends by resignation.
    🎁 Resource of the month

    The TIES Center — peer engagement tools

    The TIES Center publishes free, research-based guides on peer engagement and how adults can support connection without getting in the way — including practical peer support strategies you can set up tomorrow.

    Free. Search “peer engagement” for short guides written for classroom teams. tiescenter.org →

    Worth knowing that peer support arrangements are a formally recognized evidence-based practice for social interaction — not just a nice idea. The evidence for academic gains is more mixed, so if someone pitches this as an academic intervention, that is the part to push back on.

    One thing before your next shift

    Set it up, then walk away for two minutes.

    One two-student task tomorrow. Get it rolling, then leave the space open. Do not narrate and do not hover.

    Just watch from across the room and see what happens when you are not standing in the middle of it.
