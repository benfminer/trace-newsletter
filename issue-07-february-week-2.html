Name it, date it,
write what you learned.

The debrief in the van is ours. The three fields that turn it into evidence are not — and they take about eight minutes.

↓ Start reading
Following up on Issue 07 · Practice Spotlight 01 02 03

The monthly gave you three questions, asked in the van before the memory sands down. What part of today would you do again. What part would you skip. What does this tell us about the next thing to try.

That routine is ours — house practice, not a framework anybody published. Keep it; it works. But a conversation leaves nothing behind. Six weeks on, the afternoon has compressed into “the clinic didn’t work out,” which plans nothing and disappears the day somebody else has the van. So this week is the eight minutes after the debrief, and the three fields that make an afternoon hold.

01 This Week’s Focus

Three fields,
every single time

The assessment guidance gets unusually specific here, and it is worth copying exactly. For every tool used, record three things — and summarize them in the PLAAFP.

  1. 1
    The assessment name

    The instrument, named specifically enough that a stranger could tell whether it was formal or informal and how long it took.

  2. 2
    The date given

    Not a season, not a school year. If the tool ran across three mornings, that is three dates. This is the field that makes the assessment current rather than inherited.

  3. 3
    The information gained

    What you know now that you did not know at 8am — not that the student attended, not that it went well, but the thing about strengths, preferences, interests, or needs that the next placement decision turns on.

Run that against a real Tuesday. Five hours shadowing at a small-animal veterinary clinic on February 11, the placement he asked for by name. In the van: the laundry and the supply shelves were the good part, and the problem was not the dogs but the barking, because you cannot tell when it starts.

The same afternoon, twice

Written down. Job shadow with structured student debrief, small-animal veterinary clinic, five hours · February 11, 2027 · reported unpredictable noise as the reason the kennel area did not work; requested to keep stockroom and laundry tasks and named organizing shelves as preferred work.

Not written down. A story you told well in March, which by the annual is “we tried the vet thing.” Gone: a preference, a need, and the argument for the next placement.

Notice what the good version is not. Five hours cannot rule out a field, and student was overwhelmed is the observer’s conclusion wearing the student’s name. The line that earns its place names an environment and a request.

Feeds all four letters. Every debrief answer lands in Strengths, Preferences, Interests, or Needs. Written down, the profile sharpens; left in the van, it does not.

Why three fields becomes evidence

Indicator 13 is audited on the IEP document, not on how well anybody remembers the year. A postsecondary employment goal has to rest on age-appropriate transition assessment, and a reviewer establishes that by finding the assessment named, dated, and summarized. Three fields is not paperwork about the work; it is the form in which the work counts.

Which cuts both ways, and the second direction is the useful one. Informal assessment collected in actual work settings is particularly valuable for vocational planning, and often more informative than a formal test. Your three questions in the van are informal assessment and always were — they just were not showing up anywhere anybody else could find them.

  • Named and dated, it is assessment — citable under the postsecondary goal it supports.
  • Summarized in the PLAAFP, it is present levels — one sentence doing two jobs, which is why it goes there rather than in a folder of your own.
  • Undated, it is an anecdote — and an anecdote cannot support a goal or survive you leaving in June.

Interactive · three debriefs, three fields each

Write the file line

Three experiences from one student’s year, each already debriefed in the van. You have the answers. Now write the entry: pick the assessment name, the date, and the line of information gained. The record assembles as you go, and every choice gets read the way a reviewer would read it.

Loading the three experiences. With scripting off, the exercise is this: for each of a workplace tour in October, a job shadow at a veterinary clinic on February 11, and a three-morning situational assessment at a garden center in April, write three fields — the assessment name, the date given, and the information gained — and summarize them in the PLAAFP. A name specific enough to identify the instrument. An actual date, one per session. And a line that names an environment, a preference, or a support need rather than reporting that the student attended and enjoyed it.

The line in the file

—

Pick an experience to start

Every field you leave vague is a field a reviewer cannot use and a receiving team cannot read. Three fields, eight minutes, once per experience.

      The documentation standard — assessment name, date given, information gained, summarized in the PLAAFP — is the VCU/CTI practical tip, not ours. The three debrief questions behind these answers are house practice. The student, the sites and the answers are composites written to make the fields do something.

      Don’t run the same tool twice

      The same guidance that gives you the three fields also says, plainly, not to repeat the same tool year over year. Three identical van conversations are one instrument given three times. A workplace tour, a job shadow, and a three-morning situational assessment produce three different kinds of information about the same student.

      California, specifically. The triennial reevaluation cycle (Ed. Code § 56381) lands a comprehensive reassessment once every three years. The annual documented log is what carries the two years in between.

      It is also the answer to the afternoon that goes badly. When a student’s interests move after an experience, that is a natural part of the transition assessment process, not a failure — but only if the shift is on paper. An interest that changed between October and April, dated at both ends, is a student learning what they want. Undocumented, it is a goal that contradicts itself with nothing to explain why.

      Try this week

      Write one line, backdated.

      Take the last work experience one of your students had and write the three fields for it now — name, date, information gained. You still remember it. In April you won’t.

      If you cannot fill the third field, that is the finding. Either the experience taught you nothing, which is worth knowing, or it taught you something nobody asked for — and the next debrief is where you go get it.
