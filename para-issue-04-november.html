🛣 This month’s focus

Getting Out There: CBI Basics

Community-based instruction means the learning happens where the skill actually gets used. Not a pretend store in the life-skills room. The real one, with real carts and a real cashier who is having a day.

Your students are 18 to 22. In a year or two they will be buying their own groceries, catching their own bus, and handling their own declined card, with nobody standing nearby. The community outing is the closest thing you get to a rehearsal for that.

And your job out there is genuinely different from your job in the classroom. Different distance, different noise, different rules about who is watching. It takes practice to get comfortable, and most people are not told that in advance.

This is not a field trip. A trip is somewhere you go. CBI is instruction that happens to be held in a store — which means somebody knows what each student is working on before the van leaves.

Why the real place matters this much

A skill practiced in the real setting transfers. A skill practiced on a worksheet mostly stays on the worksheet. That is not a slogan — it is the single most common reason a student who “can do it in class” cannot do it at a job site.

When researchers went through the studies on teaching employment skills to students with disabilities, more than 60% of them used community-based instruction somewhere in the intervention — usually in the part where they checked whether the skill had actually generalized. Skills taught in real settings also varied more than school-taught ones, because a real workplace throws tasks at you that a classroom cannot invent.

The classroom can teach the steps. Only the real place can teach what to do when the steps do not work.

Which is the part they will actually need

Worth being straight about the evidence, though: community-based instruction is classified as a research-based practice rather than a fully evidence-based one. It is well supported and widely used, and it has not been tested as heavily as some of the prompting and instruction methods it gets delivered alongside. It works. It is not magic, and the outing still has to be taught rather than just attended.

Three parts, three jobs

A good outing has three parts, and your role is different in each one. Most of the trips that go badly go badly in part one, before anybody left the building.

📋 Before you leave

Know the goal for each student, the route, and what happens if something goes sideways. Prep each student on what they are doing — not where the group is going.

👀 While you’re there

Hang back. Let the environment do the teaching. Step in only as far up the prompting ladder as you actually have to, and no further.

💬 On the way back

Debrief while it is fresh. What went well, what was hard. Jot one line before the next stop erases it — you will not remember by Thursday.

One clear job each

Before you walk in, every student should know their one target: “you’re finding the milk and paying for it.” A specific job beats trailing the group and watching, and one real task done alone is worth more than an hour of accompanying somebody else’s.

    ☀️ Practice spotlight

    Three things to try tomorrow

    🎯 1. Give each student one clear job

    Say it out loud in the van, before anyone gets out. “You’re finding the milk and paying.” If a student cannot repeat their job back to you, they do not have one yet.

    🧐 2. Blend in

    These are adults in a public place. Stand back, talk quietly, skip the clipboard-hovering. The more you look like a regular shopper who happens to be nearby, the more they get to look like a regular customer — which is the dignity part of this work.

    🛡️ 3. Know your two safety anchors

    Before every outing: where you meet if you get separated, and how you reach the teacher or the program. Independence is the goal. Safety is the floor. Both, every single time.

    The third one is not optional and not negotiable. Everything else in this issue is about pulling back. None of it applies to the two anchors.
    ⚠️ Watch out for

    The Rescue Reflex

    The awkward moment in public is the lesson. It is not the emergency.

    A student fumbles the money. The cashier’s face changes. The line grows and somebody sighs, and every part of you wants to step in and make it stop.

    Resist. Those exact moments, handled by the student, are what builds real-world competence — and there is no way to practice them somewhere comfortable, because the discomfort is the thing being practiced. Unless there is a safety issue, let it be a little uncomfortable.

    A trip where you smoothed everything over is a trip where you did the shopping and they came along.

    The hardest version of it

    Somebody speaks to you about the student, in front of the student. A cashier, an employee, a stranger. Do not answer. Look at your student. Three seconds of silence redirects the whole conversation to the person it was always about, and it does it without embarrassing anyone.

    That one is worth practicing deliberately, because answering is polite and automatic and it happens before you have decided anything.

    🎁 Resource of the month

    OCALI — free transition and community resources

    The Ohio Center for Autism and Low Incidence keeps a deep, free library of transition and community-skills material — task analyses, visual supports, and planning guides you can adapt for any outing without building one from scratch.

    ocali.org

    One thing before your next shift

    Decide the moment in advance.

    On the next outing, pick one student and one moment where you will deliberately not step in. Decide it in the van, before anything happens — because in the moment you will always help more than you meant to.

    Then hold your ground and watch them handle it.
