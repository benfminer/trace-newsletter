Your student is already
the decision-maker.
Nobody told the plan.

Educational rights transferred at 18. Every student on your caseload is legally the one who signs — and most of them have never run a minute of their own meeting.

↓ Start reading

Under IDEA, all educational rights transfer from parent to student at 18 in California. Not some. All. After that the school communicates with the student, the student consents, and the parent attends because the student wants them there.

Which means the thing most programs treat as a long-term goal — the student directing their own plan — is not a goal at TRACE. It is the current legal situation, and it arrived whether or not anyone prepared for it.

So the honest question this month is not should we build self-determination. It is: the person who holds the pen already holds it. How much of the meeting have we actually handed them?

Unless a conservator was appointed. California’s limited conservatorship covers named domains only — a limited conservator may hold authority over residence and not over educational decisions. Check which domains, rather than assuming all of them.
01 This Month’s Focus

A set of teachable skills,
not a personality

“Self-determined” gets used as a compliment about a student’s temperament. It is not a temperament. It is four things, each of which can be taught, and each of which has an obvious place to practice.

  1. 1
    Making decisions

    Choosing between real options and living with the choice — including the ones that turn out badly. A choice with no possible wrong answer is not practice.

  2. 2
    Advocating

    Using their own voice to ask for what they need. In an IEP room, at a job site, at a doctor’s appointment — the setting changes, the skill does not.

  3. 3
    Setting goals

    Naming what they want and tracking their own progress toward it, rather than receiving a goal someone else wrote and being told how it is going.

  4. 4
    Developing self-awareness

    Knowing their own strengths and needs well enough to describe them to a stranger who has no file.

None of that means independence from support. It means the student is the one deciding, and knows how to ask for help doing it. Those are compatible; the field spent years acting as though they were not.

The finding that makes this worth a month

Shogren and colleagues followed 779 students with disabilities, ages 14 to 21, across six states. They measured self-determination at high school exit, then looked at where those students were one and two years later.

Self-determination status at exit significantly predicted both, one year out.

2 yr
Community access holds

Still predicted at two years — and students who had received self-determination instruction held their outcomes more steadily over time than peers who had not.

A capacity built before exit was still doing work two years after anyone from the school stopped watching.

Which is more than most of what we do can claim

Be careful with what that does and does not say. It is correlational at heart: students higher in self-determination did better, and instruction moved self-determination. It is not a promise that a semester of goal-setting lessons buys a job. What it does establish is that this is not soft — it is one of the few school-side capacities with a measured line to adult life.

Why school makes this harder than it needs to be

School runs on permission. Ask before you move, wait for instructions, follow the schedule you were handed. Students get good at that, and then the habit does not survive contact with a job or an apartment, where nobody is going to tell them what happens next.

Practice has to be deliberately built back in, because the default setting of a school day removes it.

Interactive · four years, six handovers

Schedule the handover

A student arrives at 18 and exits at 22 — four annual IEP meetings, if you get all four. Decide which year each piece of the meeting becomes theirs, and see what they can actually do on the way out.

1 Opening Welcomes the room and says who they are
2 Strengths States one thing they are good at
3 Accommodations Names a support that helps and asks for it
4 Progress Reports how their own goals are going
5 The goal itself Says what they want the postsecondary goal to be
6 The whole thing Runs the meeting start to finish
One meeting a year is the assumption here. Some students get more — an amendment, a transition-planning session, a rehearsal the week before. Every one of those is another repetition, and they are cheaper to schedule than the annual is.

    02 Practice Spotlight

    Hand Over One Meeting

    Student-led IEPs sound like a program change. Start with three sentences and one student.

    Before the next IEP, coach one student to say three things out loud, in their own words. Not read from a card — said.

    1. 1
      “Here’s something I’m good at.”

      Self-awareness, out loud, in a room where it will be written down.

    2. 2
      “Here’s one thing I want after I leave.”

      The postsecondary goal, sourced from the person it belongs to. This one sentence is the difference between a goal about a student and a goal from one.

    3. 3
      “Here’s a support that helps me.”

      Advocacy in its smallest workable form — and the exact sentence they will need at a job, at a college disability office, and at DOR.

    Three sentences exercises three of the four component skills at once, in the highest-stakes room of their year. Next year they run more of it. That is the whole strategy: hand over a little more, every time, on purpose.

    There are packaged curricula for this. The Self-Directed IEP, the Self-Advocacy Strategy, Whose Future Is It?, and Take Charge all have evidence behind them. Use one if you have it — but do not wait for one to start.

    Rehearse it, or it will not happen

    The sentences get practiced somewhere low-stakes first — with you, with a para, with a peer — because a student who has never said them before will not produce them cold in front of nine adults. The rehearsal is the intervention. The meeting is just where it gets performed.

    03 Resource of the Month
    • Free
    • imdetermined.org
    • Student-facing

    I’m Determined

    A state-directed project funded by the Virginia Department of Education, built for students rather than staff. Two pieces are worth your November: the self-determination checklists, which a student fills in themselves to name three strengths and three things to work on, and the One-Pager — a single landscape page with four boxes: strengths, interests, preferences, needs.

    Print the One-Pager and have one student fill it in before their next meeting. It is the same SPIN profile September was about — except this time the student writes it, which is what turns a file summary into self-advocacy.

    Virginia, used anywhere. The materials are curriculum-agnostic and free; nothing in the One-Pager assumes Virginia procedure.

    Do this month

    Stop answering for them.

    When somebody asks your student a question — a nurse, an employer, a parent, you — wait. Let the silence sit there and let them answer it, even slowly, even imperfectly.

    Every time you fill the silence, you take a repetition away from the person who needs it.
