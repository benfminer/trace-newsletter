💬 This month’s focus

Their Voice, Not Yours

AAC stands for augmentative and alternative communication. In practice it means any way a person communicates other than speech, or alongside it: a speech-generating device, a tablet running a communication app, a picture system, a low-tech board of symbols, signs, gestures.

If a student on your caseload uses any of those, this issue is about you. Not because the system is complicated — the student’s speech pathologist owns the setup — but because a communication system only does anything inside a conversation, and you are the other half of most of their conversations.

A screen reader works if the person using it knows how. An AAC system works only if the people around it know how to be a partner. That makes you part of the support, not a bystander to it.

A device that is not within reach is the same as taking away a student’s voice for the day.

Two things make or break AAC support, and both of them belong to you rather than to a specialist who visits on Thursdays.

🔋 Access

The system is present, charged, working, and reachable — on the bus, at the job site, in the bathroom line, at lunch. Not in a cabinet. Not in a backpack. Not “kept safe” at the front of the room.

👈 Modeling

You use the device too — pointing to symbols while you talk, in the ordinary flow of the task, without asking the student to copy you. This is called aided language modeling, and it is the single practice with the most research behind it.

On access, the strongest thing to have in your pocket is not a study. It is the Communication Bill of Rights, a consensus statement from the National Joint Committee for the Communication Needs of Persons With Severe Disabilities, now in its third edition. It is not research; it is the field agreeing on a floor. Right 12 is the one to know: the right to individualized, working AAC and other assistive technology at all times.

“At all times” has no asterisk on it. And “working” is doing real work in that sentence — uncharged, cracked, missing vocabulary, or mounted where the student cannot reach it fails the same test as a device left in a drawer. Charging is not housekeeping. It is the support.

Why modeling matters more than prompting

A child who learns to speak hears tens of thousands of spoken words before producing any. Students who use symbol-based AAC are often expected to produce a kind of language they have almost never seen anyone else produce. Modeling fills that in: you say things on the device, in front of them, without a demand attached.

Sennott, Light and McNaughton reviewed the modeling research in 2016 — ten studies, most of them single-case — and found gains across four areas of language: pragmatics (more turns taken), semantics (vocabulary), syntax (longer, multi-symbol messages), and morphology. It is a small evidence base and most of the participants were young children, so treat modeling with an 18-to-22-year-old at a job site as a well-founded bet rather than a proven result. It is still the best-supported thing you can do with your hands.

Training the para is the intervention. Kashinath, Dukhovny and Polit (2022) coached four paraeducators in aided language modeling inside their own classrooms — and every one of them increased their use of it. Four people is a pilot, not a proof. But it was a California study, and it points at coaching on the floor rather than a one-off in-service.

Then wait. Really wait.

Most people who use a symbol-based system compose a message far more slowly than speech runs. The gap that opens up is uncomfortable, and adults fill it — repeating the question, rephrasing it, answering it, moving on. Every one of those takes the turn away.

  • At least five seconds, counted silently, is the everyday floor. Not a target — a floor. Most of us think we have waited five seconds at about two.
  • Three to five seconds is the pause to leave at phrase and sentence breaks while you are modeling, so the student has time to find on the board what you just touched.
  • The real number is the student’s number. Researchers have found that pauses far longer than adults assume produce more turns and more words. Learn how long this particular student takes, including how long it takes them to get a hand moving, and use that.

What you do during the wait matters as much as its length. Stay quiet. Keep your body turned toward them. Glance at the device to show you expect it to be used. Do not repeat the question — repeating restarts the whole process from the top.

👀 Picture this

At the job site you want a student to ask her supervisor for more towels. Instead of asking for her, you pick up her device and model it — you touch I, want, more — then set it back down and say nothing. It takes her a while. You let it take a while. She asks. The supervisor hears her, and now knows she can ask, which is worth more than the towels.

“Won’t the device stop them talking?”

You will get asked this, usually by a family member, sometimes by a coworker, and often at a moment when you have nothing to say back. Here is the answer.

The one number worth memorizing

Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) pulled together every AAC study published between 1975 and 2003 that measured speech before, during and after intervention — 23 studies, 67 individuals. AAC did not slow or block spoken language. Speech production increased in 89% of participants.

The authors are careful, and you should be too: the gains were modest. The honest sentence is AAC does not hold speech back, and speech often improves a little alongside it — not AAC causes speech. Said that way it is both true and enough.

The fear underneath the question is that the device is a surrender. It is not. It is a way of being heard today, while everything else keeps developing.

Interactive · five turns, one device

Hold the turn

Five moments across one shift. Each time, a student starts building a message and a clock starts running. You can jump in whenever you like — the buttons stay live the whole time — or you can stay quiet and watch it land. The clock is real. That is the point of the thing.

Turn 1 of 5

Monday, 8:12am. You ask how her weekend went.

She reaches for the device. Two other students need signing in and the teacher is asking you something from across the room.

The student is made up and so is the board — a real system has hundreds of words on it, not twelve. What is not made up is the pace. Composing takes this long, and five seconds is longer than it sounds when somebody is waiting on you.

    ☀️ Practice spotlight

    Three Things to Try Tomorrow

    ✅ 1. The 30-second device check

    At the start of every shift, four questions. Is it here? Is it charged? Is it reachable from where the student actually sits or stands? Is the vocabulary they need for today’s task on it? Thirty seconds up front prevents a whole day with no way to speak — and the fourth question is the one everybody skips.

    👈 2. Model one message, ask for nothing

    Pick up the device during an ordinary exchange and say something on it yourself — I like this, I am finished — touching the symbols as you speak. Then move on. The moment you model and then wait for them to copy you, you have turned input into a demand. Slow down enough that they can see where your finger went.

    🌱 3. Honor every attempt

    Right 4 of the Communication Bill of Rights is a response to all communication, even when the answer has to be no. Four moves that make that real: acknowledge the attempt, whatever form it took; attribute meaning to an unclear one out loud — “you hit go, do you want to go outside?”; expand it — they touch more, you say and model want more; and recast it another way. If they gestured and you understood, respond to the gesture. Making them repeat it on the device turns a conversation into a drill.

    Those four moves come from practitioner guidance rather than a trial, so take them as craft. The rule underneath them — that every attempt gets an answer — is the part that is settled.

    ⚠️ Watch out for

    Speaking For Them

    Filling the silence is faster, and it is how the turn gets taken.

    When a message is taking forever and a coworker is standing there waiting, jumping in feels like help. It is the most sympathetic mistake on this page, and it is still the one that costs the most. Right 3 of the Communication Bill of Rights is the right to be addressed directly and not spoken for or talked about as though not there — and at a job site, being spoken for is exactly how a young adult stops being treated as an employee.

    If someone directs a question at you that belongs to the student, hand it back: look at the student, or say “ask her, she is answering.” That single sentence buys the eight seconds she needs and teaches the coworker how this works.

    And never take the device away

    Not as a consequence, not as leverage, not “until we get through this task.” It is not a privilege and it is not a reinforcer. It is the voice.

    Be precise about why, because you may have to say it to a supervisor. This is a rights argument, not a research finding — nobody has run a controlled study comparing device removal to the alternatives, and you should not claim one. What you can say is that Right 12 guarantees a working system at all times with no behavioral exception, and Right 4 guarantees a response to all communication. A policy that removes the device contradicts both. It also removes the only tool the student had for repairing the situation, at the exact moment they were trying hardest to use it.

    If the device is being thrown or banged, that is a message about something, and the question is what it is for. There are practical alternatives — lock the tablet to the communication app, hide the buttons that are not communication — that keep the voice while you work the behavior.

    Then fade. The TIES Center is blunt about the paraprofessional’s arc with AAC: train, coach, and then withdraw. Teach classmates and coworkers to model and to wait, then get out of the space — permanent adult proximity is itself the thing that blocks peer interaction. Which is January’s issue, arriving from the other direction.

    Always coordinate with the student’s speech pathologist before changing anything about the system itself — vocabulary, layout, settings. Partner habits are yours. The system is theirs.

    One thing worth carrying into a job placement. Richardson, McCoy and McNaughton (2019) studied seven employed adults with autism who use AAC, gathering accounts from the adults, their families and their employers. Seven people is a small study and the only transition-age one in this area — but two of the factors they named are squarely yours: the reliability of the technology, and coworker attitudes. A device that dies at work is a job risk, not an inconvenience. And a coworker who has been shown how to wait is a support you built.

    Which means the vocabulary for the specific job — coworker names, tasks, breaks, I need help, I am finished, the safety words — needs to be on the device before the first shift, not after the first bad day. That is a conversation to start with the speech pathologist now.

    One thing before your next shift

    Model one message. Ask for nothing back.

    Pick one student, one ordinary moment, and say one thing on their device yourself. Touch the symbols while you speak. Then carry on with the task.

    Do not wait for them to copy you. You are not testing anything. You are showing them what it looks like when someone fluent uses it — and this week, that is you.
