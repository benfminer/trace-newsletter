There is a particular kind of tired that comes from writing a lesson at nine at night for a skill you have taught in some form for years. The federal government has already written thirty of them and they cost nothing.

01 This Week’s Focus

Skills to Pay
the Bills, in detail

139 pages, 30 activities, six areas, free from the Department of Labor. It was co-developed with more than 100 young people across a multi-site pilot, and it shows — the activities assume a facilitator rather than a lecturer.

  • Communication — verbal, non-verbal, written; active listening; employer expectations
  • Enthusiasm & Attitude — positive mental attitude, resilience, self-marketing
  • Teamwork — shared responsibility, roles, reciprocity, on-the-job application
  • Networking — professional relationships, social media, email and text etiquette
  • Problem Solving & Critical Thinking — workplace ethics, feedback versus criticism, interview preparation
  • Professionalism — consistent workplace behavior, professional relationships, self-reflection

Two things sit inside it that are easy to miss. The Communication section includes practicing disability disclosure — a genuinely hard conversation that almost never gets rehearsed before a student has to have it for real. And the Networking section addresses talking about a juvenile justice background, which is not something most curricula will touch at all.

Networking is a standalone area in ODEP and is not among Virginia’s 22.

Which is the cleanest argument for keeping both

Use ODEP for the activities and Virginia for the rating criteria. They were built for different jobs and they do not collide — one is facilitation-friendly and hands-on, the other is granular and assessment-oriented. Both rest on the same employer research base.

Piloted widely. Project SEARCH sites, workforce youth centers, migrant education programs, and alternative schools all ran it before release.

The eighteen that go home

CTI’s home-based Workplace Readiness Toolkit is 18 weekly activity sheets, one per skill. Each carries a plain-language definition, a menu of two or three activities — at least one requiring no technology, deliberately — and reflection questions the family answers and sends back.

That return path is the whole point. Generalization is the known weak spot for these skills, and a sheet that comes back tells you whether Initiative exists anywhere outside your room. It is also the cheapest data you will collect all semester.

The loop it assumes

Teacher introduces the skill at school and in the community. Sheet goes home. Family reinforces it and writes back what they saw. Teacher adjusts. The toolkit is explicit that the feedback leg is the design principle rather than a nicety — and that skills should keep being reinforced across home, school and community all year rather than checked off once.

Interactive · one week, three decisions

Run the room as a job site

The monthly’s quick tip in a form you can actually execute. Pick the skill you are targeting and how hard you want to push, and the week lays itself out — with the Friday debrief written for you.

The week

    The job-site week is the monthly’s quick tip. The skills are drawn from the four termination categories rather than invented, and the debrief questions follow the reflection pattern CTI uses in its own lessons.

    Three honest limits

    The home sheets assume a particular family. They assume someone can read them, write a response, and send it back — in English. There is no documented Spanish version, and no guidance for students in foster care or without consistent family involvement. For those students the sheet is not a lighter option, it is a dead end. Use a staff member or a CBI outing as the second setting instead.

    ODEP’s stated band is 14 to 21. Your students run to 22. Nothing in the activities breaks at the top end, but the framing in places is written for someone younger — read an activity before you run it rather than after.

    Neither curriculum has outcome data. No study shows that running these activities improves employment outcomes. What is established is the underlying claim — that social skills drive retention — not that these particular worksheets move it. Use them because they save you writing time, not because they are proven.

    Also unaddressed: families with low literacy or historically low school engagement. The reflection questions assume a writer.

    Try this week

    Three weeks, one skill, a number at each end.

    Run the job-site week. Pick the one skill you rated back in Week 1 and make that the thing you give feedback on all week.

    Then debrief on Friday and re-rate. You will have a before and an after, which is more than most soft-skills instruction ever produces.
