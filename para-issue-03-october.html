🌪 This month’s focus

The Support That Fades Works

If a student can only do it when you are standing there, they have not learned the task. They have learned to do it with you.

You showed up again this month — tied a shoe, redirected a rough morning, coached a job skill, solved three quiet problems that never made a meeting note. That work is real, and it is worth saying plainly that most of it is invisible.

Last month was about using the least help that works in a given moment. Fading is what happens across weeks: pulling back on purpose, in planned steps, so the skill ends up living in the student rather than in your presence.

Fading is not something that happens in May when a student is finally “ready.” It is the plan from day one. With a young adult who ages out at 22, it is the whole clock.

Plan the fade before you start the teaching. That is the rule in every systematic-instruction curriculum: before the first session, decide who or what will carry this once you are gone.

Two things fade, not one

Most people picture fading as talking less. That is half of it. The other half is where you are standing — and for a lot of students the second one matters more, because a body right beside them is its own prompt, no matter how quiet you are.

💬 What you say

Direct verbal, every step → indirect nudges (“what’s first?”) → a glance → nothing at all. The prompt gets smaller before it disappears.

🚶 Where you stand

Beside them → a step back but in sight → across the area, watching → out of the area, returning after five minutes, then thirty, then an hour.

That second list is a real procedure, not a figure of speech. Job coaches fade proximity in exactly those stages: stay within sight first, then move to somewhere you can observe from, then leave and come back on an interval.

The student cannot be seen doing it alone until you give them room to be alone.

And being seen doing it alone is what changes how a workplace treats them

    ☀️ Practice spotlight

    Three things to try tomorrow

    📍 1. Pick one skill and one student

    Do not try to fade everything at once. Choose a single routine that the student already does with your help — clocking in, starting a task, packing up — and make that your fading project for the month.

    👇 2. Plan to drop one notch a week

    Decide the next-lower level of help before the week starts. A written plan beats an in-the-moment judgment call, because in the moment you will always help more than you meant to.

    🧘 3. Add physical distance

    Each week, stand a step further away. This is the half of fading that costs you nothing and gets skipped anyway.

    ⚠️ Watch out for

    Fading Cold Turkey

    Fading is a dial you turn down, not a switch you flip off.

    Pulling all your support at once usually backfires in a specific and predictable way: the student fails, gets discouraged, and you end up back at full help — sometimes at more help than before, because now there is a bad memory attached to the task.

    Fade in small planned steps, and let success tell you when to drop the next notch. And when a step falls apart, read it correctly: you did not fail, you found the level they still need. Hold there a while, then try the next drop again.

    The rule in one line

    When a step stalls, go back one notch — not to the beginning. Going back to full support teaches the student that struggling brings you all the way back, which is the opposite of what you want them to learn.

    🤫 The uncomfortable one

    “Good job!” is a prompt

    This one goes against every instinct you have, and it is worth sitting with.

    In systematic instruction there is a rule stated bluntly: trainers routinely get in the way of learning through praise. “Good job,” “okay, what’s next,” a nod, a smile — each one tells the student that step is done and it was right.

    Students come to rely on that signal quickly. And it is a signal that will not be there once you fade out, which means part of what you are teaching without meaning to is wait for the adult’s face before moving on.

    ✅ While they are learning

    Give enough information to do every step completely and correctly. Early instruction should be close to error-free — this is not the moment to be sparing.

    😴 Once they can do it

    Say something only when it goes wrong. Silence becomes the signal that it is going right — and silence is available at every job site in the world, after you leave.

    The part that has to be taught deliberately is how the student knows the task is finished and correct without you. What does the tray look like when it is done? How does the screen look when it went through? Teach that, or your reaction stays the finish line.

    To be clear: this is about task feedback during instruction, not about warmth. Nobody is asking you to stop liking the student.

    One thing before your next shift

    Take one step back. Literally.

    Find a routine where you usually stand right beside a student, and this week stand one pace further away. Do not change anything you say. Change only where your body is.

    Then watch what they do with the room you gave them.
