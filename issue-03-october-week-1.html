Four parts present.
Nothing connected.

A transition IEP is a chain, and a chain with every link lying on the table is still not a chain.

↓ Start reading

This month’s issue named the four required elements and made the case that having them is not the same as using them. This week: what each one actually is, what a reviewer will accept, and what makes it worth something.

The organizing idea is that these four are not a list. They are a sequence, and each one is supposed to be produced by the one before it. Assessment tells you the goal. The goal tells you the service. The service tells you the course of study. Break any link and the parts downstream are still on the page — just no longer about this student.

01 This Week’s Focus

What each one is,
and what passes

1. Measurable postsecondary goals

Required in employment and education or training, plus independent living where appropriate. They describe life after exit — not what happens in your program. That distinction is the single most common technical error: “Marcus will complete a work experience rotation” is an annual goal wearing a postsecondary goal’s clothes.

Measurable means someone could later determine whether it happened. Note the tense: postsecondary goals get measured after the student leaves, which is exactly what Indicator 14 does a year out.

Based on assessment. 34 CFR 300.320(b)(1) — the goals must rest on age-appropriate transition assessment. That is the first link in the chain.

2. Transition services

A coordinated set of activities: instruction, related services, community experiences, employment and other post-school adult living objectives, and where appropriate, daily living skills and functional vocational evaluation. In practice the test is narrower and more useful — who does what, by when, and how does it move this student toward that goal?

3. Course of study

The multi-year classes and experiences that lead toward the goal. In California this is where the diploma-versus-certificate decision lives, and that choice has consequences that outlast the IEP by decades — it shapes what postsecondary options remain open.

4. Agency linkage

With parent consent (or the student’s, once rights transfer at 18), the team must invite any outside agency likely to provide or pay for transition services — DOR, the Regional Center. It is a requirement about invitation, and it is the one most often treated as optional.

Every one of these can be present, correct, and pointed in a different direction from the other three.

Reviewers do look for some alignment — a service has to relate to a goal — but how strictly that gets read varies by district and reviewer. The reliable part is that presence is always checked and usefulness never is.

02 The one that gets skipped

The invitation nobody sends

Agency linkage is the cheapest element to satisfy and the most expensive to miss.

It costs an email. What it buys is a relationship with the agency that will still be there after you are not — and, more practically, a place in a queue. DOR eligibility, Regional Center enrollment, and waiver services all move on timelines measured in months, not weeks.

For a student with four years of eligibility left, a linkage made late in year three is a linkage that produces nothing before exit. The student ages out into a service system that has never heard of them.

Two systems, both of which have to know

Students with qualifying developmental disabilities need both an IEP and a Regional Center plan. The IEP ends at exit. The Regional Center plan is what continues — but only if the student is actually enrolled.

A student who reaches exit without an active regional center plan falls into a gap that nobody in the school system is still responsible for.

So the practical version of this requirement is not “invite the agency.” It is: find out today whether this student is enrolled anywhere that will outlive their IEP, and if they are not, start that this month.

Consent first. The invitation requires parent consent — or the student’s own, once rights transfer at 18.

Try this week

Read one IEP backwards.

Start at the course of study and work upward. Does it serve the services? Do the services serve the goal? Does the goal come from the assessment? The first place the answer is no is where the plan stops being about this student.
