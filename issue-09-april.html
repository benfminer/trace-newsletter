After exit,
no one comes
looking for them.

There is federal money already reserved for your students, and a door that opens on an IEP or a 504 Plan with no eligibility determination behind it. This month: what Pre-ETS actually is, who counts as a student in California, and why the referral is an auditable element rather than a favor.

↓ Start reading

New here? Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition — hover, tap, or tab to one to see it.

For thirteen years the system found your student. Somebody screened, somebody assessed, somebody wrote a plan, and the services arrived whether or not the family knew what to ask for. That is what an entitlement is. Then the program ends, and the whole apparatus reverses direction at once.

In postsecondary settings and in adult services, the student has to self-identify, produce documentation, and request what they need. The wiki puts it in five words: no one will seek them out. The move from entitlement to eligibility is one of the most consequential and least prepared-for shifts in the whole transition, and it is nearly invisible while it is happening, because on the last day of school nothing looks different yet.

15% of federal VR funds are already reserved for this Under WIOA Title IV, vocational rehabilitation agencies must set aside 15 percent of their federal VR funds to provide Pre-Employment Transition Services to students with disabilities who are eligible — or potentially eligible — for VR services. The money is committed before anybody refers anybody. What is not committed is whether your students are among the ones it gets spent on.
What Pre-ETS is and is not. Pre-ETS is a policy framework, not a research-validated intervention with its own evidence base. Its five service categories map onto practices that do carry independent evidence — work-based learning, self-determination instruction, career exploration — but the package has never been studied as a distinct intervention. Evidence level: policy. Federally mandated is not the same as proven, and this issue is not going to blur the two.
01 This Month’s Focus

Money already
set aside

The sentence that does the work in this whole topic is one most staff have heard once and never in full. Here it is in full, from the wiki: “Potentially eligible” means students do not need a full VR eligibility determination to receive services; an IEP or 504 Plan is sufficient.”

Two things hang off that. The first is the set-aside above: this is not a grant somebody has to win, and it is not a line in a budget that gets spent on your students only if there is money left over. It is a fixed federal percentage reserved for exactly this purpose, and services that go unclaimed do not roll over to your caseload as anything. The second is the door. An IEP is sufficient. A 504 Plan is also sufficient.

The 504 inclusion is significant — students who are not in special education but have documented disabilities can access Pre-ETS. This is a broader eligibility than traditional VR served.

The wiki, on who counts as potentially eligible

That is the most under-used fact in this month’s material, and it is specifically Californian in its consequences. Significant numbers of California students with learning disabilities, ADHD and anxiety are on 504 Plans rather than IEPs. Those students sit outside the special education referral habits entirely — no annual IEP where an agency invitation is a required element, no case manager whose job description includes agency linkage — and the wiki’s instruction is direct: partner schools should communicate this pathway to 504 students and families who may not know VR services are available to them at all.

Issue 05 said the eligibility fact in a single clause back in December, as an aside inside a work-based learning argument. This is the first full treatment, and the full version is a different proposition than the aside was.

The five
categories

Pre-ETS is not a program a student enrolls in. It is a framework of five required service categories delivered before a student exits school. In California, DOR administers them; the five are identical under federal law. The concrete examples below are CTI’s (Fast Facts, 2024).

  1. 1
    Job exploration counseling

    In classroom or community settings; helping students understand what career options exist. On the student side that looks like completing interest inventories, identifying career clusters, attending a career fair. On the school side: interest inventories administered, employers invited to speak.

  2. 2
    Work-based learning experiences

    In-school, afterschool or internship formats. Informational interviews, job shadowing, a workplace tour, an internship. Schools contribute school-based enterprises, travel training, and matched in-school work experiences.

  3. 3
    Counseling on postsecondary education

    CTI’s activity table titles this one Counseling on Educational and Training Options, which is the broader and more honest form: not only college, but apprenticeships, certifications, career and technical programs. Campus tours, college fairs, shadowing classes at a career or technical school.

  4. 4
    Workplace readiness training

    Social and independent living skills for the workplace. Resume writing, job search strategies, money management and budgeting — and, notably, scheduling and attending an SSI or SSDI benefits counseling session, which is a Pre-ETS activity and not an extra.

  5. 5
    Self-advocacy instruction

    Listed in the activity table as Instruction in Self-Advocacy. Teaching students to request accommodations and then giving them somewhere to practice; leading their own IEP or 504 meeting; practicing disability disclosure on the job. This is the category that speaks directly to the opening of this issue, because self-identifying is the skill the next system requires and the current one does not.

Who counts as a
student in California

This is where national materials will hand you the wrong number, and where your own 22-year-olds are most likely to be turned away by somebody acting in good faith. Federal law fixes neither end of the age band. It delegates both to state law, and California has answered.

34 CFR § 361.5(c)(51), implementing the Rehabilitation Act § 7(37), sets the floor at the state’s IDEA transition-services age unless the state elects a lower minimum, and the ceiling at 21 — or, if state law provides a higher maximum age for receipt of IDEA services, “not older than that maximum age.” California’s floor is 16, because 16 is California’s IDEA transition age under Education Code 56345.1. California never made the lower-age election that some other states did. And California’s ceiling is where it gets locally useful.

The California band, printed correctly

Age 16 through 21 — continuing past the 22nd birthday for a student still participating in a special education program. That extension is 9 CCR § 7026.5, DOR’s own regulation, and its stated purpose is “to ensure there is no gap in pre-employment transition services.” Cal. Educ. Code § 56026(c)(4) carries the identical schedule. A short form that stays safe: 16 and up while they are still in the program. A bare “16 to 21” silently drops your own oldest students.

The extension is bounded, and the boundary is the birth month. For a student who turns 22 while still in a special education program:

  • Turns 22 January through June — may continue through the end of that fiscal year, extended school year program included.
  • Turns 22 July, August or September — may not begin a new fiscal year in the program, except to finish a term already underway in a year-round program.
  • Turns 22 October, November or December — terminated December 31 of that fiscal year, unless the IEP would conclude at fiscal year end anyway.

Two consequences worth holding separately. The extension attaches to program participation, not to age: a student who has already exited special education is outside it, however recently they exited. And that student is not out of options — general VR services and an Individualized Plan for Employment have no upper age limit at all. They simply cannot be served under the Pre-ETS set-aside. Those are two different doors, and only one of them closes.

Expect the mismatch. DOR’s public Student Services page publishes a flat “Age 16 through 21” and does not mention the turn-22 extension its own regulation grants. So a family who follows the link will read a narrower rule than the law provides. Cite 9 CCR § 7026.5 directly if a student is turned away on age, and confirm with the district office how they handle it — that operational question is not one this page can answer for you.

Early, not
in the last year

There is one timing instruction in this material and it is unambiguous. CTI (2026) tells counselors to initiate referrals early — “not in the final year of high school when there is insufficient time to establish eligibility and services.”

Which means the intuitive April move is the wrong one. The student closest to exiting is the student for whom a referral started now has the least room to work — eligibility, an assigned counselor, a plan, and services all have to fit inside the months you have left. The right April move is to find the student who is not exiting this year and has no DOR connection, and start there. The exit-year student still gets a referral. They just are not the one this month is about.

What that changes about April

The goal is a warm handoff, not a disconnect at exit — CTI’s phrase, and the vault’s word is handoff. A handoff needs two people who have met, a document that says so, and enough calendar in front of it to be more than an introduction. That is a partnership question rather than a paperwork question, and partnerships can be measured.

What we are not claiming. Interagency collaboration is a Research-Based predictor, for employment outcomes only — not education, not independent living. Predictors are correlational and carry no guidance on dosage, timing or delivery; Mazzotti and colleagues (2021) is a systematic literature review rather than a meta-analysis, and career and technical education is the only Evidence-Based predictor among the 23. We also do not have a figure for how many students do or do not connect to adult services. Anybody quoting you one should be asked where it came from.

Interactive · seven dimensions, four stages

Rate the partnership

CTI’s Interagency Collaboration Assessment turns “we have a good relationship with DOR” into seven things you can be honest about separately. Read each dimension, pick the stage that describes where your program actually is, and get back the next concrete move. There is no score and no right answer — the tool is an action-planning scaffold, and the lowest rung is a starting point rather than a grade.

Before you start

Rate one relationship at a time

The rubric was written for a landscape with one VR partner. California has several. The wiki’s California note is explicit that a program should apply the same four-stage rubric to each agency relationship independently rather than averaging across them — DOR, the Regional Center, and the SELPA are three different partnerships and they are rarely at the same stage. Run this once with DOR in mind. Then run it again for the next one and see how differently it comes out.

Substitute “DOR transition counselor” for the rubric’s DARS counselor throughout — that substitution is the wiki’s, not ours.

Loading the seven dimensions. With scripting off, the exercise is this: for each of the seven, decide which of the four stages — Undeveloped, Emerging, Developing, Well-Developed — describes your program today. The seven dimensions are the interagency agreement (is there a signed one, and is it reviewed annually); student referrals (what share of students with IEPs and 504 Plans are connected to DOR before exit — the Well-Developed threshold is greater than 75 percent); collaborative service delivery (are Pre-ETS and transition activities routinely coordinated, with shared decisions about funding, staffing and delivery); outreach (is there a systematic process for informing all students and families about DOR, with DOR information always provided at IEP meetings); contact and information exchange (is there a designated primary school contact who knows the referral process, with regularly scheduled exchange of progress and records); IEP meeting notification and participation (invited with at least 14 days advance notice, signed consent and information release obtained, and an arrangement for input when the counselor cannot attend); and logistical support (confidential meeting space, internet access, and staff who know when the counselor is coming). Then find your two lowest and start there.

      02 Practice Spotlight

      The referral is
      an auditable element

      Everything above argues that connecting a student to DOR is a good idea. This section argues something narrower and harder to wave off: in the IEP, it is not a good idea. It is a required element, checked by document review, and it has a companion requirement in the VR regulations that almost nobody quotes.

      IDEA specifies four required transition elements in the IEP: measurable postsecondary goals, transition services, course of study — and agency linkage, the invitation to relevant outside agencies (DOR, the Regional Center, and others) when their participation may be needed. Issue 03 established that list back in October. What that issue did not say is what happens to it downstream.

      • Indicator 13 is a compliance indicator, held to 100%. Not a performance target with a state-set threshold — a compliance standard, meaning every IEP for a transition-age student is expected to contain all federally required transition components. And Indicator 13 is audited, not surveyed: it rests on IEP document review rather than post-school outcome data. What is written is what is measured.
      • Agency linkage is one of those components. Which makes “put it in the IEP notes” something other than tidy record-keeping. It is the element itself. A referral that happened but was never documented is, for audit purposes, a referral that did not happen.
      • The plans are required to be coordinated — 34 C.F.R. § 361.46(d). The VR Individualized Plan for Employment must be coordinated with the IEP. That is a federal VR regulation, not a recommendation from a toolkit, and it is the sharpest thing in this month’s material. Two documents, two agencies, one legal obligation to make them agree.

      Put those together and the argument for referring stops depending on anyone being persuaded that DOR is worth the trouble. The IEP has to invite the agency. The IPE has to be coordinated with the IEP. Neither of those is satisfied by a warm feeling about a counselor you like.

      Fourteen days,
      signed consent, a fallback

      CTI’s assessment turns the invitation into three testable parts. All three have to be present for that dimension to be Well-Developed, and the wiki notes that many programs sit one stage below because the second and third are missing.

      1. 1
        At least 14 days advance notice

        The counselor is invited with a minimum of fourteen days of warning. This is the part most programs technically do and functionally miss: an invitation that lands three days out is an invitation a counselor with a full caseload cannot accept. The wiki names this exact pattern — invited, but without sufficient advance notice — as the Developing stage.

      2. 2
        Signed consent and information release, obtained first

        An outside agency cannot be brought into a meeting about a student, or receive that student’s records, without written permission. This is the step that gets skipped when the invitation is late, and it is the one that cannot be improvised in the room.

      3. 3
        A written or virtual fallback when they cannot attend

        VR counselors can participate in person, virtually, or by submitting information prior to the meeting. Which means a counselor who cannot make the date is not a failed invitation — unless nobody asked for the written version. Arrangements for input when the counselor cannot attend in person are part of the Well-Developed descriptor, not an apology for missing it.

      4. 4
        Then write it into the document

        Who was invited, when, whether they attended, and what they contributed. Agency linkage is the auditable element; the IEP is where the audit looks. It also survives your caseload changing, which memory does not.

      Who signs, in a program serving 18-to-22-year-olds

      Most published guidance on inviting a VR counselor says “with parent permission,” because most of it was written for minors. For your students that language is wrong. Educational rights transfer to the student at 18. The adult student signs the consent and the information release; the family is looped in with the student’s agreement, and they remain invited as interested parties. Asking a parent to sign for a 20-year-old is not a small procedural slip — it is the same reflex that makes self-advocacy the fifth Pre-ETS category and the hardest one to teach.

      One phrase to retire. You will hear that DOR is “voluntary, so the family has to be on board.” The first half is not something this vault documents about DOR at all, and the second half has the wrong person in it. Adult services are eligibility-based rather than automatic — with a real California exception, since the Lanterman Act establishes a legal entitlement to Regional Center services for eligible Californians with developmental disabilities. Eligibility-gated is not the same as discretionary, and “nothing is an entitlement after 22” is simply wrong for a Regional Center client.

      DOR Student Services

      Pre-ETS in California is administered by the Department of Rehabilitation and branded publicly as DOR Student Services. The five required categories are identical under federal law; what is local is who delivers them and who counts as a student.

      The page is the authoritative front door, and it is also where the age mismatch lives. dor.ca.gov/Home/StudentServices →

      Read it with the caveat attached. The page publishes eligibility as a flat “Age 16 through 21” and omits the turn-22 extension that DOR’s own regulation grants. Both things are true at once: the site is the right place to send a family, and the regulation is the thing to have in hand when a 22-year-old still in the program is told they have aged out. Send the link and the citation together — 9 CCR § 7026.5 — and the mismatch reads as information rather than a contradiction.

      DOR may deliver Pre-ETS directly or through contracted providers, which is worth knowing before you assume a service does not exist locally. San Diego Workforce Partnership is a WIOA partner able to deliver work-based learning and workplace readiness components; JVS San Diego and Ability Innovations are local providers that may deliver DOR-contracted Pre-ETS. For students with intellectual and developmental disabilities, coordinate with San Diego Regional Center so that Pre-ETS and Regional Center transition services come out complementary rather than duplicative — duplicated effort is the first of the four documented barriers to collaboration, ahead of competing demands, attitudes and stereotypes, and staffing shortages.

      One gap, named. This vault holds no address, direct phone number, or named contact for the San Diego DOR district office. The only number it holds is DOR’s statewide line, 1-800-952-5544. The honest instruction is to ask your program lead or search dor.ca.gov for the district office — and then, having found the counselor, to write the name somewhere that outlives you.

      Quick tip for April

      Refer the student who is not leaving.

      Pick one student who is not exiting this year and has no DOR connection — and if they are on a 504 Plan rather than an IEP, pick that one first. Invite the DOR counselor to their next IEP with fourteen days of notice, the student’s signed consent in hand, and a written alternative offered in the same message.

      Then put it in the document. Not because the audit is coming, though it is. Because the referral you remember making is not the referral the next case manager inherits.
